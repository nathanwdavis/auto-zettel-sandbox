The obvious argument against open theism is definitional: omniscience means
knowing everything, the future free acts of creatures are something, so a God
who does not know them is not omniscient. Grudem does say this — "Such a God
is not truly omniscient, for he learns many billions of new facts every day"
— but he also records why it cannot be decisive. Richard Rice "redefines
omniscience: 'An omniscient being knows everything logically knowable,' and
then he defines 'logically knowable' to exclude future human choices"
([[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]], distilled at
[[grudem-defines-omniscience-as-one-simple-eternal-act-and-rejects-open-theism--202609061915]]).
Once the word is redefined, the definitional argument only restates the
disagreement. The open theist agrees that God knows everything knowable and
denies that future choices are in that class.

The argument Grudem actually rests on is a different kind. Following Bruce
Ware, he sets out five passages from Isaiah 40–48 in which God distinguishes
himself from idols by one test — that he declares beforehand what is to come:
"Tell us what is to come hereafter, that we may know that you are gods"
(Isa. 41:23); "I am God, and there is none like me, declaring the end from the
beginning and from ancient times things not yet done" (Isa. 46:9–10). Ware's
conclusion, which Grudem adopts, is that to deny foreknowledge is to "deny
the very basis by which God himself has declared that his claim to deity shall
be vindicated and made known". The premise is not *omniscience entails
foreknowledge* but *God's own criterion of deity is foreknowledge*. It does not
depend on how the word "omniscient" is defined, and it is immune to Rice's
move, because it is not about what an omniscient being must know but about
what the true God, on his own account, is distinguished from false gods by
knowing. To this Grudem adds Steven C. Roy's count of 4,017 biblical
prophecies, 2,323 of them concerning free human decisions, presented in
Scripture "as God's firm declarations of what will certainly occur" and not as
estimates of the likely.

The shape of that argument is what connects it to the rest of this map. It is
an identification of the true God by a mark — the capacity to declare the
future — that the rivals lack, and in that respect it belongs with the
tradition-neutral description in
[[anselms-formula-identifies-god-by-description-alone--202609010840]]. But it
is not tradition-neutral. The mark is supplied by God's own speech in one
scripture, and what makes it a mark is a record of predictions fulfilled, so
that identity is carried here by deeds in history, which is where
[[evangelical-theology-identifies-god-by-a-tri-personal-life--202608311940]]
found this tradition putting its weight. Isaiah's test is a description, but a
description of a track record.

**Sources and their limits.** The section is on file in the second edition's
own words from the owner's licensed export, and the five Isaiah passages are
printed there in full. Rice, Pinnock, Ware and Roy are known to this base only
as Grudem quotes them: the export has no footnotes, and none of the four has
been read. So the note can say what Grudem's argument is and what it rests on;
it cannot say whether Ware's reading of Isaiah 40–48 is right, whether Rice
would accept "logically knowable" as the accurate summary of his view, or how
Roy's 2,323 were counted. The claim that the definitional argument "cannot be
decisive" is this base's reading of why Grudem, having made it in one
sentence, spends the rest of the sub-section on Isaiah; the section does not
itself rank its arguments.
