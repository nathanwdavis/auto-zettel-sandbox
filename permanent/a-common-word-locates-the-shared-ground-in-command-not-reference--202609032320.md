The most prominent collective Muslim statement addressed to Christians never
asks whether the two worship the same God. Invited by its own title to answer
the question, it establishes common ground somewhere else entirely: in what
God *commands*, not in who is being referred to and not in how he is described
(see [[a-common-word-on-the-ground-shared-with-christians--202609032310]]).

The letter's common ground is two commandments -- love of the one God, love of
the neighbour -- and its argument is that both scriptures issue them. Divine
unity appears, but in a specific and easily misread position: as what the two
commandments "arise out of" and are prefaced by. It is the premise the shared
command rests on, not the thesis being argued. The letter grants that Islam and
Christianity are different religions with formal differences that cannot be
minimised, and lets the concession stand: on its account the common ground
survives the difference rather than being established by denying it.

Two details show this is a deliberate relocation and not an omission.

First, the letter is built on Qur'an 3:64, which contains exactly the clause a
Christian reader would expect to be the sticking point -- *ascribe no partner
unto Him*, the prohibition of shirk. The Qur'an elsewhere applies language of
that kind to trinitarian confession explicitly, telling the People of the
Scripture to "say not 'Three'" and condemning those who say "Allah is the third
of three" (4:171, 5:73; see [[quran-tawhid-passages--202608311923]]), so the
clause is available as an objection to precisely the doctrine the addressees
hold. Whether Muslim theology has generally pressed it that way is not
something this repository has a source for, and no claim about that is made
here. What can be observed is what this letter does with it. It
glosses the clause as bearing on divine unity, and routes the neighbouring
clause ("none of us shall take others for lords beside God") through
al-Tabari's commentary into a point about not obeying others in disobedience to
God, and thence to religious freedom and justice between communities. The verse
that could have been the objection is used as the invitation.

Second, the letter states the disagreement about Christ plainly rather than
avoiding it -- Muslims recognise Jesus as Messiah "not in the same way
Christians do" -- and then argues, from the gospel sayings about who is with
and against, that Christians should count Muslims as *with* them. That is an
argument about alignment between communities. It is not an argument that the
descriptions match, and it does not need one.

**What this does to this repository's framing.** The claim that the dispute
turns on whether reference or description decides
(see [[the-same-god-question-turns-on-reference-versus-description--202608311942]])
was assembled entirely from Christian and secular sources. It is not thereby
wrong: the Qur'an does answer the reference question, and answers it
affirmatively (see
[[the-quran-asserts-co-reference-and-difference-in-one-address--202609032315]]),
so the axis is not foreign to Islamic material. What this letter shows is that
the dichotomy is *incomplete*. It has no slot for a party that declines both
horns and proposes a shared command instead, and that is what 138 signatories
did when they addressed the Christian churches directly on this subject. A
frame that cannot classify the most prominent statement on one side of the
dispute is a frame with a gap in it.

The Jewish material in this repository produced a structurally similar surprise
from a different direction: the Rema's gloss concedes the referent and makes the
verdict turn on who is worshipping (see
[[shituf-grades-the-worshipper-instead-of-identifying-the-worshipped--202609011542]]).
The two are not the same move -- shituf grades the worshipper, and A Common
Word grades nobody, changing the subject to what both are commanded. What they
share is the finding that neither tradition, argued from inside, reaches for the
reference/description dichotomy on its own.

**The limits of this note.** One document is not a theology. A Common Word is a
public appeal to named church leaders with a stated practical purpose -- peace
between the communities -- and a text written to find common ground is the least
likely place to find the sharpest statement of difference. The classical texts
that argue the other way, Al-Ghazali's *Faysal al-Tafriqa* and Ibn Taymiyya's
*al-Jawab al-Sahih*, are not in this repository and would be the test of whether
the relocation observed here holds outside an irenic register. This note claims
only what the letter shows: that when Muslim scholars answered the Christian
churches collectively and in their own voice, the axis they chose was not the
one this repository had assumed was the neutral shape of the dispute.
