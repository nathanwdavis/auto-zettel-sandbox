Compound growth is usually stated as arithmetic, but the arithmetic hides a
standing task: every period, something must actually move the output back
into the base. Franklin's 1789 codicil makes that task visible by
specifying who performs it and how, for two hundred years — borrowers repay
yearly interest plus a tenth of principal, and those repayments "shall be
again let out to fresh borrowers"; unpaid managers keep the books; and the
design's stated hope is that "no part of the money will at any time be
dead" ([[franklin-directs-two-cities-to-compound-a-bequest-for-two-centuries--202609010407]],
[[franklin-last-will-and-testament-with-codicil--202609010406]]). Franklin
even prices the operational risk into his own projections, conditioning the
£131,000 and £4,061,000 checkpoints on the plan running "without
interruption" and "if no unfortunate accident has prevented the operation."

This elaborates [[reinvested-returns-compound--202701010011]]: reinvestment
is not a property money has but a step an operator performs, so a
compounding scheme is only as strong as whatever institution performs that
step every period. It supports
[[compounding-favors-horizons-longer-than-one-life--202609010205]] from the
design side — Franklin did not just observe that long horizons win; he
built an operator (a self-refilling loan fund with volunteer management)
intended to outlive him by two centuries. And it shares its concept with
[[atomic-notes-and-compound-interest-share-a-reinvestment-loop--202608311038]]:
in a note system too, the loop only compounds while the practitioner keeps
performing the reinvestment step — writing new notes against old ones —
period after period.
