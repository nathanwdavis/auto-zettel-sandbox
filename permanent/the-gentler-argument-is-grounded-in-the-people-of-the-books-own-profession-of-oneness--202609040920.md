Asked why Q 29:46 orders one manner of argument for the People of the Book and
another for the idolaters, al-Razi answers from what the People of the Book
already hold: `فَوَحَّدُوا وآمَنُوا بِإنْزالِ الكُتُبِ وإرْسالِ الرُّسُلِ
والحَشْرِ` — they professed [God's] oneness and believed in the sending down of
books, the sending of messengers, and the gathering. The differential treatment
is earned by shared theological content, and the content he names first is
*tawhid* itself.

**The claim is about where the weight is placed.** The verse hands a commentator
two possible grounds for treating the People of the Book differently: its own
clause that "our God and your God is one", or facts about what they believe.
al-Razi takes the second and leaves the first unglossed
([[the-kalam-commentary-is-silent-on-the-clause-too-so-the-silence-is-not-a-genre-effect--202609040915]]).
His formulation is `جاءُوا بِكُلِّ حَسَنٍ إلّا الِاعْتِرافَ بِالنَّبِيِّ` — they
came with everything good except acknowledgement of the Prophet — and the
consequence he draws is procedural: their views are not to be made light of and
their forefathers not charged with error, unlike the idolater's. He closes the
comment on the same footing: they were distinguished from the idolaters
`بِكُلِّ فَضِيلَةٍ، إلّا هَذِهِ المَسْألَةَ الواحِدَةَ`, by every merit except
this one question.

**Why it matters to the same-God question.** This base has been reading the
Islamic side largely through what the texts say about the *reference* of "God" —
whether the two parties' object of worship is identified as one. al-Razi's move
is different in kind. He does not identify the object; he attributes an act to
the other party. They *did tawhid*. A claim about what someone has professed is
weaker than a claim that their worship reaches the same God, and it is also
harder to dismiss, because it does not require the commentator to adjudicate
reference at all. It is the same asymmetry
[[internal-plurality-is-not-what-divides-the-monotheisms--202609011543]] notices
from the Jewish side: traditions grade the worshipper's profession where an
outsider expects them to identify the worshipped.

**The obvious objection, and it is answered in the same passage.** "They
professed oneness" cannot mean, for al-Razi, that their doctrine is sound: two
sentences earlier he has said that those of them who assert a son for God have
committed association
([[the-classical-exclusion-of-trinitarians-is-made-inside-the-verse-that-carries-the-shared-god-clause--202609040925]]).
Both statements stand in his text, unreconciled and apparently unnoticed as a
tension. What this note claims is only that the profession of oneness is the
ground he *gives* for the gentler treatment — which is a fact about his
argument, whatever else he says elsewhere in it.

**Scope.** One commentator, on one verse. Whether the same grounding appears in
the other classical commentaries is untested: their comments on 29:46 develop
the abrogation question and the *jizya* rather than the reason for the
distinction, so the question would have to be put to them separately.
