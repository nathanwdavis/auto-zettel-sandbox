The Qur'an addresses Christians in two registers, and a reading that hears only
one of them is selecting, not summarising.

In one register it hands Muslims a formula of shared reference to use in
dispute. 29:46 instructs them to argue with the People of the Scripture only in
the better way and then to say: "We believe in that which hath been revealed
unto us and revealed unto you; our Allah and your Allah is One, and unto Him we
surrender" (Pickthall). The oneness there is not merely numerical but
identifying — the shared God is affirmed in the same clause as shared
revelation — and it is framed as a *command about what to say*
([[the-quran-also-tells-muslims-to-say-our-god-and-yours-is-one--202609040105]],
[[quran-shared-god-passages--202609040056]]).

In the other register it names a formula that trinitarian language cannot
satisfy on its face: the instruction not to say "Three", and the statement that
"They surely disbelieve who say: Lo! Allah is the third of three" (4:171; 5:73)
([[the-quran-asserts-tawhid-and-names-the-formula-it-rejects--202608311933]],
[[quran-tawhid-passages--202608311923]]).

Both registers are addressed to the same audience, and they are not in different
books. 3:64 shows them in a single verse: an invitation to the People of the
Scripture to come to an agreement, whose terms include worshipping God alone and
that "we shall ascribe no partner unto Him". The invitation and the exclusion
arrive together.

**What follows, and what does not.** What follows is a constraint on argument:
neither the position that the Qur'an affirms Muslims and Christians worship the
same God nor the position that it denies this — both phrasings here are this
note's own, quoting nobody — can be established by citation, because citation
supports both
and the texts sit side by side. Anyone settling the question from the Qur'an
alone is choosing which register governs, and that choice is an interpretive
commitment that has to be defended rather than a reading that can be quoted.
This is why the base holds two Qur'an captures rather than one: the first was
assembled around difference, and a set of passages chosen to bear on the
same-God question that holds the rejecting verses and omits 29:46 is
selection-shaped whether or not it was meant that way.

What does *not* follow is that the Qur'an is inconsistent. 5:48 states the
principle that reconciles the registers from inside the text: "For each We have
appointed a law and a way. Had God willed He could have made you one community."
Difference of law and way is presented as intended. That is a claim about
divergent *religions*, and it leaves the identity of the God who appointed both
untouched — which is precisely the shape this base has already found the
Abrahamic dispute to take, dividing over internal distinction rather than over
oneness
([[abrahamic-monotheisms-divide-over-internal-distinction-not-oneness--202608311941]]).

The limits are the ordinary ones for a scriptural claim and are worth stating.
Both captures are Pickthall's 1930 English, and his choices are visible in the
places this note leans on — "agreement" at 3:64 where the 2007 Muslim scholars'
letter has "a common word", and "Allah" left untranslated at 29:46 where the
same word is elsewhere rendered "God". The doctrinal weight in Islam rests on
the Arabic. And no commentary is captured on either side: how the exegetical
tradition has actually harmonised these registers is a question this base cannot
yet answer from anything it holds.

This note is not tagged `contested` even though its subject is: what it asserts
is that both registers are present in the text, which two captures of the text
settle directly. The contested part — which register governs — is exactly what
the note declines to decide.
