When a field says an effect has been replicated hundreds of times, the sentence
can mean two different things, and which one it means is checkable. It can mean
that someone pooled the studies and got an interval that excludes zero. Or it
can mean that someone read the studies and most of them pointed the right way.
The most comprehensive audit of retrieval practice in real classrooms means the
second.

Agarwal, Nunes and Blunt screened nearly 2,000 abstracts, coded 50 experiments
from 37 studies, and derived effect sizes where the reporting allowed —
"Across the 50 experiments coded, we derived 66 effect sizes (60 Cohen's *d*
and 6 partial eta squared). For 16 comparisons, data were insufficiently
reported to calculate an effect size"
([[agarwal-nunes-and-blunt-screened-two-thousand-abstracts-and-counted-what-they-found--202609040405]],
preprint p. 19). The headline is then a tally of that count, not a pooled
estimate: 62% of the 60 were medium or large, 22 were small or very small, one
was negative. There is no weighting by precision, no confidence interval, no
heterogeneity test, and no single number for how big the effect is. The
sixteen comparisons that could not be quantified do not disappear from the
argument either — the appendix enters them as "Numerical benefit from retrieval
practice", a direction with no magnitude.

The authors name the bias this method is most exposed to and then leave it
where it lies: "Our literature review is subject to publication bias, when
studies with positive outcomes tend to be published to a greater extent than
studies with negative outcomes" (p. 37). Nothing in the paper corrects for it —
no funnel plot, no trim-and-fill, no sensitivity analysis. A vote count over a
literature the counters describe as filtered toward positive outcomes will come
out positive whether or not the effect is robust, which is exactly the
inference a bias correction exists to block.

So the claim this note makes is narrow and it is about standing, not about
truth. Retrieval practice may well be as robust as its field says. What is now
established is that the audit most often pointed to as establishing it is a
census, and a census of a population the authors say is not randomly drawn.
[[retrieval-not-re-exposure-is-what-makes-learning-last--202609040340]] was
right to hold its robustness paragraph open, and this note does not close it:
it names what the first of the three named audits actually contains. Two remain
unread, and one of them —
Rowland's 2014 meta-analysis in *Psychological Bulletin* — is a pooled analysis
by an author outside the Roediger–Karpicke line, which is to say it is the one
that would answer the question this note leaves standing.

**Why this base is fussy about the distinction.** It has been burned once
already, in the same fortnight, by a headline effect that an outside team could
not reproduce
([[a-failed-replication-leaves-the-mechanism-standing-and-the-effect-unproven--202609040210]]).
The lesson taken from that was not *distrust psychology* but *ask who did the
checking, and what the checking consisted of*. Applied here, the answer is: a
partially-inside team — Pooja K. Agarwal is first author of a study this base
already cites — reading and coding, carefully and transparently, without
pooling. That is worth more than the survey chapter's assurance
([[karpickes-survey-vouches-for-the-effect-and-never-mentions-notes--202609040330]],
p. 434), which was written by the assessed study's own co-author. It is worth
less than a meta-analysis, and the gap between those two is precisely the size
of what is still unknown here.
