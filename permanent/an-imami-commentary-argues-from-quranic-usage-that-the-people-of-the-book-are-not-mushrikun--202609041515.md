[[the-qurans-own-syntax-is-the-classical-proof-that-the-people-of-the-book-are-not-mushrikun--202609041320]]
records the argument from Q 98:1 — that a verse coordinating "those who
disbelieved *from among* the People of the Book" *and* "the *mushrikin*" treats
the two as distinct classes, so the Qur'an's own syntax excludes the People of
the Book from the word. The base held that from al-Qurtubi and al-Sabuni: two
Sunni authors. It is now held from an Imami one too, made independently and
carried further.

**The same argument, from the other side of the divide.** Al-Tabataba'i:

> على أن لفظ المشركين في القرآن غير ظاهر الإطلاق على أهل الكتاب بخلاف لفظ
> الكافرين

moreover the word *al-mushrikin* in the Qur'an is not apparently applied to the
People of the Book, unlike the word *al-kafirin*.

He runs it over the whole usage rather than one verse — Q 98:1, Q 9:28, Q 9:7,
Q 9:36, Q 9:5 — and, unlike the Sunni witnesses, he takes on the two passages
that look like counter-examples. Q 2:135, where "and he was not of the
*mushrikin*" answers Jews and Christians, he reads as pointing past them to a
third party, on the evidence of Q 3:67. Q 12:106, "most of them do not believe in
God except that they are *mushrikun*", he reads as not being in the business of
naming at all, since on a naming reading the description would catch most
believers too.

**The addition that is his own, and it is a distinction the base did not have.**
Grant that the People of the Book commit *shirk* in some graded sense — he grants
it himself, ranking manifest polytheism, then their position, then trust in
secondary causes, then `الغفلة عن الله`, heedlessness of God, from which only the
sincere escape. It still does not follow that the Qur'an *names* them
*mushrikun*:

> غير أن إطلاق الفعل غير إطلاق الوصف والتسمية به

predicating the deed is not the same as predicating the description and naming by
it.

The example is domestic and disarming: Q 3:97 says of one who neglects the
pilgrimage `ومن كفر`, and yet `ليس تارك الحج كافرا` — the one who omits the
*hajj* is not an unbeliever. A Muslim can *do* something the Qur'an calls *kufr*
without being *a kafir*. The same grammar covers a Christian who can be said to
associate without being one of the *mushrikun* the legal verses address.

**Why this is worth its own note.** It separates two questions the base's earlier
material kept fusing. Whether trinitarian worship *is* a form of association is a
doctrinal question; whether the Qur'an's word `المشركون` *ranges over* the People
of the Book is a question about the reference of a term in a legal text. Al-Sabuni
and al-Qurtubi's syntax argument settles the second and leaves the first open;
al-Tabataba'i states the independence explicitly, which is what makes it a
distinction rather than an evasion. That is also why
[[one-verse-runs-mushrik-at-two-extensions-and-the-difference-is-legal-not-doctrinal--202609041330]]
is right that one verse can run the word at two extensions without contradiction:
the wide extension is the deed, the narrow one is the name.

**Where it bears on the same-God cluster.** It cuts against reading the Qur'an's
*shirk* vocabulary as a straightforward denial that Christians worship the same
God. On this reading the vocabulary is doing legal work — fixing who falls under
which rule — and the graded sense that would reach Christian doctrine is
explicitly *not* the sense in which the legal verses use the name. The base
already has the converse pressure, that classical commentators place trinitarians
among those excluded inside the shared-God verse itself; both stand, and the
tension between them is the finding, not a defect in either.

**What limits it.** One commentator, one host, one work, and a *tafsir* rather
than a legal text. The argument is his reading of Qur'anic usage, not a consensus
report, and the Imami commentaries eight centuries earlier read the same word the
other way — see
[[the-imami-tradition-is-split-on-2-221-so-the-strict-reading-is-the-classical-commentaries-not-the-school--202609041510]].
What this note claims is that the argument exists on both sides of the
Sunni–Shi'i divide, not that either side settled it.
