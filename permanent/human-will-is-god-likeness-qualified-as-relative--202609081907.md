Grudem closes the attributes of purpose by turning them on the creature, and the
sentence that does it carries its own limit: "We exercise choice and make real
decisions regarding the events of our lives. Although our will is not absolutely
free in the way God's is, God has nonetheless given us relative freedom within
our spheres of activity in the universe he has created"
([[grudem-splits-gods-will-into-necessary-free-secret-and-revealed--202609081904]],
from [[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]]). Two claims in one breath:
the human will is a genuine likeness of God's, and the likeness is bounded in
the one respect that matters for the free-will dispute.

That is the communicable-attribute pattern this base recorded from the wisdom
material, running again on a different attribute
([[grudem-describes-a-communicable-attribute-by-route-and-fruit--202609081647]]).
Wisdom was "in part communicable"; will is a "faint reflection" held in
"relative freedom." In both cases the sharing is quantified downward in the
sentence that grants it, and in both the boundary is drawn at the point where a
creature might be thought to have the thing as God has it. Here the boundary is
named exactly: not *absolutely* free.

The word doing the work is worth keeping in view, because it is the same
concession the omniscience section made and the providence chapter cashed out.
Chapter 12 conceded "some sense in which our choices are not absolutely free";
chapter 16 named the residue as freedom of inclination and said it "does not
rule out the idea that our choices may ultimately be determined by God in ways
invisible to us"
([[the-freedom-grudem-deferred-is-compatibilist-freedom--202609081827]]). Here
the same qualifier appears in the doctrine of the imago Dei, which means the
compatibilist position is not confined to the polemical chapters: it is built
into what Grudem thinks a human being *is*.

There is a rhetorical tension the passage does not resolve. Having capped the
creature's freedom as relative, Grudem spends the closing paragraph praising
freedom in the political register — deprivation of "their ability to make free
choices by evil governments" suppresses "a significant part of their
God-likeness," and Patrick Henry's "Give me liberty or give me death!" is said
to find "an echo deep within every soul created in the image of God." Nothing
here is inconsistent: the freedom tyranny removes is freedom from *external
compulsion*, which is exactly what freedom of inclination preserves and what
Calvin's necessity/compulsion distinction was built to protect. But the passage
moves between the two senses without marking the shift, and a reader who arrives
at "free choices" from the political paragraph will not be reading the word as
the doctrine defines it.

**Limits.** This is a closing devotional application, not an argument, and it
does not engage the free-will question it touches; the sentence about relative
freedom is the only definitional content, and its terms — "absolutely free,"
"relative freedom," "spheres of activity" — are left unglossed here. Grudem's
doctrine of the image of God is in a later chapter not on file, so whether
"faint reflection" is his considered account of the imago Dei or a summary
gesture cannot be checked. The tension noted in the last paragraph is this
base's reading; Grudem does not raise it.
