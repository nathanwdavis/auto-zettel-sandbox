Put the two arguments side by side and they are the same argument.

**Maimonides, on how idolatry began.** God "placed them on high and treated them
with honor, making them servants who minister before Him. Accordingly, it is
fitting to praise and glorify them" — because it is God's own will "that they
magnify and honor those whom He magnified and honored, just as a king desires
that the servants who stand before him be honored. Indeed, doing so is an
expression of honor to the king."

**Aquinas, on why honouring creatures is licit.** Servitude "is due to God and to
man under different aspects": God has "absolute and paramount lordship," while a
man "partakes of a certain likeness to the divine lordship." So dulia, paid to
those who excel, is a distinct virtue from latria and not a defection from it.

Same premise — a great lord's ministers may be honoured, and honouring them
honours him. Maimonides files it as the reasoning that produced the first
idolatry, offered by "the wise men of that generation." Aquinas files it as the
structure that keeps the veneration of saints and images from being idolatry.

Neither is confused, and the disagreement is not about the facts. Both hold that
God created these beings, that they hold their office from Him, and that honour
is owed to excellence. What they disagree about is whether the *aspect* under
which the honour is rendered can be held distinct in practice. Aquinas says it
can, and names the aspect that cannot be shared: "the creature does not partake
of the power to create." Maimonides says that once the act is directed at the
creature it is idolatry whatever aspect the agent has in mind, and makes the
point by granting the agent full knowledge of the true God.

This is a cleaner specimen of the base's central disagreement than the same-God
literature provides, because here the two traditions share their premises down
to the analogy and still divide. And it is one more instance of the pattern in
[[maimonides-puts-islam-inside-the-boundary-and-christianity-outside-it--202609042040]]:
where Maimonides draws the idolatry line, Christian practice falls on the far
side of it — not by accident, but because the line is drawn exactly through the
distinction Christian practice relies on.
