Asking for the earliest writings that touch this debate invites an anachronism,
because the earliest texts on the subject are not arranged as the later debate
is. They defend human freedom, at length and explicitly, but the thing they
defend it against is never God's sovereignty.

Justin, writing about 155, raises the problem from his own side: having argued
that Christ's coming was foretold, he stops "lest some suppose, from what has been
said by us, that we say that whatever happens, happens by a fatal necessity,
because it is foretold as known beforehand". The opponent is the Stoic and astrological doctrine of fate,
and God's foreknowledge enters as a premise to be defused, not as a rival claim
to be weighed: "we do not say it as if they came about by a fatal necessity"
([[justin-answers-the-fatalist-reading-of-prophecy-by-making--202609141702]]).

Irenaeus, about 180, states the two halves together — "Men are possessed of free
will, and endowed with the faculty of making a choice. It is not true,
therefore, that some are by nature good, and others bad" — and the second
sentence is the purpose of the first. His target is the Valentinian doctrine of
fixed natures, and free will is the premise that destroys it. Divine sovereignty
stands on his side of the argument, since it is the one Creator's justice that
the fixed natures impugn ([[irenaeus-grounds-free-will-in-creation-and-aims-it-at-the--202609141703]]).

Origen, about 229, is the hinge. His chapter is still a defence of freedom, but
its longer title concedes that the threat has moved inside Scripture: "On the
Freedom of the Will, With an Explanation and Interpretation of Those Statements
of Scripture Which Appear to Nullify It". Pharaoh, Ezekiel's stony heart and
Romans 9 are now the objections to be answered
([[origen-treats-the-hardening-of-pharaoh-and-romans-9-as-the--202609141704]]).
The material of the later debate is assembled here; the opposing party is not.

That party first appears at Hadrumetum. Augustine writes in 426 to monks who had
read his case for grace as a denial of freedom, and warns against two errors at
once, those who defend free will so as "as boldly to deny, and endeavour to do
away with, the grace of God" and those "who so defend God's grace as to deny
man's free will"
([[augustine-writes-to-monks-who-took-his-case-for-grace-to-be--202609141705]]).
Only there do both positions have Christian advocates.

So the answer to "earliest" depends on which question is asked. The earliest
preserved Christian writings that touch the *materials* of the debate are the
second-century apologists, Justin first among them. The earliest that treats the
sovereignty texts as a standing difficulty is Origen. The earliest that records
the debate as a dispute *between Christians* is Augustine, some two and a half
centuries after Justin.

**Limits.** "Earliest preserved" is a claim about survival, not about
composition: the second-century apologists are themselves answering positions,
Gnostic and Stoic, whose own texts largely do not survive. The New Testament
material that both later sides quote — Romans 9 above all — is earlier than any
of these writers, and is not treated here as a witness to the debate, since it is
the disputed text rather than a party to the dispute. This note also rests on
four works only, each read in a nineteenth-century English translation; Tertullian,
Clement of Alexandria, Methodius and Pelagius' own writings are not on file.
