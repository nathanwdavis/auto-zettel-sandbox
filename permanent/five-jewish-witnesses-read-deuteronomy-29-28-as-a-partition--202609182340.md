The claim that the rabbinic reading of Deuteronomy 29:28 sorts sins by jurisdiction rested, three days ago, on one commentator reporting one Talmudic passage that was not on file ([[deuteronomy-29-29-anchors-the-hidden-knowledge-partition--202609151914]]). The passage and four more commentators are now captured, and the claim holds across all of them, with one division inside it and one rejected alternative that is the more instructive for having been rejected.

**The Talmud.** Sanhedrin 43b brings the verse into the discussion of Achan and reads its dots as a statement about when the nation became answerable for individuals' sins. Rabbi Yehuda: God "did not punish the nation for hidden sins until the Jewish people crossed the Jordan River". Rabbi Nehemya: the nation is never answerable for hidden sins, the verse's "forever" "indicating that the Jewish people will never be collectively held responsible for the secret sins of individuals", and the dots date liability for *public* sins to the crossing instead ([[sanhedrin-43b-reads-the-dots-on-to-us-and-to-our-children--202609182335]]). Both take the hidden things to be sins done in secret. Rashi's comment follows Rabbi Nehemya.

**The commentators.** Ibn Ezra: "The reference is to one who serves idols in secret", whose "punishment is in the hands of God", while public sin obliges "us and our children" ([[ibn-ezra-reads-the-secret-things-as-secret-idolatry--202609182337]]). Rashbam: hidden sins are God's "because sins committed in secret cannot be dealt with by the courts", and the revealed half is the duty to punish "on the basis of reliable witnesses" ([[rashbam-makes-the-hidden-sins-god-s-to-punish-because--202609182338]]). The Or HaChaim: the verse is Israel's reply to Moses, refusing liability for sins "committed by fellow Jews in private" and accepting mutual responsibility for public ones ([[the-or-hachaim-reads-the-verse-as-israel-s-reply-to-moses--202609182339]]). Rashi, already on file, reads it the same way ([[rashi-reads-the-secret-things-of-deuteronomy-29-28-as--202609151905]]). Four commentators across six centuries, and the Talmud behind them, put the verse on one axis: what the community can judge and what only God can.

**The division inside the tradition.** Ramban reports that reading, agrees that the Rabbis hold it, and then reads the verse otherwise: "the secret things are the sins that are hidden from those who commit them", unwitting sins, for which the covenant curse does not fall, with Onkelos's "before the Eternal" as his evidence ([[ramban-reports-the-commentators-reading-and-the-rabbis-then--202609182336]]). This is the one Jewish reading on file in which the verse turns on knowledge, and the knower whose ignorance matters is the sinner: hidden means unknown to the one who did it. It is still a sin and still a question of liability. The tradition's internal disagreement is therefore about *whose* concealment the verse means, the community's or the sinner's own, and not about whether the verse concerns concealment of sins at all.

**The rejected alternative.** Ibn Ezra reports a reading in which "the secret and the revealed things belong to God. It is incumbent upon us only to observe the words of the Torah", and dismisses it: "this interpretation has no head or foot" ([[ibn-ezra-reads-the-secret-things-as-secret-idolatry--202609182337]]). It is the only reading in the corpus that removes the division of jurisdiction, and it is the nearest any of them comes to the Christian use of the verse, since it gives both halves to God and leaves Israel only obedience. Even it is not the epistemic reading: it does not make the secret things knowledge withheld. So the reading Calvin and Grudem build on, that the verse partitions what God has disclosed from what he has kept, has no representative among these witnesses, and its closest relative was reported once, in order to be rejected.

**Consequence for the base.** The earlier note's finding stands and is stronger: the anchor verse of the Christian secret/revealed doctrine is read by the Jewish tradition, in the Talmud and in every commentator captured, as a rule about liability for sins. The two traditions are not contradicting each other, because the verse's nounless participles admit both; but a note citing the verse for a partition of knowledge is citing one tradition's reading, and the other tradition, asked directly, never gives it.

**Limits.** Five commentators and one Talmudic passage; Sforno was served without a declared licence and is not captured; Ramban's Hebrew is not captured; two of the translations are undated on Sefaria. The Samaritan reading, the third claimant to the Pentateuch, is still absent. *Six centuries* runs from Rashi (d. 1105) to the Or HaChaim (d. 1743).
