The hardening of Pharaoh's heart is where the two traditions read one Pentateuch
narrative against each other, and — unusually — the disagreement is explicit on
both sides rather than reconstructed.

Maimonides makes the hardening retributive and second. A person may sin so
gravely that repentance is withheld as the penalty; "For these reasons, it is
written in the Torah [Exodus 4:21], 'I will harden Pharaoh's heart.' Since, he
began to sin on his own initiative and caused hardships to the Israelites who
dwelled in his land … judgment obligated that he be prevented from repenting so
that he would suffer retribution." Sichon, the Canaanites and Israel under
Elijah are handled identically, and the section closes without hedging: "the
Almighty did not decree that Pharaoh should harm the Israelites … They all
sinned on their own initiative and they were obligated to have Teshuvah held back
from them"
([[maimonides-makes-free-will-a-pillar-of-the-torah-and-refers-the-paradox-upward--202609081912]]).

Grudem states that reading almost in Maimonides' own terms, as an objection to
be met: "It is sometimes objected that Scripture also says that Pharaoh hardened
his own heart (Ex. 8:15, 32; 9:34), and that God's act of hardening Pharaoh's
heart was only in response to the initial rebellion and hardness of heart that
Pharaoh himself exhibited of his own free will"
(sec. B.7, captured at raw/202609081918 and summarised at
[[grudem-defines-providence-as-preservation-concurrence-and-government--202609081824]],
from [[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]]). He answers in three moves.
First, chronology: "God's promises that he would harden Pharaoh's heart (Ex.
4:21; 7:3) are made long before Scripture tells us that Pharaoh hardened his own
heart (we read of this for the first time in Ex. 8:15)." Second, concurrence:
"even when Pharaoh hardens his own heart, that is not inconsistent with saying
that God is causing Pharaoh to do this"
([[concurrence-is-the-mechanism-the-wisdom-section-assumed--202609081826]]).
Third, a pre-emption: if the objector retreats to God merely intensifying
existing evil desires, that concession "could still in theory at least cover all
the evil in the world today," since everyone has such desires.

Two observations that the texts support and that are easy to miss.

The chronological argument is the only one of the three that could move
Maimonides, because it is an argument about the narrative rather than from the
system. And it is not obviously decisive: Maimonides grounds Pharaoh's prior
guilt not in Ex. 8:15 but in Ex. 1:10, "Come, let us deal wisely with them" —
the oppression of Israel, which stands three chapters before God's first promise
to harden. On Maimonides' reckoning the freely chosen sin does precede the
announced hardening; Grudem's dating counts only the narrator's use of the
hardening formula, not the sin the hardening is said to answer. Neither author
addresses the other's starting point.

Both, remarkably, build on the same verse for God's purpose. Maimonides reads
Ex. 9:16 as explaining why Moses was sent to a man who would not relent — to
publish "that when the Holy One, blessed be He, withholds repentance from a
sinner, he cannot repent." Grudem reaches the same verse through Paul's use of
it in Romans 9. The shared text is doing opposite work: for one it advertises a
judicial withdrawal, for the other it displays sovereign purpose.

**Limits.** Grudem is not answering Maimonides and shows no sign of having him
in view; the objection he states is the standard Arminian one, and the
convergence with Hilchot Teshuvah 6:3 is this base's observation. The Ex. 1:10
point in the fourth paragraph is likewise this base's, drawn from putting the two
captures side by side; neither author makes it. Both texts are read in
translation here — Grudem quoting the ESV, Maimonides in Touger's English — and
the Hebrew of the hardening verbs (*chazaq*, *qashah*, *kaved*), which is where
a serious treatment would go next, has not been captured.
