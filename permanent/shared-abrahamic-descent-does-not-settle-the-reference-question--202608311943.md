The premise that the three traditions descend from a common Abrahamic root is
accepted on both sides of the same-God dispute, and both sides reason from it to
opposite conclusions. A premise that yields either answer is not the one doing
the work.

Zeki Saritoprak argues from the lineage to a shared object of worship, via the
Qur'an's account of Jacob's sons: "this God that Jacob worshipped, this God that
Abraham, Isaac worshipped, is the same God that Muslims worship today" (see
[[gjelten-do-christians-and-muslims-worship-the-same-god--202608311927]]).

Philip Tachin's abstract concedes the same starting point -- shared roots in
Abraham, which "makes some, including Christian scholars, conclude that they
worship the same God" -- and denies the conclusion, because he takes the
question to turn on the substance of worship and its acceptance rather than on
ancestry (see [[tachin-do-they-worship-the-same-god--202608311925]]).

The lesson is narrow but load-bearing: historical continuity establishes that
the traditions are talking about the God of Abraham, and leaves entirely open
whether talking about him under incompatible descriptions still counts as
worshipping him. Arguments that stop at the shared ancestry have not yet reached
the disputed step.
