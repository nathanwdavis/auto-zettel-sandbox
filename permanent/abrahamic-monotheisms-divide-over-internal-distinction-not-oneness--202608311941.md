The dispute between Judaism, Christianity and Islam about God is not a dispute
about how many gods there are. All three assert exactly one. It is a dispute
about whether that one admits real internal distinction.

- The evangelical statement asserts one God "eternally existent in three
  persons" -- oneness and threeness in a single clause (see [[nae-statement-of-faith--202608311920]]).
- Maimonides asserts a oneness "unified in a manner which [surpasses] any unity
  that is found in the world," explicitly ruling out both the unity of a
  category containing individuals and the unity of a body divisible into parts
  (see [[maimonides-mishneh-torah-yesodei-hatorah--202608311922]]).
- The Qur'an asserts that God "begetteth not nor was begotten" and that "there
  is none comparable unto Him," and then names and rejects the formulation
  directly: "say not 'Three'" (see [[quran-tawhid-passages--202608311923]]).

Read together these are not three vague monotheisms that might be reconciled by
goodwill. They are three precise and incompatible answers to a further question
that bare monotheism leaves open. This is why the disagreement survives every
demonstration that the traditions share an ancestry, and why it is not resolved
by observing that all three call God one, merciful and creator -- they do, and
they still differ on what the one is.

The Shema is often cited as settling the Jewish side, but it does not settle it
on its own: the Hebrew carries a live crux between "the LORD is one" and "the
LORD alone" (see [[shema-deuteronomy-6-4-5--202608311921]]). Maimonides, not Deuteronomy, is where the
structural claim is made explicit.
