The category Molina named is fixed by two properties: its content comes
before the act of God's will, and its content could have been otherwise
([[middle-knowledge-sits-between-natural-and-free-knowledge-because-it-is-prevolitional-yet-contingent--202609090100]]).
Ware keeps the first by derivation and, his critics say, loses the second.
Helm: "But if God knows what Jones, if placed in circumstances C, would do,
then this is surely part of God’s natural knowledge, his knowledge of all
necessities and possibilities." Middle knowledge "‘works’ for the
compatibilist, Ware says (GGG 115), but it only works because it is not
genuine middle knowledge, a distinct type of divine knowledge, but part of
God’s natural knowledge. It works precisely because it is not middle
knowledge." The scriptural cases Ware cites "are not counterfactuals of human
(libertarian) freedom, and so are not middle knowledge at all", and the
verdict is a pun: "not so much middle knowledge as muddle knowledge"
([[helm-reads-wares-compatibilist-middle-knowledge-as-natural-knowledge-under-another-name--202609111815]],
from [[helm-shunning-middle-knowledge--202609111810]]). Van Horn gives the
reason in the vocabulary of the definition: "if God really can derive which
CCFs are true in this way, then once again CCFs are included in his natural
knowledge, since they would be necessary truths"
([[van-horn-records-the-concession-and-argues-that-counterfactuals-can-be-natural-or-free-but-not-middle--202609111817]],
from [[van-horn-on-incorporating-middle-knowledge-into-calvinism--202609111812]]).

Laing, arguing from the Molinist side eight years earlier, reaches the
opposite shelf by the same road. For the compatibilist who says the
counterfactual is true because God "created my will in such a way that I
would freely choose to decline instead of accept", the knowledge "is not
prevolitional. But this is not middle knowledge at all! Rather, it is merely
a part of God’s free knowledge!"; and the alternative, grounding it in the
creature's character as an idea in God's mind, leaves "simple
self-determination" as the only sufficient reason, a view "virtually
indistinguishable from libertarian freedom"
([[laing-argues-that-calvinist-middle-knowledge-is-caught-in-a-dilemma--202609111818]],
from [[laing-the-compatibility-of-calvinism-and-middle-knowledge--202609111813]]).
Van Horn allows both shelves and denies the third: "while CCFs may be either
natural or free, they cannot be middle, given classical Calvinism", the fork
being whether God's concurrence and his endowment of causal powers are
counted inside the circumstance (an argument summarised here from uncaptured
text). He adds that the knowledge itself is not news: "Historically, very
few have denied that God has such knowledge"; Molina's Thomist opponents,
Bavinck and Baxter all granted it, and Laing notes that Bañez "also held to a
belief that God knows all true counterfactuals of freedom (which are true in
virtue of an act of his will)". "What distinguishes middle knowledge from
natural and free knowledge is that its contents are known independently of
God’s decree, like natural knowledge, but are contingently true, like free
knowledge."

So three critics from two camps agree on the verdict and differ on the shelf:
natural for Helm, who reads the derived counterfactual as a possibility God
surveys; free for Laing, who reads the nature it is derived from as something
God decrees; either for Van Horn, depending on what the circumstance is taken
to include. The disagreement about the shelf is itself the finding. It is the
question whether a creature's nature is a possibility God contemplates or an
actuality God ordains, and the compatibilist who uses it as a truthmaker has
to say both, which is why the word "middle" slides between the two
neighbours it was coined to stand between.

**Limits.** Helm's own placement is not constant: his 2019 post reports the
seventeenth-century Reformed as putting the conditional in free knowledge,
true because decreed
([[helm-grounds-the-reformed-refusal-of-middle-knowledge-in-the-decree--202609111816]],
from [[helm-and-more-on-molinism--202609111811]]), so the natural-or-free
fork runs through Helm as well as between the critics. Van Horn's second
thesis, that Calvinism could hold middle knowledge at the price of weakening
concurrence or the claim that freedom requires determinism, is summarised
and not assessed. Ware's reply in *Perspectives on the Doctrine of God*
(2008) is unread; what he conceded is known only as Van Horn reports it.
