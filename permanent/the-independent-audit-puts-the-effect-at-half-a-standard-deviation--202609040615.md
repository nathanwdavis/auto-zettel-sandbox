The testing effect survives being checked from outside the group that made its
reputation. Pooled across 159 effect sizes from 61 studies reported between 1975
and 2013, retrieval beats restudy by `g = 0.50, CI [0.42, 0.58]`, and the
author's verdict on his own result is that the meta-analysis "demonstrates the
reliability of the testing effect"
([[rowland-meta-analysed-the-laboratory-half-and-excluded-the-classroom--202609040605]],
advance online pp. 8, 16).

Two things make that sentence worth a note rather than a citation. The first is
who wrote it: Rowland was at Colorado State University and is a co-author of
nothing else this base holds, which makes this the first estimate of the effect
in the base that is not produced, supervised or surveyed by the people whose
effect it is. Everything else here traces to the Roediger–Karpicke line or, in
the case of the classroom review, one step out from it. The second is that this
base had already been burned once by taking a field's word for its own
robustness — see
[[a-failed-replication-leaves-the-mechanism-standing-and-the-effect-unproven--202609040210]]
— and so treats "replicated hundreds of times" as a claim to check, not a fact to
repeat.

**Checked, and it holds, and it is smaller and messier than the phrase implies.**
Half a standard deviation is a real effect and an unremarkable one. The
distribution behind it is wide: the paper's own stem-and-leaf plot of all 159
effect sizes has **28 negative values**, the most extreme near −1.5 (Figure 1,
advance online p. 12). Roughly one laboratory comparison in six found restudy
beating retrieval. A pooled estimate that positive with a tail that long is what
a real but condition-dependent effect looks like — which is exactly what the
moderator analyses say it is, and what
[[a-failed-retrieval-that-is-never-corrected-buys-nothing--202609040620]] records
the sharpest instance of.

**The file-drawer question was asked, and answered by argument rather than by
correction.** Published effect sizes averaged 0.58 and unpublished ones 0.25, a
significant gap and on its face the signature of publication bias; Rowland names
it as such (p. 20). He then shows that the unpublished studies cluster on the two
design variables that most depress the effect — feedback absent in 89% of them,
mean initial test performance 59% — and that the gap vanishes when those are held
roughly constant, concluding that the low unpublished estimate was "very likely
attributable to the designs and procedures employed" (pp. 20–21). No funnel plot,
trim-and-fill or Egger test is reported, and he says why: with heterogeneity as
high as this, those methods mislead (p. 20).

That reasoning is plausible and it is not the same as a bias-corrected estimate.
What this base can say is that the one meta-analyst who went looking found the
signature, gave a specific mechanical explanation for it, and did not adjust the
headline number. Anyone quoting `g = 0.50` should know that is where it stands.

**What it does to the survey chapter's account.** Karpicke's survey names three
audits and calls all three meta-analyses
([[karpickes-survey-vouches-for-the-effect-and-never-mentions-notes--202609040330]],
p. 412). This base has now read two of them. Agarwal, Nunes and Blunt is not a meta-analysis — its authors
refused to pool and said so. This one is. So the survey was right about Rowland
and wrong about Agarwal et al., and the pattern is the same in both cases: the
description was inherited rather than checked, and reading the paper corrected
it. Two for two is a small number, and it is the whole of this base's experience
of checking that chapter's summaries against their sources.
