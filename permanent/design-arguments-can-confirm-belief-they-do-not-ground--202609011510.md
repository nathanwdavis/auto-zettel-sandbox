An assessment of a design argument can come apart into two questions that
are usually run together: whether the argument could carry the weight of
belief, and whether it is any good. Plantinga's 1986 notes answer no to the
first -- "belief in God is properly basic" -- and yes to the second: "At
least a couple of dozen or so" good arguments exist, but they are
non-coercive and "probabilistic", and their office is that "They can serve
to bolster and confirm ('helps' à la John Calvin), perhaps to convince"
(see [[plantinga-two-dozen-theistic-arguments--202609011500]], pp. 203,
210). On this framing an argument can succeed completely at its actual job
-- raising the probability of, and publicly vindicating, a belief formed on
other grounds -- while failing utterly as a foundation, and no inconsistency
follows from holding both.

This is the philosophical twin of a move the base already documents in
dogmatics. Hodge argues the design argument's conclusion at length while
denying that anyone's belief in God originates in it (see
[[hodge-keeps-the-design-argument-while-denying-it-originates-belief--202609011037]]),
and the knowability doctrine holds possessed and demonstrated knowledge of
God side by side (see
[[design-arguments-argue-for-what-the-doctrine-says-is-already-possessed--202609011041]]).
Plantinga's word "helps" is itself Calvin's frame: the outer display
confirms an inner sense that was doing the believing all along (see
[[calvin-pairs-an-inner-sense-with-an-outer-display-of-god--202609011036]]),
and his reading of the naive teleological argument -- contemplation of
order producing "a strong impulse to believe", perhaps by putting the
arguee where the Sensus Divinitatis operates -- makes the confirming office
explicit at the level of a single argument (same source, pp. 216-17).

The consequence for ranking design arguments: within this strand of
evangelical assessment, whether an argument proves is the wrong scale. An argument
ranks by how much confirming work it does for someone who already
believes, or how far it moves a sensible person's probabilities -- which is
why Plantinga can dismiss the deflations of fine-tuning ("From a theistic
point of view, however, no mystery at all and an easy explanation", pp.
215-16) without ever claiming the argument compels.
