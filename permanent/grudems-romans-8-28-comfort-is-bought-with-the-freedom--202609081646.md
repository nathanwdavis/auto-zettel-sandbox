At the close of the wisdom attribute (2nd ed., pp. 232-233) Grudem states the
consolation that the whole section has been building toward, and states it at
maximal scope: God "does work wisely in all the things that come into our
lives," and "God causes all things to move us toward his ultimate goal for our
lives" — with the goal fixed in advance as conformity to the image of Christ
(distilled at [[grudem-closes-gods-wisdom-in-the-readers-own-life--202609081645]],
from [[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]]). Nothing is exempted. The
example chosen is the one where the exemption would be claimed if it were
available anywhere: a thorn God "had chosen not to remove."

Ten pages earlier, in the section on knowledge, the same book pays for that.
Grudem concedes in his own voice that "if God knows all our thoughts, words,
and actions long before they occur, then there must be some sense in which our
choices are not absolutely free," and keeps the knowledge rather than the
freedom; Pinnock accepted the identical entailment and kept the freedom instead
([[grudem-and-pinnock-accept-the-same-entailment-and-give-up-opposite-sides-of-it--202609061922]]).
Read on its own that concession looks like a cost with no visible return in the
passage that incurs it — the omniscience section defends foreknowledge by
Isaiah's test of deity ([[grudem-answers-open-theism-with-isaiahs-test-of-deity-not-with-the-definition-of-omniscience--202609061921]])
and defers everything about how choices and providence fit together to a later
chapter.

The wisdom section is where the return is collected, and it is collected in the
pastoral register rather than the argumentative one. The comfort has a specific
logical form: *whatever arrives today is already ordered to a fixed end.* That
form requires the arriving things to be under the ordering — and most of what
comes into a life comes through other people's choices. On the open theist's
side of the same entailment those choices are precisely what God does not know
in advance and therefore has not ordered, so the promise cannot be issued in
this prospective, unconditional, applies-to-today form. Something weaker
survives there — that God is good, responds wisely, and will not be outmatched
— but it is an assurance about God's character and skill, not about the
outcome of every event, and it is not the sentence Grudem writes.

So the two sections are not two doctrines standing side by side. They are one
commitment and its use, and the exchange rate between them is visible only when
the passages are read together: exhaustive foreknowledge is what makes "all
things" a promise instead of a hope, and "some sense in which our choices are
not absolutely free" is its price.

**Limits.** Grudem does not draw this connection. The wisdom passage never
mentions foreknowledge, providence or open theism, and it argues nothing — it
consoles. The joining premise, that God's ordering of all things is what Rom.
8:28 asserts, is supplied by Grudem's paraphrase of the verse rather than by
the verse as he quotes it, and the base has not examined the verse itself. The
chapter on providence, where this would be argued rather than assumed, is
deferred to by both sections and is still not on file, so this note reads two
pastoral and polemical passages of one book and should be replaced by that
chapter's own argument when it can be captured. Finally, the claim about what
the open theist can and cannot say is an inference from the position as Grudem
reports it; no open theist has been read directly in this base.

**Amended 2026-09-08, from chapter 16.** The limits above named the providence
chapter as the thing that would replace this note's inference with an argument,
and said the note "should be replaced by that chapter's own argument when it can
be captured." The chapter is now on file, and it does two things to this note.
It supplies the mechanism the wisdom passage asserted without defending —
concurrence, on which every event is "fully (100 percent) caused by God and
fully (100 percent) caused by the creature as well," God as primary cause and
creaturely properties as the observable secondary ones
([[concurrence-is-the-mechanism-the-wisdom-section-assumed--202609081826]]). And
it turns the second paragraph's premise from this base's reading into Grudem's
own argument: objecting to middle knowledge, he insists that "every situation in
which people find themselves is itself the result of thousands, in fact
millions, of individual human choices," so that no one can bring about a
situation without determining the choices it is made of
([[a-situation-is-made-of-other-peoples-choices--202609081830]]). What remains
this base's own is the *pairing* of the two chapters; Grudem does not cite his
wisdom section here. One thing the chapter does not supply is an explanation:
he lists "Exactly how can God cause us to choose something willingly?" among the
questions his position cannot answer.
