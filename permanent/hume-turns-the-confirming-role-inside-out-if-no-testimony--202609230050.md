On the classical account miracles support faith by making preaching
credible. Aquinas says the word "needs to be confirmed in order that it be
rendered credible", and miracles are how
([[aquinas-ascribes-miracles-to-faith-as-their-end-and-their--202609230047]]).
Hume's "Of Miracles" attacks exactly that relation. A reported miracle is
believable only if the testimony's "falsehood would be more miraculous,
than the fact" it reports, and Hume argues that no historical testimony
meets this standard (Enquiry X, para. 91, in
[[hume-sets-testimony-against-experience-then-calls-faith--202609230043]]).
Miracles reach anyone after the eyewitnesses only as testimony, so the
chain from sign to credible word breaks.

Hume then moves the miracle instead of simply dropping it. The Christian
religion "cannot be believed by any reasonable person without" a miracle,
and "whoever is moved by Faith to assent to it, is conscious of a continued
miracle in his own person, which subverts all the principles of his
understanding" (para. 101). The miracle is now inside the believer. Faith
no longer rests on a miracle it has confirmed. Faith is the miracle, and
the proof of that is that nothing else could produce assent against
"custom and experience".

The remark is ironic, and the irony works by accepting the classical
position that miracles confirm faith and taking away the external miracles
it needs. What remains is a religion "founded on Faith, not on reason"
(para. 100). That is Hume's description of it, and it is the opposite of
Aquinas's model, in which miracles do for supernatural truth what natural
effects do for natural knowledge of God.
