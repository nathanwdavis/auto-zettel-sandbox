Three published replication attempts have been made on the paper that named the
"Google effect on memory", and all three failed. All three ran the same
experiment, and it is not the experiment that gave the effect its name.

Sparrow, Liu and Wegner's paper contains two research programmes. Experiment 1
is a *priming* study: answer hard trivia questions, then name the colour of
computer words in a Stroop task, and the computer words are supposed to slow
you down because the concept of the Internet has been activated. Experiments 2,
3 and 4 are *offloading* studies: tell people their typing will be saved or
erased, and measure what they remember afterwards. Only the second programme is
about external memory at all
([[sparrow-liu-and-wegner-ran-one-priming-experiment-and-three-offloading-ones--202609041810]]).

The replication record is entirely on Experiment 1. Two attempts inside the
Social Sciences Replication Project found no significant effect with adequate
power; a third, built specifically to answer the original author's objections
to those two, reported a Bayes factor of 5.07 in favour of the null, rising to
16 against the exact pattern the original claimed
([[hesselmann-failed-to-recover-the-priming-effect-and-audited-the-original--202609041815]]).
The third paper does not test Experiments 2 through 4, does not claim anything
about them, and mentions them only in its opening summary. So far as this
repository has been able to establish, nobody has published a direct
replication of them.

This is why the shape of the record matters more than its direction. The
discipline in
[[a-failed-replication-leaves-the-mechanism-standing-and-the-effect-unproven--202609040210]]
was written for a case where a replication failed and the *mechanism* was left
untouched. Here a second and stricter version of the same discipline applies:
the replication failed and three quarters of the *evidence* was left untouched
too. It is not that the offloading findings survive scrutiny; it is that they
have not been scrutinised. Neither a citation nor a debunking is available.

Two errors follow, and this repository is at risk of the second rather than the
first. **You may not cite "the Google effect" for the claim that expecting a
saved copy weakens memory** — not because that claim has been refuted, but
because the phrase now denotes a failed priming result and the citation will be
read as claiming the tested thing. **You may not treat the failed replications
as having disposed of externalised memory**, because no replication has gone
near it. The convenient move for a repository whose subject is a slip box would
be the second one; it is the one to refuse.

What the failure does license is a downgrade of the paper as a whole. An
audit of the original found its degrees of freedom irreconcilable with either
of its two reported sample sizes and its headline interaction resting on four
words chosen after the fact
([[the-google-effect-headline-rests-on-four-words-seen-once-each--202609041835]]).
A paper whose one tested result did not survive and whose reporting will not
survive an audit is weak evidence for its untested results as well — not zero,
but not the Science paper the citation count suggests. The right posture toward
Experiments 2 through 4 is that of an unreplicated finding from an unreliable
report, which is a long way below "established" and a long way above "refuted".
