Both traditions arrive at the same impasse and both admit it. Neither claims to
show how exhaustive divine knowledge and genuine human choosing consist
together. What separates them is not whether a mystery is conceded but *where it
is placed* — and the placement is not incidental, because each tradition puts
the mystery around whichever member of the pair it has already decided not to
give up.

Maimonides states the objection in the form the Christian debates use it:
if God knows a man will be righteous, "[it appears] impossible for him not to be
righteous"; and if he could still be wicked, "then His knowledge would be
incomplete." He answers from the doctrine of God. God "does not know with a
knowledge that is external from Him as do men, whose knowledge and selves are
two [different entities]. Rather, He, may His name be praised, and His knowledge
are one," and since God's essence is beyond comprehension so is his knowing.
The mystery is therefore located in *divine knowledge*. What is expressly not
left in doubt is the human half: "this is known without any doubt: That man's
actions are in his [own] hands and The Holy One, blessed be He, does not lead
him [in a particular direction] or decree that he do anything"
([[maimonides-makes-free-will-a-pillar-of-the-torah-and-refers-the-paradox-upward--202609081912]],
from [[maimonides-mishneh-torah-hilchot-teshuvah-5-6--202609081910]]).

Grudem inverts both halves of that. The divine side is where he is most
confident — foreknowledge is exhaustive, the decrees determine everything that
happens, and choices "may ultimately be determined by God in ways invisible to
us" ([[the-freedom-grudem-deferred-is-compatibilist-freedom--202609081827]]).
The mystery is placed on the human side, and he names it as one of the two
questions his position cannot answer: "Exactly how can God cause us to choose
something willingly?" So the residue for Maimonides is *how God knows*; the
residue for Grudem is *how a determined choice is a willing one*.

That symmetry explains why the dispute is so hard to adjudicate by argument.
Each side's concession is placed exactly where the other side's evidence would
have to land. Press Maimonides on foreknowledge and he refers you to the
incomprehensibility of God; press Grudem on willingness and he refers you to the
greatness of God — which is also, at the close of his providence chapter, how he
proposes to decide the question at all
([[the-dispute-is-decided-by-which-residue-honours-god-more--202609081831]]).
Both are appeals of the same form, made about different things.

Behind both stands the older rabbinic formula that states the pair and solves
nothing: *ha-kol tzafui, ve-ha-reshut netunah*, "Everything is foreseen yet
freedom of choice is granted" ([[pirkei-avot-3-15--202609081911]]). Maimonides
is elaborating that formula — his *reshut* is Akiva's word — and the Christian
discussion inherits the same pair without the formula. The traditions differ
less in seeing the problem than in which half they will not trade.

**Limits.** Maimonides here is the Mishneh Torah's Maimonides; the *Guide* III
treats providence at greater length and sometimes differently, and is not on
file. Grudem is one Reformed voice, and the Arminian and Molinist positions his
own chapter reports would place the mystery elsewhere again, so "the Christian
doctrine" is not a single thing and this note compares two authors, not two
religions. The claim that each locates the mystery around what it refuses to
give up is this base's reading of the two texts; neither author says it of
himself.
