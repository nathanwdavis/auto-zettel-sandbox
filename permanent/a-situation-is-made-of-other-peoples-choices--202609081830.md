Middle knowledge offers to keep libertarian freedom and exhaustive divine
foreknowledge together: God knows what each creature *would* freely do in any
circumstances, and actualises the world whose circumstances yield the outcomes
he wants. Grudem raises four objections, and three of them are the expected kind
— the theory is foreign to how Scripture speaks of election, it lacks explicit
biblical support, it satisfies neither party. The third is different, because it
is an argument from the structure of a circumstance rather than from a text.

Craig's formulation requires God to *bring about* a situation. But, Grudem
observes, "every situation in which people find themselves is itself the result
of thousands, in fact millions, of individual human choices," so "in order for
God to 'bring about a situation,' literally millions of prior human choices
would have to be decided in a certain way, or else the situation would be
different" — and therefore "such a process denies the kind of libertarian free
will that middle knowledge advocates are claiming to protect"
([[grudem-answers-the-arminian-by-comparing-unanswered-questions--202609081825]],
from [[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]]). The illustration is his own
life: great-grandparents emigrating from Norway in the 1870s, conservative
faculty founding Westminster in 1929, pastors founding a seminary in Arizona in
1987. A "set of circumstances" is not a stage that exists prior to the actors;
it is made out of what other actors did.

This does something for two notes already here. It confirms, from Grudem's own
mouth, a premise
[[grudems-romans-8-28-comfort-is-bought-with-the-freedom--202609081646]]
supplied on its own authority — that most of what arrives in a life arrives
through other people's choices, so a promise covering "all things" must reach
those choices too. That note flagged the premise as this base's inference; it is
now Grudem's argument, deployed for a different purpose. And it bears on
[[grudem-grounds-knowledge-of-the-possible-in-self-knowledge-without-separating-what-creatures-would-do--202609061923]],
which found that chapter 12 moved between what God *could do* and what creatures
*would do* with the bridge unstated. Chapter 16 states the bridge, in Frame's
words rather than Grudem's own: counterfactual knowledge of creatures "is
already part of God's necessary knowledge, or what Craig calls natural
knowledge." So the two were never meant to be separate faculties — which is why
Grudem can say the middle-knowledge debate "is not about whether God knows how
people would respond," and cite chapter 12 as having already affirmed it.

**Limits.** The objection tells against Craig's *bringing about* of
circumstances, not against middle knowledge as such; a Molinist who says God
selects a whole possible world rather than assembling situations piecemeal is
not obviously touched by it, and Grudem does not consider that reply. Craig is
quoted from a single essay in Pinnock's volume, and Frame from one page of his
*Systematic Theology*; neither work is on file here, so both are second-hand,
and Frame's claim that counterfactual knowledge collapses into natural knowledge
is exactly the contested premise a Molinist would deny — it is reported here as
Grudem's bridge, not endorsed as sound. Grudem's classification of Molinism as
"a third Arminian viewpoint" is his own and is argued for on one ground, that
the decisive factor in salvation is the creature's libertarian choice.
