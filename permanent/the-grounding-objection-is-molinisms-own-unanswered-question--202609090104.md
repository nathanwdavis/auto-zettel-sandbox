Every position this base has read on sovereignty and freedom leaves a
question it cannot answer, and the map has been recording where each puts
it. Grudem names his at the close of the providence chapter: "Exactly how can
God cause us to choose something willingly?" and how God is not to blame for
the evil he ordains
([[the-dispute-is-decided-by-which-residue-honours-god-more--202609081831]]).
Maimonides' is how a knowledge identical with God's essence can be known to
leave choice free
([[the-same-wall-and-the-mystery-put-on-opposite-sides--202609081914]]).
Molinism's is the grounding objection. It asks, in Laing's statement, "what
grounds the truth of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom." They "cannot be
grounded in God because determinism would follow," and the prevolitional
character of middle knowledge rules out grounding them in God's will; but
"they also cannot be grounded in the individuals to which they refer for at
least four reasons": they are true before the individual exists; the
individual's existence depends on God's will, so the counterfactual's truth
would too; they describe what never happens; and grounding them in the
individual's psychology would make the act unfree
([[laing-defines-middle-knowledge-as-prevolitional-knowledge-of-counterfactuals-of-freedom--202609090056]],
from [[laing-middle-knowledge-iep--202609090051]]). In Molina's own words the objection's target is the phrase that
does all the work: the middle knowledge sees what a will would do "ex
altissima et inscrutabili comprehensione" — a comprehension he himself calls
inscrutable
([[molina-distinguishes-three-knowledges-and-makes-the-middle-one-depend-on-the-creature--202609090055]],
from [[molina-concordia-liberi-arbitrii--202609090050]]).

Laing surveys five Molinist replies, and each moves the question somewhere
else. That counterfactuals "do not need to be grounded" makes them brute
facts, which names the residue rather than answering it. That the objection
"begs the question of compatibilism" is true of one form of it and leaves
the four reasons untouched. That propositions about the actual future "are
either true or false now, even though there is nothing in the present that
can be pointed to as grounding their truth" spreads the difficulty to
future-tense truth rather than resolving it. Grounding a counterfactual "in
the occurrence or nonoccurrence of the event in the closest
possible-but-not-actual world to the actual world" grounds it in a world
that is itself not actual. And grounding it in individuals "as they exist in
the precreative mind of God as ideas" puts the truthmaker back in God, which
is where the objection began. Laing's verdict concedes the shape: some
replies "may be seen as more of a shifting of the burden of proof than an
answer to the specific objection," even if "some sort of idea of grounding is at least
conceivable."

So the Molinist pays too, and in the same coin as the others: an admitted
inscrutability at the joint between God's knowing and the creature's
choosing. What differs is the joint. Grudem's inscrutable is how a determined
choice is willing; Maimonides' is how God's self-identical knowledge knows;
Molina's is how God comprehends what a will that does not yet exist would
freely do. This bears on the tie-breaker Grudem uses at the end of his
chapter, that the position to prefer is the one whose unanswered questions
leave God greater. Molinism's residue concerns the reach of God's knowledge
into a creature's freedom, not God's power or the reliability of his
promises; whether that diminishes God more or less than Grudem's residue is
the kind of comparison the tie-breaker cannot make without assuming its
answer, which is what the earlier note said of Grudem's own use of it.

**Limits.** The five replies are Laing's classification, reported as his; the
philosophers who made them, and Adams, who gave the objection its modern
form, are unread here. That each reply "relocates" the question is this
base's assessment, offered against Laing's more guarded verdict, and a reader
who finds the closest-world or divine-ideas reply successful will reject it.
Molina's "inscrutabili" is his word for the comprehension, but he does not
offer it as a concession; the parallel with Grudem's and Maimonides'
concessions is drawn here.

**Amended 2026-09-11, from Helm, Van Horn and Laing.** This note said the
Molinist pays the grounding objection and that each of the five replies
relocates it. Two further ways of paying are now on file, both Reformed.
Ware, a compatibilist, accepts the objection against Molina and grounds his
own counterfactuals in the creature's character together with its
circumstances, so that the question has an answer
([[wares-compatibilist-middle-knowledge-derives-the-counterfactual-from-character-and-circumstance--202609111820]]); the answer's cost, on Helm's and Van Horn's showing, is that a
knowledge so grounded is natural or free and not middle
([[a-counterfactual-derived-from-nature-and-circumstance-is-natural-or-free-knowledge-but-not-middle--202609111821]]), and Van Horn adds that a Calvinist who wants to keep middle
knowledge anyway must give up the grounding objection itself. The
seventeenth-century Reformed answer, as Helm reports it, is the decree: what
makes the counterfactual true is only what is decreed
([[the-reformed-answer-to-the-grounding-objection-is-the-decree-and-the-confession-runs-opposite-to-molina--202609111825]]), which from the Molinist side concedes the objection's own
premise rather than meeting it. Nothing above is withdrawn; the map of who
pays where now has a Reformed column.
