The secret/revealed distinction does the heaviest lifting in Grudem's doctrine
of God's will: it is what lets him say both that God "works all things according
to the counsel of his will" and that God "desires all people to be saved" while
holding that not all are saved. But the distinction cannot be applied by reading
the word. Scripture uses one vocabulary for both — *thelēma*, *theleō* — and
Grudem never claims otherwise. Every sorting he performs is argued from
something outside the verse
([[grudem-splits-gods-will-into-necessary-free-secret-and-revealed--202609081904]],
from [[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]]).

Three instances, three different outside facts.

*From the outcome.* 1 Tim. 2:4 and 2 Peter 3:9 are assigned to the revealed
will, and the reason given is not linguistic: "This is because the New Testament
is clear that there will be a final judgment and not all will be saved." The
doctrine of particular salvation, held on other grounds, settles which will the
verse is about.

*From the pragmatics of the act.* "Your will be done" (Matt. 6:10) cannot be the
secret will, because a petition for what is already decreed "would simply be to
pray, 'May what is going to happen happen.' That would be a hollow prayer
indeed, for it would not be asking for anything at all" — with a second leg, that
one could not know what one was praying for. The argument is from what prayer
must be in order to be prayer.

*From the speaker's situation.* "I will come to you soon, if the Lord wills" (1
Cor. 4:19) must be the secret will, because Paul already knows his apostolic duty
to come; the revealed will is not in doubt, so the conditional must be about
something else.

Naming this is not an objection. A distinction between aspects of one will could
not be legible in the vocabulary, since the vocabulary is what the distinction is
imposed on, and Grudem argues each case rather than asserting it — which is more
than the distinction's critics usually grant. But it does fix what kind of
instrument it is. The two wills are not read off the text; they are a frame
brought to the text, and its assignments inherit the security of whatever
settles them. That is why the 1 Tim. 2:4 assignment is the load-bearing one and
also the most exposed: an Arminian who denies that the New Testament teaches
particular salvation has been given no independent reason to put the verse under
the revealed will, and Grudem offers none here.

**Limits.** These are three sortings out of about a dozen in the section; the
rest are asserted with a citation and no argument, and this note does not claim
that no linguistic case could be made — only that Grudem makes none. Whether the
distinction is defensible on other grounds is a question this section defers
twice, to chapter 16 and to Piper's essay "Are There Two Wills in God?", neither
of which is read here for this purpose. Grudem himself marks the residue on the
adjacent problem of evil: "how this can be so may remain a mystery for us in this
age."
