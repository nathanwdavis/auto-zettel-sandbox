Count the terms in each articulation and the structures come apart. Kabbalah's
is ten *and* an eleventh that is not one of the ten. Christianity's is three
and there is no fourth.

On the kabbalistic side both captured sources are explicit that the One is not
among the sefirot. *Patach Eliyahu* addresses a "You" who "produced ten
constructs", binds them, directs them and is concealed in them, while "all the
sephirot, each one has its own name... But You have no specific Name" — and if
that One withdraws, the names are "left like a body without a soul" (see
[[patach-eliyahu-names-the-sefirot-and-leaves-ein-sof-nameless--202609011536]]).
Cordovero puts the same asymmetry in the language of predication: of the
Emanator "we can neither speak nor depict nor predicate — not judgement, not
mercy", and that holds "neither before the emanation, nor now after the
emanation"; even Keter, the first emanated, has name and depiction denied of it
(see [[cordovero-states-the-essence-vessel-dispute-and-answers-it-with-both--202609041710]]).
Cordovero's answer to the essence/vessel dispute adds a rank *inside* the ten —
inner sefirot that are essence, outer ones that are vessels — and leaves that
outer boundary exactly where it was.

On the Christian side there is deliberately no such term. Hodge's report of what
the councils decided puts the divine essence nowhere but in the three: "It is
not the Father as such, nor the Son as such, who is self-existent, infinite, and
eternal, but the Godhead, or divine essence, which subsists in the three
persons" (see [[hodge-on-the-two-readings-the-councils-ruled-out--202609041715]]).
The Father is not a hidden One behind the persons from whom they proceed as
products; the Son is "begotten and not made, consubstantial with the Father",
and the anathema falls precisely on making Him a thing produced out of some
other substance.

So the difference is not the number of terms — ten against three is the least
interesting thing here — but whether the articulation is exhaustive of the God
articulated. In Kabbalah it is not: the ten are how the Infinite acts and is
known, and the Infinite is other than the ten and unnameable apart from them. In
the Nicene doctrine it is: there is no remainder, and to posit one would be to
posit a fourth. That, rather than plurality as such, is where the two
articulations diverge, and it explains why the divergence survives whichever
reading of the sefirot is taken. On the instrumentalist reading the ten are
tools of a God outside them; on the essentialist reading they are His essence
under aspects — and even then Cordovero's Ein Sof stands beyond every predicate
the ten carry.

**Tagged contested, and the reasons.** The comparison is this repository's; none
of the three sources draws it. Two limits are real. First, the Christian
tradition has apophatic strands, and a divine essence that "subsists in" three
persons is not straightforwardly identical with them; someone could argue that
*ousia* plays a role structurally like Ein Sof's. The answer this note gives is
that Hodge's councils explicitly deny the persons are relations *ad extra* of
something behind them, which is the move that would make *ousia* a fourth term,
but that is an argument and not a quotation. Second, *Ein Sof* is a term neither
captured source uses — *Patach Eliyahu* says only "You", and the identification
with Ein Sof is the standard construal, recorded as a construal in
[[tikkunei-ha-zohar--202609011531]]. Nothing above depends on the name, only on
there being an addressee who is not one of the ten.
