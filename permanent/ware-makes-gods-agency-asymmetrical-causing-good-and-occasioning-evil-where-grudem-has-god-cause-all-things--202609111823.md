Ware's middle knowledge is not offered as a theory of knowledge for its own
sake. Helm records its purpose: Ware "offers his thoughts about middle
knowledge as a contribution to establishing and further understanding God’s
asymmetrical attitude to good and evil", on the principle that "the way in
which he works his will regarding evil is different from the way in which he
works good, since being immaculately holy, he cannot be the author of sin"
([[helm-reads-wares-compatibilist-middle-knowledge-as-natural-knowledge-under-another-name--202609111815]],
from [[helm-shunning-middle-knowledge--202609111810]]). Van Horn gives the
content of the asymmetry and the passage that states it. In *God's Greater
Glory* Ware "explicitly rejects the traditional Calvinist claim that God
causes human sinful actions. While he is eager to affirm that God causes
good actions and is sovereign over evil, the idea that he causes evil
actions is difficult to square with divine goodness." Ware (GGG 123-24):
"Perhaps we should think of God’s regulating the factors of a situation,
then, as “occasioning” a particular choice to be made, rather than as
“causing” a particular choice to be made. Because God knows the natures of
each person perfectly, he knows how those natures will respond to
particular sets of factors presented" to them. "Thus, without causing a
person to do evil, he nonetheless controls the evil they do. He controls
whether evil is done, what evil is done, and in any and every case he could
prevent the evil from being done. But in no case does he cause the evil to
be done"
([[van-horn-records-the-concession-and-argues-that-counterfactuals-can-be-natural-or-free-but-not-middle--202609111817]],
from [[van-horn-on-incorporating-middle-knowledge-into-calvinism--202609111812]]).
The middle knowledge is the hinge: God can occasion rather than cause
because he knows, from natures and factors, what each arrangement of factors
would yield
([[wares-compatibilist-middle-knowledge-derives-the-counterfactual-from-character-and-circumstance--202609111820]]).
Van Horn reads the rejection narrowly: Ware still holds that all free
actions "are caused by prior factors, and these may result in a causal chain
which can be traced back to God", so what he rejects is that God "pre-moves
his creatures to sin (although he does not use that term)".

This is a difference inside the Reformed camp, and it is the one place on
file where Ware and Grudem part. Grudem's concurrence is symmetrical: every
event "fully (100 percent) caused by God and fully (100 percent) caused by
the creature as well", and, in the sentence that governs his chapter, "It
seems better to affirm that God causes all things that happen but that he
does so in such a way that he somehow upholds our ability to make willing,
responsible choices"; on God and sin he ends with Berkhof, that "the problem
of God’s relation to sin remains a mystery"
([[grudem-defines-providence-as-preservation-concurrence-and-government--202609081824]],
from [[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]];
[[concurrence-is-the-mechanism-the-wisdom-section-assumed--202609081826]]).
Ware's "in no case does he cause the evil to be done" denies, for one class
of events, the claim Grudem affirms for all of them, and it denies it in
order to answer the question Grudem lists as unanswerable, "Exactly how can
God not be blamed for evil when he ordains that we do evil"
([[the-dispute-is-decided-by-which-residue-honours-god-more--202609081831]]).
The two share the aim and the Isaiah-shaped doctrine of foreknowledge; they
differ on whether God's causing of an evil act is to be affirmed and left a
mystery or denied and replaced by a mechanism.

The asymmetry has a closer relative than Grudem. Molina's list of what
coheres has God able to bend the will anywhere "praeterquam in peccatum",
permitting sin but not inciting it, and cooperating with every free act
while being in no way the cause of sins
([[molinas-own-list-of-what-coheres-keeps-sovereignty-whole--202609090102]]).
Ware's "controls the evil they do" without causing it is that list restated
for a will whose choices follow from its nature. What the Molinist secures
by the creature's freedom of indifference, Ware secures by the distinction
between arranging the factors and causing the act, which is why Helm counts
his proposal as a mitigation of the tradition's mystery and judges the
mitigation futile
([[helm-leaves-the-mystery-of-god-and-evil-where-augustine-and-calvin-left-it--202609111824]]).

**Limits.** Ware's own terms for the two modes of agency, which the
secondary literature reports as direct and causative for good and indirect
and permissive for evil, appear in no capture on file; Helm's uncaptured
paragraph describes the relation to evil as indirect and permissive, and
that description is attributed summary here, not quotation. Grudem's "causes
all things" is stated as what "seems better to affirm", and his chapter
elsewhere distinguishes the divine and creaturely causes as working "in
different ways", so the contradiction recorded in the links is between
Ware's denial and Grudem's affirmation as each is worded, not a claim that
Grudem holds God to be the author of sin, which he denies. Whether "occasion"
differs from "cause" in more than name is Helm's question, and it is not
settled here.
