The two fallen-nature accounts keep guilt from Christ by different routes,
because they disagree about whether anyone else inherits it.

Meyendorff denies that guilt is inherited at all. "Eastern patristic thought
ignores the notion of a transmission" of guilt from Adam; "What is being
transmitted from parents to children is not sin but mortality and slavery"
([[meyendorff-holds-that-eastern-patristic-thought-knows-no--202610081437]]).
In Byzantine Theology there is "no place, then, in such an anthropology for
the concept of inherited guilt", and "the inheritance of the Fall" is "an
inheritance essentially of mortality rather than of sinfulness"
([[meyendorff-finds-a-greek-patristic-consensus-that-the--202610081438]]).
On that account Christ, born of Mary, inherits the mortality Meyendorff says
every child inherits, and there is no inherited guilt for him to be spared.
Nothing about his conception has to do that work.

Irving keeps a doctrine of original sin that passes from Adam, and so he
needs a way round it. He finds it in "the manner of His conception": Christ
took part with the fallen children "not through connexion with Adam" but by
the free will of Father, Son and Spirit, came "not by ordinary generation",
and is not a human person, "and thus original sin is avoided, though yet the
body He took is in the fallen state"
([[irving-holds-that-original-sin-is-kept-from-christ-by-the--202610081436]]).
That puts him with Augustine and Aquinas, who also stop original sin at the
manner of conception, rather than with Calvin, who credits the Spirit's
sanctifying
([[augustine-and-aquinas-block-original-sin-at-the-manner-of--202610031828]]).
The difference is that Augustine and Aquinas keep the flesh unfallen as
well; Irving keeps it fallen and exempts it only from original sin. That
also corrects the earlier note's opening, which counted Irving as dissenting
from the premise that Christ inherited no original sin: on pp. 159-60 he
holds that premise too, and dissents about the condition of the flesh.

The Westminster Larger Catechism is the foil for both. It counts "the guilt
of Adam's first sin" in the sinfulness of the fallen estate and has original
sin conveyed "by natural generation"
([[the-larger-catechism-defines-sin-as-want-of-conformity-or--202610032109]]).
Meyendorff denies the first. Irving's way round original sin presupposes
the second, descent from Adam by ordinary generation, and argues that Christ
falls outside it.
