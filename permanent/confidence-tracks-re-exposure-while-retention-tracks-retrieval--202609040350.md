People asked to predict how much they will remember answer a different question
from the one they were asked. Their predictions rise with how often they have
been re-exposed to the material; what they actually remember rises with how
often they have retrieved it. The two measures come apart, and they come apart
in the direction that hurts — the strategy that feels most productive is the one
that performs worst.

Both experiments this base has read measured it, on different scales, in the
same session as the retention data. Roediger and Karpicke's four-times-reading
group predicted their week-later recall at 4.8 on a 7-point scale, the highest
of the three conditions, and the repeated-retrieval group at 4.0, the lowest;
recall came in at 40% and 61%
([[roediger-and-karpicke-measured-retrieval-against-re-exposure-and-let-time-decide--202609040310]],
Table 2 and Figure 2, p. 252; the authors' own statement of it, p. 254). Agarwal and colleagues asked for a percentage instead, and predictions
climbed monotonically with rereading — .57, .65, .71 for one, two and three
readings — while actual recall for those same conditions came in at .40, .50,
.54, below the exposure-matched testing conditions that had been predicted lower
([[agarwal-et-al-put-the-notes-on-the-desk-and-measured-what-happened-a-week-later--202609040320]],
Table 4, p. 868). Their word for it is "a striking dissociation" (p. 870), and
they close by calling it a "metacognitive illusion" with, in their view,
potentially "dire consequences for students when they must monitor and regulate
their own learning" (p. 873).

The proposed reason is worth keeping separately from the finding, because it is
theirs and it is a hypothesis: judging how well you will remember something is
inferential, and after rereading the cue available is fluency — how easily the
material went down — while after retrieving the cue available is whether you
actually produced it. Fluency is the misleading one, and rereading manufactures
it.

**Two limits on the convergence.** The two studies share authors and a
laboratory, so they are not independent confirmations of each other; what they
are is the same dissociation surviving a change of scale, of task, and of
comparison condition. And nobody here measured anyone judging their own *notes*.
The result is about predicting memory after study or after test, and extending
it to a person surveying a full file and feeling well-equipped would be exactly
the kind of stretch this base refuses elsewhere.

Held against that limit, the connection to
[[a-trusted-structure-frees-attention-from-willpower--202608311142]] is a
question rather than a claim, and it is the sharpest one this cycle produced.
That note's argument, from Ahrens, is that a structure you can trust is worth
having because it relieves you of holding things in mind. Nothing measured
contradicts it. But the measured fact that the feeling of being well-prepared
is generated by re-exposure rather than by retrieval means the *feeling* of
having a trustworthy structure is not evidence that one has it — and that
feeling is, as things stand, the whole of the evidence anyone offers for this
practice.
