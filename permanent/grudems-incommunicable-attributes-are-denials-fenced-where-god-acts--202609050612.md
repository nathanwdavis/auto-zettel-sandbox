All five of Grudem's definitions of God's "incommunicable" attributes are built
to one pattern: a denial of something creaturely in God, then the word *yet*,
then an affirmation that God nevertheless acts, feels, or acts differently.
Independence denies that God needs the creation for anything, yet grants that
the creation can glorify him and bring him joy. Unchangeableness denies change
in God's being, perfections, purposes, and promises, yet says that God "does
act and feel emotions, and he acts and feels differently in response to
different situations." Eternity denies beginning, end, and succession of
moments in God's own being, yet says that God "sees events in time and acts in
time." Omnipresence denies size and spatial dimensions, yet says that God "acts
differently in different places." Unity denies that God is "divided into
parts," yet says that "we see different attributes of God emphasized at
different times" ([[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]], distilled at
[[grudem-divine-attributes-incommunicable-and-communicable--202609050547]]).

**Sources and their limits.** Four of the five sentences are witnessed by two to
four exact witnesses at the same pages; the omnipresence sentence by the lesson
outline alone, the two blog witnesses each differing from it at one word ("in
space", "spacial"). The omnipresence *yet* clause is used below, and it rests on
that single exact witness. Only one witness names an edition at all: a critique
that quotes the eternity definition at p. 168 of the 1994 text. The others give
those page numbers without naming an edition, among them a lesson outline of the
chapter posted on Grudem's website beside his lecture audio and dated there
2006/03/26 — unsigned, and with a third party named in its file metadata — whose
page ranges from 160 to 180 agree with them throughout (see
[[grudem-systematic-theology-chapter-11-course-outline--202609050602]],
distilled at
[[grudems-chapter-11-outline-lists-five-incommunicable-attributes-with-unity-glossed-as-simplicity--202609050646]]).
No captured witness gives a second-edition page for any of the five. The claim
below is therefore about a formula pinned to the 1994 pagination and only
assumed, not shown, to stand unchanged in 2020.

The pattern is not decoration. It fixes, in the definition itself, how far each
denial is allowed to reach, and the stopping point is not a metaphysical one.
The axis in
[[divine-simplicity-is-the-question-the-monotheisms-actually-divide-on--202609042081]]
sorts positions by how much of what we say about God the refusal of composition
is taken to eliminate: Maimonides lets it consume the attributes themselves and
finally number (see
[[maimonides-denies-god-has-attributes-at-all-not-merely-that-they-divide-him--202609042075]]);
Aquinas terminates it at metaphysical composition after enumerating six kinds
(see [[no-composition-does-different-work-in-each-tradition--202609042080]]).
Grudem's definitions are cut off somewhere else entirely — at whatever would
leave God unable to act, to feel, or to act differently here rather than there.
That is a stop fixed by what it preserves rather than by where an analysis of
composition runs out, and it is a kind of setting the axis did not yet have on
it.

The lesson outline is what forces the stopping point to be described that way,
in place of the simpler description the unity sentence invites. The outline
heads the section "Unity (Simplicity)," putting simplicity at the unity
attribute rather than anywhere else in the chapter's scheme, and prints three
sub-points under it: that God's being is "not a collection of attributes added
together," that his attributes "are not additions to his real being," and that
every attribute "also qualifies every other attribute"
([[grudem-systematic-theology-chapter-11-course-outline--202609050602]]). Those
three carry the denial well past the "parts" the definition sentence names, into
the relation between the attributes and the being. So the reading on which
Grudem's reach is simply a short one — stopping at parts, where Aquinas's
enumeration is only getting started — is not available, and the stopping point
has to be located by what the denial spares instead. What it never reaches is
what the *yet* clauses protect: not a plurality of attributes in God, but their
being emphasized differently at different times and, across the other four
definitions, God's acting and feeling in time and place. The same outline gives a reason to think that fence is argued for rather
than an accident of phrasing: it takes up impassibility directly under
unchangeableness, noting that some theologians have denied that God has passions
or emotions and answering that the Bible "seems to consistently affirm that God
has emotions."

Two limits keep this from being more than it is. The outline is somebody else's
summary of the 1994 chapter, hosted on the author's site: good evidence of that
chapter's headings, definitions and pagination, no evidence at all of Grudem's
own phrasing beyond the sentences it quotes, and not the chapter's argument. No
part of it is witnessed for the 2020 text. And the five *yet* clauses are not
all of one kind. Four are about God — God acts, feels, sees, acts differently —
while the unity clause is about us, saying that "we see" attributes emphasized
at different times. A reading on which that last clause reports our perception
rather than a distinction in God would leave the unity definition standing
wholly inside the classical settings, and neither the sentence nor the outline
decides between the two readings.
