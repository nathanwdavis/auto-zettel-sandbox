There is an experimental literature on note taking, and it does not reach the
claims a slip-box makes. Not because the experiments are weak — the best of
them is preregistered, replicated and meta-analysed — but because they measure
a different thing.

What they manipulate is the medium of capture during a single lecture, and
what they measure is a quiz taken the same day, usually minutes later
([[urry-et-al-replicate-the-notes-effect-and-not-the-learning-effect--202609032205]]).
Every study in the pooled set of eight matched on those criteria: laptop versus
longhand, same-day quiz, video lecture, undergraduates. The dependent variables
are quiz score, word count, and verbatim overlap. A slip-box claim is about
none of that. It is about what happens to a note *after* it is written — whether
it is found again, whether it is linked, whether it recombines with notes made
years apart. The experiments stop before the interesting part starts.

The gap is not this note's invention; the replicators name it themselves. Their
third recommendation for future work is that studies "should, ideally, include
a no-notes control condition to see the effect of taking notes regardless of
medium," because focusing on laptop versus longhand "encourages dichotomous
thinking when the story likely is more complicated." Read plainly, that says
this literature contains no test of whether taking notes helps at all. It
compares two ways of doing it.

So the honest statement of what this base can claim: on the evidence it holds,
the causal claims in its `zettelkasten method` cluster are unmeasured. Not
disproved — unmeasured, which is a different and less comfortable position.
[[atomic-notes-compound-over-time--202701010001]] asserts that reuse compounds;
[[a-slip-box-surprises-only-after-critical-mass--202609010113]] asserts a
threshold effect on surprise; [[one-idea-per-note-enables-reuse--202701010002]]
asserts that atomicity is what enables reuse. Each is a claim about
accumulation over years, and the strongest experimental evidence anyone has is
about fifteen minutes.

Two things follow. First, the historical evidence this base does hold —
Blair's early modern note takers whose files were bequeathed, bought, and used
by people who never did the reading
([[systematic-method-is-what-makes-a-note-usable-by-someone-who-did-not-take-it--202609032110]])
— is doing more work than it looks like, because it is the only evidence here
that reaches past the moment of capture at all. It is existence proof, not
measurement. Second, the direction to look is not more laptop-versus-longhand
studies. It is the literature on retrieval practice, the generation effect and
transactive memory, where the manipulations are about what happens to material
after it is recorded. That is where a test of a slip-box claim could come from,
and this base has captured none of it.
