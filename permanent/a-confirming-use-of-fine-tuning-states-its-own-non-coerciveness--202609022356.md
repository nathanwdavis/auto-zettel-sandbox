If design arguments confirm belief without grounding it, the people using them
that way should say so in their own voice — otherwise the distinction is only a
defensive move made on their behalf. They do say so, and the clearest statement
puts the disclaimer in the same paragraph as the claim.

John Polkinghorne, setting out what contemporary natural theology is for,
draws the line against the tradition he is revising: Aquinas and Paley "spoke
in terms of 'proofs' of God's existence", whereas "Contemporary natural
theology is more modest in its character. Its aim is not logical coerciveness
but insightful understanding, and the claim being made is that theism explains
more than atheism can" (see
[[polkinghorne-the-science-and-religion-debate--202609022347]], p. 2). That
last clause is comparative and explanatory — a claim about which hypothesis
makes the observations less surprising. It is the form a confirmation takes,
and it is deliberately not the form a proof takes.

Two paragraphs later he names the exits from his own argument rather than
closing them: "Those who refuse this insight are either driven to regard
fine-tuning as another incredibly happy accident, or to embrace the
extraordinary supposition that there is, in fact, a vast multiverse" (same
source, p. 3; see
[[polkinghorne-trades-coerciveness-for-insight-and-names-the-exits--202609022351]]).
"Extraordinary supposition" charges the alternative with implausibility, not
with contradiction. The dissenter is described as *refusing an insight* and
paying a price for it — not as reasoning badly.

So the confirming office is not a retreat performed after the argument is
attacked; it is how the argument is issued. This is the same division of labour
the Reformed statements make between knowledge possessed and knowledge
demonstrated (see
[[design-arguments-argue-for-what-the-doctrine-says-is-already-possessed--202609011041]]),
now stated by a physicist about physics rather than by a dogmatician about
Scripture. What follows for assessment is that "it does not compel" is not an
objection to such an argument. It is the argument's own description of itself,
and the thing to evaluate instead is how much less surprising the evidence
actually becomes.
