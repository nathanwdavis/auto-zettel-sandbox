"Fallen nature" does not name one position. The two writers on file who use
it mean different things by it.

For Meyendorff the fallen humanity Christ assumed is the human condition
under death: "It is the mortal, corruptible, and fallen humanity which was
assumed by the Logos"; "mortality and corruptibility were assumed by the Son
of God from the beginning of his human life"; Jesus "lived in time, "grew in
wisdom," did not know, suffered, and died"; he was tempted, and overcame
"the temptations inherent in fallen humanity"
([[meyendorff-holds-that-eastern-patristic-thought-knows-no--202610081437]]).
John of Damascus, whom Meyendorff quotes against the Julianists, gives the
list the same shape: Christ "assumed all the natural and innocent passions of man",
those that "have entered into the life of man owing to the condemnation by
reason of the transgression", hunger, thirst, weariness, tears, corruption,
the shrinking from death, fear and agony, but "all man's attributes save
sin"
([[john-of-damascus-has-christ-assume-the-natural-and-innocent--202610081439]]).
Nothing in the Meyendorff pages on file puts an inclination to evil in
Christ's flesh.

Irving does. He would maintain "that Christ's flesh was as rebellious as
ours, as fallen as ours", and credits the Holy Ghost with "subduing,
restraining, conquering, the evil propensities of the fallen manhood"
([[irving-holds-that-christ-took-fallen-flesh-and-was-kept--202610031823]]).

The base already draws the mainstream line between infirmity and fault:
Christ took the penalties of the fall, hunger, weariness, mortality, real
temptation, and not its disordered inclination, and Irving moves the
inclination across that line
([[christ-shared-the-penalties-of-the-fall-but-not-its--202610031827]]).
Meyendorff's "fallen" stays on the infirmity side of it. He calls fallen what
Aquinas would call the penalties of the fall. So the Orthodox use of the
word is closer in substance to the mainstream Western account than to
Irving's, even though Irving and Meyendorff share the word.
