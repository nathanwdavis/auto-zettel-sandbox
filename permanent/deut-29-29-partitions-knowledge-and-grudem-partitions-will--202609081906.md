The secret/revealed distinction rests on one Pentateuch verse, and Grudem quotes
it in full: "The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but the things that
are revealed belong to us and to our children forever, that we may do all the
words of this law" (Deut. 29:29,
[[grudem-splits-gods-will-into-necessary-free-secret-and-revealed--202609081904]]).
What that verse partitions is *disclosure*. On one side are things God has not
made known; on the other, things he has, given for a stated purpose — obedience.
It is a claim about the distribution of knowledge between God and Israel, and it
says nothing about God willing in two modes.

Grudem's doctrine partitions something else. His secret will "includes most of
his hidden decrees by which he governs the universe and determines everything
that will happen"; his revealed will is "God's declared will concerning what we
should do." That is a distinction between *decreeing* and *commanding* — between
what God brings about and what God requires — and the two can diverge, which is
the whole point of the apparatus: Joseph's brothers were commanded not to sell
him and decreed to do so. Deut. 29:29 asserts no such divergence. Read on its
own it is compatible with a single divine will of which part is published and
part withheld, which is precisely the reading the doctrine needs to go beyond.

So the verse supports the distinction only after the extra step has been taken
elsewhere. Once you already hold that God decrees what he forbids, Deut. 29:29
supplies the vocabulary — secret, revealed — and the assurance that a withheld
remainder is legitimate rather than a defect in the doctrine. That is real work,
and it is the work of a warrant for reticence rather than a proof of two wills.
The pattern is the same one visible in how the individual texts get sorted
([[grudems-two-wills-are-sorted-from-outside-the-verse--202609081905]]): the
frame arrives from outside, and Scripture is then read through it.

There is a further reason to notice which verse this is. Deut. 29:29 belongs to
the Torah, the one body of scripture Judaism and Samaritanism hold with
Christianity, and its Jewish reading has generally gone the other way — the
withheld things are God's business and the revealed things are ours to *do*,
which is a charter for practice rather than a partition inside the divine will.
This base has no Jewish or Samaritan commentary on the verse on file, so that is
stated here as the reason to go looking, not as a finding.

**Limits.** Grudem does not claim Deut. 29:29 proves the two wills by itself; he
introduces it with "Surely a distinction between different aspects of God's will
is evident in many passages of Scripture," so the verse is offered as one of
many and as the source of the terms. This note is about what that verse can and
cannot carry, not a claim that the doctrine is unsupported — the argument for it
is largely in chapter 16, and in Piper's essay, which is not on file. The base
has captured no Hebrew text of Deut. 29:29 and no commentary on it in any
tradition; the verse is here only as Grudem quotes it in the ESV.
