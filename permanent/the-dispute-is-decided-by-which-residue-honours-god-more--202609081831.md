Chapter 16 does not end by claiming that the Reformed position has answered
everything. It ends by conceding that it has not, and then proposing a criterion
for choosing between two incomplete positions. Grudem lists the two questions
his own side cannot answer — "Exactly how can God not be blamed for evil when he
ordains that we do evil willingly?" and "Exactly how can God cause us to choose
something willingly?" — and says of them that "the effect of these unanswered
questions is to increase our appreciation of the greatness of God." The
Arminian's residue (how God knows the future, why he permits evil against his
will, whether he will certainly triumph) "tends to diminish the greatness of
God—his omniscience, his omnipotence, and the absolute reliability of his
promises for the future"
([[grudem-answers-the-arminian-by-comparing-unanswered-questions--202609081825]],
from [[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]]).

The criterion is neither exegetical nor logical. Both positions are granted a
mystery; what separates them is the *direction* in which each mystery points the
believer's estimate of God. Call it a doxological tie-breaker. It is applied
openly and as the chapter's last word, and the closing sentence names the
attribute at stake in terms this base already holds from pp. 232-233: the
Arminian position "diminishes the wisdom and skill of God the Creator."

Two things follow. First, the tie-breaker only operates once the exegetical
argument has been run to a draw, and Grudem plainly does not think it a draw —
the preceding sections argue at length from texts. So this is a closing
consideration, not the load-bearing one, and citing it as Grudem's *reason* for
Calvinism would misrepresent the chapter. Second, it is an unusually candid
instance of something this base tracks elsewhere: an argument doing work other
than establishing its conclusion. The distinction between what grounds a belief
and what confirms or commends it is already recorded here from Plantinga and
Calvin's *helps*
([[design-arguments-can-confirm-belief-they-do-not-ground--202609011510]]), and
this is the same shape in dogmatics — a consideration offered to a reader who
already accepts the framework, sorting residues by their effect on reverence
rather than by their truth-value.

That connection also sharpens a limit the base put on its own earlier reading.
[[wisdom-in-a-life-is-read-off-the-promised-goal--202609081649]] found the
wisdom passage insensitive to what happens, and credited it with not claiming
evidential support it lacked. Chapter 16 shows the same author, on the same
doctrine, being similarly explicit about where an argument stops: the two
questions are named, not buried, and the appeal that follows them is labelled as
an appeal to God's greatness. Whatever one makes of the criterion, it is stated
rather than smuggled.

**Limits.** The criterion is stated once, in a closing sub-section, and Grudem
does not defend it or ask what would follow if the two residues pointed the same
way. Whether the Arminian would accept his characterisation of their residue is
untested here, and by construction they would not: the framing already assumes
that a God whose purposes can be frustrated is a lesser God, which is the point
at issue. This note describes the move and does not endorse it, and the
comparison with the confirm/ground distinction is an analogy of function, not a
claim that Grudem holds Plantinga's epistemology.
