Before the Social Sciences Replication Project collected any data, it asked
researchers in the field which of its twenty-one findings would replicate,
through a survey and through a prediction market in which they traded on the
outcomes. The market's average forecast was a 63.4% replication rate and the
survey's 60.6%. The observed rate was 61.9%. The forecasts also tracked the
individual studies, not merely the aggregate: both belief measures correlate
strongly with which particular findings came back
([[camerer-et-al-evaluating-the-replicability-of-social-science-experiments--202609041855]],
[[the-ssrp-replicated-one-result-per-paper-and-said-so-in-its-limitations--202609041900]]).

The inference the project draws from this is the sharp one, and it is not about
forecasting. Chance failures cannot be predicted; these were predicted;
therefore the failures were not chance. What the market was reading were
"systematic differences between the studies that replicated and those that did
not" — features visible on the face of a published paper, before anyone tried
to reproduce it. The community, in aggregate, could already tell.

That is a claim about knowledge, and an uncomfortable one. The information was
distributed but not acted on: it existed well enough to be priced, and it did
not stop the findings from being published in the two most prestigious journals
in science, cited, and built upon. A field can know which of its results are
unreliable and publish them anyway, because knowing-in-aggregate and
knowing-in-a-decision are different things, and only the first is what a market
elicits. The peer review that gatekeeps publication asks a single reviewer for a
verdict; the market asks everyone for a bet, and pays them for being right.

For a knowledge base the practical form is a question to ask of any striking
finding: *would the field have bet on this?* The signal is usually available
without a market — the P value just under 0.05, the small sample, the effect
that is surprising in the direction the author wanted. This repository has used
those cues piecemeal. What the SSRP adds is evidence that reading them is not
idle scepticism but a measurably calibrated skill, and that a base which
records a finding without recording how confident the field was in it has
discarded information it could have had for free.
