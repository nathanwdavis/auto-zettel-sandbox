An effect's size is a property of the contrast that produced it, not of the
thing on the good side of the contrast. In the classroom literature on
retrieval practice, the thing on the other side is almost always a student
doing less: re-reading a review sheet (*k* = 19), sitting through the same
lesson without quizzes (*k* = 14), or being scored on the items the quiz
happened not to cover (*k* = 12)
([[agarwal-nunes-and-blunt-screened-two-thousand-abstracts-and-counted-what-they-found--202609040405]],
preprint p. 23).

The interesting cell is the fourth and it is nearly empty. Where the comparison
was not *less activity* but *less of the same activity* — some quizzing against
more quizzing — the advantage collapses: "Only five experiments included a
comparison condition of fewer opportunities for retrieval practice (e.g.,
comparing two exams vs. weekly quizzes); experiments using this type of
comparison yielded small and very small effect sizes" (p. 23). Five experiments
is far too few to settle anything. But it is the only place in the review where
the question is a dose question, and every one of those five came back small.

The authors do not treat this as a footnote. Their third recommendation for
future research asks for the comparison to be raised: "More stringent and
realistic comparisons to retrieval practice in classroom settings include
traditional lectures, flipped classroom activities, think-pair-share
discussions, and student presentations" (p. 38). That is an admission that the
literature's realistic-alternative comparison has mostly not been run yet.

**What this costs the claim next door.**
[[retrieval-not-re-exposure-is-what-makes-learning-last--202609040340]] is
unaffected as stated — its two primary experiments were built precisely as
retrieval against exposure-matched re-study, and the crossover it reports is
real. What is affected is any slide from that claim to a practical one. *Pull
it out of your head rather than read it again* is supported. *More retrieval
is better than less* is a different sentence, and the five experiments that
tested it did not support it.

**And it is the second sentence that a slip-box would need.** Nobody keeps a
zettelkasten instead of re-reading a review sheet; the alternatives a note-taker
actually chooses between are all effortful, and the choice is about *how much*
of which practice. The literature has measured the easy contrast and asked for
the hard one. That is the same shape of gap as
[[having-the-notes-to-hand-buys-present-performance-not-durable-knowledge--202609040345]]
records from the other direction — there, what was measured was having the
notes versus not having them, over one week, in one room.
