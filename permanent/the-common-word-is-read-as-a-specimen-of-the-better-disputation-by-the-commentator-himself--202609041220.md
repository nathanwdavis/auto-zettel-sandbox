Commenting on the command at Q 29:46 to dispute with the People of the Book only
in the better way, al-Maturidi points to the other verse: `والأوّل كقوله:
{ تَعَالَوْاْ إِلَىٰ كَلِمَةٍ سَوَآءٍ بَيْنَنَا وَبَيْنَكُمْ... } الآية
[آل عمران: 64]، والمجادلة الحسنة هي التي جاء بها الكتاب ويوجبها العقل` — and the
first is like His saying, "Come to a word common between us and you"; and the
good disputation is that which the Book brought and which reason requires. The
"common word", on this reading, is not a statement of shared ground but an
**example of how to argue**.

**One ambiguity, stated before it is built on.** The sentence begins
`والأوّل`, "and the first", and the capture does not put beyond doubt which
first is meant: most plausibly the first limb of the distinction he has just
drawn — dispute with those of them who affirm, do not dispute with those who
conceal — and possibly the first of the three construals he opened with. On
either reading Q 3:64 is being produced as an instance of what Q 29:46 commands,
which is the only thing this note rests on. What would settle the referent is a
second copy of the *Ta'wilat* with different punctuation, and there is none.

**Why that is more than a cross-reference.** This base has treated Q 3:64 and
Q 29:46 as a pair since the first commentary sweep, and the pairing was its own:
the two verses are the ones most on-point for the same-God question, so every
commentator has been asked about both. That is a reader's construction, and a
reader's construction is exactly the kind of thing that can smuggle a frame into
a finding — as this cluster has already learned once, when its "asymmetric
silence" turned out to be an artefact of which hosts earlier cycles used. Here
the pairing turns out to be the commentator's too, and he joins the verses on a
ground the base had not proposed: not that both are about a shared God, but that
one shows what the other commands.

**What it does to the summons reading.** That the classical commentaries read
Q 3:64 as a summons rather than as a description of ground already held is
established from four of them, by what they fill the "word" with — the exclusion
of every rival object of worship, and in two cases the cross by name
([[four-commentaries-read-the-common-word-as-a-summons-and-two-name-the-cross-among-its-exclusions--202609040830]],
[[the-common-word-is-a-summons-in-its-classical-reading-not-ground-already-shared--202609040720]]).
This is a different kind of evidence for the same conclusion, and a stronger one
per instance: it is not another commentator voting the same way but a structural
placement. A verse cited as an instance of *how to conduct a dispute* is being
classified as procedure. You do not offer a shared premise as a specimen of good
argumentative manner; you offer an opening move.

**The company it keeps in the same passage.** The classification is not a stray
remark. Al-Maturidi draws from the same verse a defence of disputation as such —
`ثم فيه دلالة جواز المناظرة والمجادلة مع الكفرة في الدين`, in it is an
indication of the permissibility of debate and disputation with the unbelievers
in religion — and attributes the contrary position to `جهلهم بحجج الإسلام
وبراهينه`, ignorance of Islam's proofs. A theologian reading Q 29:46 as a charter
for *kalam* will naturally read Q 3:64 as a specimen of it, and the same reading
that makes the "common word" a good opening move is what makes the shared-God
clause inside Q 29:46 dispensable enough to elide.

**What it does not establish.** One commentator makes this link; nothing here
says the classical tradition generally read Q 3:64 as an instance of Q 29:46's
command, and the direction of the base's own pairing is unaffected either way.
Nor does it settle whether the commentator's frame is faithful to the verses: the
"good disputation" and the "common word" may be joined in his exegesis and remain
distinct in the text.
