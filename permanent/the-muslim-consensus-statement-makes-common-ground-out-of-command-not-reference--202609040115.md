When Muslim scholars addressed Christians collectively and at length, they did
not answer the same-God question. They changed which question was being asked.

*A Common Word Between Us and You* (2007) is the largest thing of its kind the
tradition has produced — a consensus letter to the leaders of the Christian
churches. It never asks whether Muslims and Christians worship the same God,
and never asserts that they do as a claim to be defended. What it argues, at
the length of three sections, is that the same two commandments bind both:
love of the one God, and love of the neighbour. Its own statement of the common
ground puts a doctrine and two commands together — "the Unity of God, love of
Him, and love of the neighbour form a common ground upon which Islam and
Christianity (and Judaism) are founded" — and the argument is spent almost
entirely on the second and third
([[a-common-word-builds-common-ground-from-the-two-commandments--202609040110]],
[[a-common-word-between-us-and-you--202609040058]], secs. I-III).

**This is a third move, not a position within the reference/description
dispute.** That dispute, as this base has framed it, asks whether the traditions
pick out the same being (reference) or give the same account of him
(description), and sorts positions by which question decides
([[the-same-god-question-turns-on-reference-versus-description--202608311942]]).
The letter takes neither horn. It concedes descriptive divergence outright —
"Muslims recognize Jesus Christ as the Messiah, not in the same way Christians
do" — and treats the concession as no obstacle. And it presupposes a shared
referent rather than arguing for one: it cites that God raised a messenger in
every nation, and that Muhammad was told nothing "but what already was said to
the messengers before thee", but spends no section establishing co-reference
and rests nothing on it. What it puts in the place where an answer would go is
an obligation both sides already acknowledge.

**Why this is a limit on the framing and not a footnote to it.** The
reference/description distinction is a genuine analytic gain, and this note does
not withdraw it. But it was drawn in this base from Pauw, Nostra Aetate, Tachin,
Mohler and Vroom — Christian and secular voices, every one. The claim it invites
is that the dispute *is* two questions wearing one sentence. The most
significant Muslim intervention in that dispute uses neither question, which
means the framing is at best incomplete as a description of the argument as it
is actually conducted, and at worst a Christian-philosophical apparatus
presented as the neutral shape of a dispute one side declines to have in those
terms.

**The sharpest evidence that the letter is doing something other than claiming
co-reference.** The "common word" it offers is 3:64, and one of that verse's
three clauses is that "we shall ascribe no partner unto Him" — the Qur'anic
formula whose contested application is precisely to trinitarian Christology, and
the same idiom the Qur'an elsewhere turns against saying "Three"
([[the-quran-supplies-a-shared-god-formula-and-an-excluding-one--202609040120]]).
A document arguing for a shared referent would not build its common ground out
of the tradition's own exclusion formula. A document arguing that the same thing
is *commanded* of both can, because on that reading the clause states an
obligation rather than adjudicating a description.

The nearest thing to this move already in the base is the Jewish category of
*shituf*, which likewise declines to answer who is being worshipped and instead
grades the worshipper
([[shituf-grades-the-worshipper-instead-of-identifying-the-worshipped--202609011542]]).
Two traditions, asked the same question from outside, both answer by relocating
it onto the worshipper's obligations. That convergence is this note's own
observation and is not claimed by either source.

**What this note does not claim.** Not that Muslim theology *has* no view about
co-reference; a consensus letter written to be signed by many is poor evidence
of what its signatories individually hold, and the argumentative registers —
al-Ghazali on who falls outside the faith, Ibn Taymiyya's reply to Christianity
— are still uncaptured here. Not that the letter is representative of Islamic
opinion generally; it is irenic by design and addressed outward. And not that
the reference/description framing is wrong about the Christian and analytic
debate it was drawn from, where it remains accurate.

**On the missing `contested` tag.** This note bears on a contested question and
its central move — reading the letter's silence about co-reference as
deliberate relocation rather than oversight — is an inference that a second
witness would strengthen. It is deliberately *not* tagged `contested`, because
that tag carries a three-independent-source bar in this repository and this note
rests on one document read directly. Tagging it while sourcing it from a single
letter would either fail the gate honestly or invite link-stuffing to clear it.
What would earn the tag is a second Muslim source arguing the same question in
its own voice — Saritoprak, al-Ghazali's *Faysal al-Tafriqa*, or Ibn Taymiyya's
*al-Jawab al-Sahih* — and that corroboration is filed as open work in INBOX
rather than assumed here.
