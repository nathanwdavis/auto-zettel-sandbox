The dispute between Grudem and open theism is not over logic. Both parties
accept that exhaustive foreknowledge of human choices leaves those choices, in
some sense, not free. Clark Pinnock, as Grudem quotes him, came to open theism
by exactly this route: "I knew the Calvinist argument that exhaustive
foreknowledge was tantamount to predestination because it implies the fixity of
all things from 'eternity past,' and I could not shake off its logical force",
and so he gave up the foreknowledge. Grudem, at the end of the same section,
concedes the same inference in his own voice: "If God knows all our thoughts,
words, and actions long before they occur, then there must be some sense in
which our choices are not absolutely free" — and gives up the absolute freedom
([[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]], distilled at
[[grudem-defines-omniscience-as-one-simple-eternal-act-and-rejects-open-theism--202609061915]]).

That is the whole difference: one entailment, two sides, and a decision about
which side Scripture fixes. Pinnock takes freedom as given and reads the
knowledge down; Grudem takes the knowledge as given — on the Isaiah argument
recorded in
[[grudem-answers-open-theism-with-isaiahs-test-of-deity-not-with-the-definition-of-omniscience--202609061921]]
— and reads the freedom down. What he keeps is deliberately described without
the word: Augustine's "reasonable self-determination", valued precisely because
"his statement does not involve the terms free or freedom", and glossed as two
affirmations, that we think about what to do and consciously decide, and that
"our choices really do determine what will happen". If the word *free* is
wanted, it is Ware's "freedom of inclination": "we are free when we do what we
most want to do." Whether that is freedom in the sense the open theist was
protecting, Grudem does not argue here; he says outright that reaching "a
satisfactory understanding of the way in which our choices are 'free'" is "not
the really important issue", and defers the rest to the chapter on providence.

The consequence for reading the debate is that the standard framing — one
side affirms foreknowledge and the other denies it — hides an agreement that
does the real work. Neither party in this section tries to hold exhaustive
foreknowledge and libertarian freedom together. The position that would deny
the entailment itself, and keep both, is not represented; Grudem does not
mention it, and Pinnock's "could not shake off its logical force" is the
report of someone who looked for it and did not find it.

**Sources and their limits.** Grudem's concession and his adoption of
Augustine's phrase and Ware's definition are in the second edition's own words,
from the owner's export. Pinnock is known only as Grudem quotes him, without a
footnote, so what Pinnock meant by "the Calvinist argument" and whether he
considered and rejected a compatibility view cannot be checked here; the last
paragraph's "not represented" is a claim about this section and not about the
literature. Grudem's fuller treatment is in his chapter on providence, which is
not on file, and this note should be read as the position of the omniscience
section alone.

**Amended 2026-09-08, from chapter 16.** Two of this note's statements were
bounded to chapter 12 and can now be discharged against the chapter it deferred
to. (1) The deferral itself: the freedom Grudem keeps is named in chapter 16 as
Ware's "freedom of inclination," which a footnote there glosses as "compatibilist
freedom," and which Grudem says "does not rule out the idea that our choices may
ultimately be determined by God in ways invisible to us"
([[the-freedom-grudem-deferred-is-compatibilist-freedom--202609081827]]). The
reading this note reached by inference is confirmed in the primary text. (2) The
last paragraph said that the position which denies the entailment and keeps both
"is not represented," and said so explicitly of that section rather than of the
literature. Chapter 16 does represent it — Jack Cottrell's foreknowledge without
foreordination, quoted at length — and answers it with this note's own
entailment, now running as an argument rather than a concession
([[the-foreknowledge-entailment-is-a-weapon-in-chapter-16--202609081829]]).
Nothing above is withdrawn; the bound held.
