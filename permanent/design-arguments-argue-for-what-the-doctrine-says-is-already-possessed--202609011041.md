The knowability doctrine and the design arguments share their conclusion but
assign it different epistemic offices: the doctrine claims a knowledge that
is *possessed*, the arguments supply a knowledge that is *demonstrated* --
and the classic Reformed statements hold both at once without treating this
as a conflict.

Hodge is the sharpest case, because he does both in one volume. The
knowledge of God is innate -- "due to our constitution" -- and his section
heading rules out the rival account by name: it "is not due to a Process of
Reasoning," since inferential routes "are possible only for cultivated
minds" while the belief is universal. Yet in the very next chapter he states
and defends the teleological syllogism in full (see
[[hodge-systematic-theology-volume-1--202609011033]], Part I chs. I-II).
The only coherent reading, and the order of his chapters enforces it -- the
arguments precede a knowability chapter that opens "Having considered the
arguments in favor of the doctrine that God is..." -- is that the argument's
office is not to originate belief but to vindicate and articulate a
knowledge already there. Calvin has the same structure in different idiom:
the "seed of religion" is sown in every mind by natural instinct, and the
display of God in the works functions as testimony that leaves even "the
most illiterate and stupid" inexcusable -- not as a demonstration mounted to
win over doubters (see
[[calvin-institutes-of-the-christian-religion--202609011032]], I.iii and I.v).

This is the second thing they have in common, and it explains a persistent
feature of the design-argument literature: within the traditions that
confess knowability, the arguments' failure to *compel* has never been felt
as fatal, because compelling was never their assignment. The doctrine
carries the claim; the arguments parade it.
