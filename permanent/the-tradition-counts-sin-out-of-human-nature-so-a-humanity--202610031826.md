Chalcedon confesses Christ "consubstantial with us as touching his manhood;
made in all things like unto us, sin only excepted"
([[chalcedon-confesses-christ-consubstantial-with-us-and-like--202610031825]]).
In one sentence the council counts him fully of our nature and counts sin out
of the likeness. That only works if sin is no part of what a human nature is.

Aquinas states the premise outright. A sinful Christ would prove nothing about
his humanity, "since sin does not belong to human nature, whereof God is the
cause", and so "Christ nowise assumed the defect of sin", "either original or
actual"
([[aquinas-denies-christ-both-original-sin-and-the-fomes-of--202610031818]]).
Calvin's version puts the standard case in view: Christ's generation was
"pure and spotless, such as it would have been before Adam's fall"
([[calvin-rejects-the-father-line-explanation-of-christ-s--202610031820]]).
The Larger Catechism's "yet without sin" (Q. 37) rests on the same footing
([[the-westminster-larger-catechism-states-original-sin-for--202610031821]]),
and Ineffabilis Deus applies the same logic to Mary, who "had only nature in
common with them, not sin"
([[ineffabilis-deus-defines-mary-s-preservation-from-original--202610031822]]).

On this shared premise, asking whether Christ had a sin nature is asking
whether he had a defect, not whether his humanity was complete. A sin nature
is a corruption of human nature, and Adam before the fall is the proof that
the nature can exist without it.
