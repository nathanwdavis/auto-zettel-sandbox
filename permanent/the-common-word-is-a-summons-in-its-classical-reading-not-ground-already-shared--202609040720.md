The verse that gives the largest modern Muslim overture to Christianity its
name does not, in the tradition's foundational commentary, describe anything the
two parties already hold. It issues a demand, and the reports attached to it
record the demand being turned down.

al-Tabari glosses `كَلِمَةٍ سَوَاءٍ` in Q 3:64 as `كلمة عدل` — an *equitable*
word — and then says what the equitable word is: "that we affirm God's oneness
and worship none besides Him, and disavow every object of worship other than
Him, associating nothing with Him." The clause about not taking one another as
lords he reads as withholding obedience to any human in what God has forbidden,
and withholding the prostration owed to God alone. He extends the address to
both communities on a principle rather than a report: worship devoted to God
alone is owed by every creature who is commanded and forbidden, and *ahl
al-kitab* covers Torah and Gospel alike with nothing in the verse singling out
either ([[tabari-on-the-common-word-and-the-shared-god-clause--202609040705]],
ad Q 3:64).

**Every English rendering of al-Tabari here is this repository's own working
translation of the captured Arabic**, with the Arabic printed beside each
rendering that carries weight
([[tabari-jami-al-bayan-on-3-64-and-29-46--202609040700]]). The one quotation
from *A Common Word* below is the letter's own published English.

**The occasion reports make the direction of the verse unmistakable.** One
family of reports puts it at the Jews of Medina, with Ibn Jurayj adding that the
Prophet called them to it and they refused. The other puts it at the Christian
delegation of Najran, and Ibn Zayd's version is the sharpest: after the
delegation refused on the question of Jesus, the instruction was to call them to
something easier — 3:64 is the easier thing — `فأبوا أن يقبلوا هذا ولا الآخر`,
*and they refused to accept this or the other*. A verse transmitted with a
refusal attached is not being transmitted as a statement of common ground.

**What this does to the 2007 letter, and what it does not.** *A Common Word
Between Us and You* takes its title from this verse and argues that the basis
for peace "already exists" in the foundational principles of both faiths. On the
classical reading the verse is a call to something the addressees are not doing:
its content is tawhid plus the disavowal of every rival object of worship, and
its recorded reception is refusal. So the letter's use of the verse is a
re-reading, not a continuation — the same words turned from summons into
description.

That is an observation about how the verse has been read, not a verdict on
whether the letter's reading is wrong. Both readings have purchase on the words:
3:64 is an invitation, and an invitation can be glossed either by what it asks
for or by what makes it askable. What the classical reading removes is the
option of treating the letter's reading as simply what the verse has always
meant. It also strengthens, from a direction its author did not have, the
observation that the letter builds its common ground out of the tradition's own
exclusion formula
([[the-muslim-consensus-statement-makes-common-ground-out-of-command-not-reference--202609040115]]):
al-Tabari confirms that the clause is doing exclusionary work, because he makes
disavowal of every other *maʿbud* the content of the equitable word itself.

**Consistent with the base's reading of the Qur'an, and a caution about it.**
That 3:64 holds invitation and exclusion in one verse is already recorded here
([[the-quran-supplies-a-shared-god-formula-and-an-excluding-one--202609040120]]).
What is added is that the earliest sustained commentary resolves the tension by
letting the exclusion govern — and that this resolution, being an interpretive
commitment rather than a reading that can be quoted, is exactly the kind of
choice that note says has to be defended rather than cited. al-Tabari defends
his; the observation stands.

**Limits.** One commentator, on one verse, with al-Qurtubi, al-Razi and Ibn
Kathir uncaptured. And the reports themselves are al-Tabari's transmission of
earlier authorities, not independent evidence of the verse's occasion — this
note reports what the commentary carries, and makes no claim about what happened
at Najran.

**AMENDMENT, 2026-09-04 08:00Z cycle: the first limit is largely closed, and
this reading held.** al-Qurtubi, al-Baghawi and Ibn Kathir were captured on
Q 3:64 and all three gloss `سواء` as justice or equity, fill the word's content
with the exclusion of every rival object of worship, and read the closing clause
as a restatement of the Muslim position after refusal; two of them name the
cross among the exclusions, and all three quote the letter to Heraclius
([[four-commentaries-read-the-common-word-as-a-summons-and-two-name-the-cross-among-its-exclusions--202609040830]]).
Unlike the co-reference gloss on Q 29:46, which no successor repeats, this
reading travelled. al-Razi is still uncaptured; a modern commentator's use of
`مشتركة` — shared — of the same word is recorded as a qualification in the note
just linked, not suppressed here.
