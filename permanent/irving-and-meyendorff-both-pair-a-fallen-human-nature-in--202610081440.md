Irving, a pastor whose sermons on the incarnation carry a dedication dated
1828, and Meyendorff, writing from St Vladimir's Orthodox Seminary in 1989,
work in different traditions and both say that the humanity Christ assumed
was fallen. They also make the same move to
keep sin out of him: they deny that he is a human person.

Irving: "You have original sin taken away in Him by the manner of His
conception. He is not, as it were, an individual of the sinful individuals;
He is not a human person; He never had personal subsistence as a mere man".
The manhood is "in its fallen state", yet the one person is "wholly without
sin, holy and blameless in the sight of God"
([[irving-holds-that-original-sin-is-kept-from-christ-by-the--202610081436]]).

Meyendorff: "Christ was not a human hypostasis." The humanity is "our common
humanity which he assumed in its fallen state", and sin is "a conscious,
personal act of rebellion against divine will". So "It is because he was
God, not as "mere man," that Jesus was able to overcome the temptations
inherent in fallen humanity"
([[meyendorff-holds-that-eastern-patristic-thought-knows-no--202610081437]]).
His Byzantine Theology gives the principle behind it: "only this free,
personal mind can commit sin and incur the concomitant "guilt"", and sin is
"never an act of nature"
([[meyendorff-finds-a-greek-patristic-consensus-that-the--202610081438]]).

The shared logic is that fallenness is predicated of the nature and sin and
guilt of the person. If the only person in Christ is the Word, a fallen
nature does not make a guilty or sinning subject.

They differ on what the fallen nature contains: Irving gives the flesh "the
evil propensities of the fallen manhood"
([[irving-s-fallen-flesh-christology-disputes-the-source-of--202610031830]]),
and nothing in the Meyendorff pages on file does.
