Commenting on `وما كان من المشركين` at Q 3:67, three verses after the
common-word summons, al-Zamakhshari offers as a second sense: `أو أراد
بالمشركين اليهود والنصارى لإشراكهم به عزيراً والمسيح` — or He meant by "the
associators" the Jews and the Christians, because they associate 'Uzayr and the
Messiah with Him. The Qur'anic term that elsewhere marks the pagans is here
extended, in a classical commentary, to the People of the Book.

**Why this matters to the question the base is working.** This repository has
been asking whether the Islamic tradition, argued from inside, treats the God of
the Christians as the same God. The load-bearing distinction on the Muslim side
is between *ahl al-kitab* and *mushrikun*: the first is a category the Qur'an
addresses as recipients of revelation, the second one it excludes. If the two
categories can overlap, the shared-God formula and the exclusion of associators
are not talking past one another — they can bite on the same people.

**What is actually claimed here, and it is modest.** al-Zamakhshari gives this
as one of two possible senses (`أو أراد`), does not prefer it, and does not
develop it into a ruling. The primary sense he gives is the ordinary one, and
the verse's own subject is Abraham. So this is a classical commentator finding
the extension *available* in the text, not a jurist deciding a case. That is
still the first evidence in this base that the extension is made at all, and it
is made in the commentary on the passage that carries the summons.

**How it sits with what the base already holds.** It runs alongside al-Razi's
reading of "except those of them who do wrong" at Q 29:46 as excluding those who
`أشركوا` by asserting a son and a third of three
([[the-classical-exclusion-of-trinitarians-is-made-inside-the-verse-that-carries-the-shared-god-clause--202609040925]]),
and the two are independent: different verses, different commentators, different
centuries, the same move. It is also the nearest thing the base has yet found to
the juristic question of whether Christians count as *mushrikun*, still answered
from tafsir rather than from *fiqh*, which remains untried.

**The comparison worth noticing.** The Jewish category *shituf* grades the
worshipper's conception without denying that God is the one addressed
([[shituf-grades-the-worshipper-instead-of-identifying-the-worshipped--202609011542]]).
The move recorded here runs the other way: it reclassifies the worshipper into
the excluded category outright. The two traditions have a comparable problem —
what to do with a monotheist whose monotheism you judge defective — and their
available answers are not symmetrical.
