Grudem states the doctrine in one sentence: God "eternally exists as three
persons, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and each person is fully God, and there
is one God" ([[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]], distilled at
[[grudem-trinity-three-persons-one-god--202609050548]]). That sentence treats the
doctrine's two classical rivals unequally, in the same way the creed of 325
does. A lesson outline of the chapter the sentence introduces prints, further
on, the proposition the sentence withholds. That is good reason to expect the
chapter itself closes the gap the conciliar record left open; it is not proof
that it does.

**Sources and their limits.** The sentence is witnessed at page 226 by three
documents, two of them independent of the lesson outline and both printing the
1994 edition; a fourth page turns out to be the outline republished. No witness
gives a second-edition page. That outline is posted on Grudem's website beside
his lecture audio for chapter 14 and dated there 2006/08/27: it carries no
byline, and its file metadata names a third party, so
it is an independent witness to the chapter's headings, definitions and
pagination and not Grudem's own voice (see
[[grudem-systematic-theology-chapter-14-course-outline--202609050601]],
distilled at
[[grudems-chapter-14-outline-maps-each-trinitarian-error-to-a-denied-statement--202609050645]]).
Everything below about what "the chapter" contains is what that outline prints
about it. The 2020 chapter is established only by that edition's table of
contents, and nothing here rests on a second-edition page.

**In the sentence, the two rivals are not treated alike.** Against the reading
that makes the three produced things — vessels, instruments, a lesser or other
deity through which the one God works — the sentence puts a proposition of its
own: *each person is fully God*. It states the contradictory, and no
interpretive tradition is needed to see it. Against the reading that makes the
three aspects, names, or modes of a single person, the sentence puts one word:
*persons*. Its other clauses are sayable by someone holding that reading — a God
who "eternally exists" and of whom "there is one" is what such a reading also
asserts — so the whole weight of that exclusion rests on how much distinctness
the word *persons* is taken to carry, which the sentence does not itself say.

**What the outline prints further on.** It breaks the definition into three
statements — "God is three persons," "Each person is fully God," "There is one
God" — and heads its next section "Errors have come by denying any of the three
statements summarizing the biblical teaching," at pages 241–248. Under that
heading the denial is named in the entry itself for two of the three errors:
Arianism "denies the full deity of the Son and the Holy Spirit," tritheism
"denies that there is only one God." The modalism entry is descriptive rather
than paired: it "claims that there is one person who appears to us in three
different forms (or 'modes')," with Sabellianism and Modalistic Monarchianism
given as other names for it. The flat proposition against that reading comes
later still, in a section on the distinctions between the persons at pages
248–256, which states that the persons "are not just three different ways of
looking at the one being of God." So the single word is not left to carry the
exclusion alone through the chapter — on this evidence it carries it for some
twenty pages, until a section that is not about the errors at all says the thing
outright.

That is where the case parts company with the councils, though less cleanly than
it first looks. [[the-sefirot-comparison-survives-on-one-leg-and-loses-the-other--202609042012]]
found one hard boundary and one soft one in the conciliar record: the Arian
reading anathematized at Nicaea by proposition, the modalist reading not reached
there at all and condemned in 381 only as a party name in a canon (see
[[nicaea-325-anathematizes-the-arian-reading-and-says-nothing-about-the-modalist-one--202609042010]]).
Seventeen centuries later the asymmetry reappears in the summary sentence — that
much is established, because the sentence is the best-witnessed thing in this
whole file. Whether it is then dissolved is a claim about the chapter, and the
chapter has not been read; a lesson outline is evidence that the proposition is
in there somewhere, not evidence of how it is argued or qualified. What can be
said is that the asymmetry belongs to the one-sentence formula rather than to
the doctrine, and that a genre with room to keep going does not appear to be
stuck with it — which is the reverse of the creed's situation, where there was
no later section.

The middle clause does one more piece of work.
[[evangelical-theology-identifies-god-by-a-tri-personal-life--202608311940]]
reads the evangelical identification of God off a statement of faith that says
one God "eternally existent in three persons" and then devotes a separate
article to the Son's deity. Grudem's sentence does in one clause what that
statement does across two articles: the per-person predicate sits inside the
definition, as part of what the word *God* is being defined to mean, and it is a
predicate of full deity rather than of share or degree. That is what makes the
evangelical answer to the question in
[[internal-plurality-is-not-what-divides-the-monotheisms--202609011543]] — may
the one God be really articulated, and how? — an answer pitched at the essence
and not at instruments of it.
