The objection recorded at
[[a-counterfactual-derived-from-nature-and-circumstance-is-natural-or-free-knowledge-but-not-middle--202609111821]]
was accepted by both of its targets, and they drew different conclusions.
"Surprisingly, in the face of criticism (primarily by Paul Helm),41 both
Tiessen and Ware have come to agree with the above reasoning that CCFs are
part of God’s natural knowledge." Tiessen, in the conversation with Helm
(WTJ 71 [2009] 448): "Reflection on Professor Helm’s recent comments has
finally brought me to the conclusion that he is correct on this point. God’s
knowledge of counterfactuals is not different from his knowledge of
possibilities; it is therefore part of his necessary knowledge." He then
gave up the doctrine as well as the label (Van Horn's note 53, outside the
capture, gives the page as 450): "the sole rationale for
positing middle knowledge is to give room for libertarian creaturely freedom
as a fact of the world God chooses to actualize. I now believe that
rejection of the Molinist construction because of its faulty understanding
of freedom also entails rejection of the concept of divine middle
knowledge." Ware conceded the point and kept the word: Van Horn cites his
response in *Perspectives on the Doctrine of God* (2008), p. 74, and adds
that "Ware still wants to hang onto the term “middle knowledge” as a label
of a useful subset of natural knowledge, but this is clearly a misuse of the
term that will inevitably lead to confusion"
([[van-horn-records-the-concession-and-argues-that-counterfactuals-can-be-natural-or-free-but-not-middle--202609111817]],
from [[van-horn-on-incorporating-middle-knowledge-into-calvinism--202609111812]]).
Ten years after the exchange Helm still listed Ware, for his "Reformed
Molinism", as "an odd and an inexplicable exception" among the Reformed
([[helm-grounds-the-reformed-refusal-of-middle-knowledge-in-the-decree--202609111816]],
from [[helm-and-more-on-molinism--202609111811]]).

Van Horn's own position is that Tiessen drew the wrong lesson: "Rejection of
libertarianism does not require rejection of MK", and a Calvinist could hold
middle knowledge by weakening concurrence and the claim that freedom
requires determinism, or by giving counterfactuals with impossible
antecedents non-trivial truth values. But he states the price, and the price
is the point for this map. A Calvinist who takes either route "would need to
abandon the objection, presented above by Bavinck, that Molinists are
sacrificing the aseity or pure actuality of God.74 Moreover, the grounding
objection would also have to be abandoned." The label, that is, costs the
weapons: the two arguments by which the Reformed have refused Molina are
unavailable to a Reformed theologian who keeps Molina's category, and Ware,
as Van Horn's note 26 and Laing both report, is on record as using the
second of them.

There is a naming pattern here worth setting beside Grudem's. Grudem takes a
standard position and declines its standard name, on the ground that the
name concedes more than the position
([[grudem-takes-positions-whose-standard-names-he-declines--202609081828]]).
Ware takes a non-standard position and keeps a standard name, on the ground,
as Van Horn reports it, that the name is a useful label. Both moves are
about what a name is taken to concede, and in both the critics' complaint is
the same: the name and the doctrine have come apart.

**Limits.** The concessions are reported, not witnessed: the 2009
conversation and the 2008 volume are unread, and Ware's page 74 is known
only from Van Horn's note. Whether Ware has kept the label since 2012, and
what Tiessen has written since, are not on file. Van Horn's two routes are
summarised from uncaptured text and not assessed.
