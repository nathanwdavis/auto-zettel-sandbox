Meeting material again and pulling it out of your own head are not two routes
to the same place. Measured after a delay, only the second one holds, and the
difference is large enough to survive being handicapped four to one on
exposure.

The handicapping is what makes the claim worth making. In Roediger and
Karpicke's second experiment, students who read a passage four times got
through it 14.2 times in total; students who read it once and then tried three
times to recall it from memory, with no feedback and no chance to check, got
through it 3.4 times. A week later the second group recalled 61% of the
passage's idea units and the first group 40%
([[roediger-and-karpicke-measured-retrieval-against-re-exposure-and-let-time-decide--202609040310]],
p. 253). Five minutes after learning the ordering was the other way round, and
strictly by exposure — 83%, 78%, 71% (p. 252). The crossover is the finding: re-exposure
buys performance now, retrieval buys performance later, and if you measure
early enough you will conclude the opposite of the truth.

That the effect is not simply extra reading in disguise is not an inference of
this note; it is the point the study was built to establish, and the authors
state it: "restudying allowed students to reexperience 100% of the material but
produced poor long-term retention" (p. 253). The 2008 open-book study makes the
same comparison with the exposures counted rather than merely bounded, and the
testing conditions beat their exposure-matched study controls again — .66
against .54 at three exposures, .66 and .55 against .50 at two
([[agarwal-et-al-put-the-notes-on-the-desk-and-measured-what-happened-a-week-later--202609040320]],
Table 4, p. 868).

**What kind of thing this claim is, in a base that has just been burned once.**
The nearest comparable finding this repository holds — the longhand-over-laptop
advantage — did not survive an independent direct replication, and
[[a-failed-replication-leaves-the-mechanism-standing-and-the-effect-unproven--202609040210]]
records what that cost. So the replication status of *this* effect is the first
thing to ask, and the honest answer has a hole in it. The field's current survey
says the benefits of retrieval practice "have been replicated hundreds of times"
and names three meta-analyses (Rowland 2014, Adesope et al. 2017, Agarwal et al.
2021) that have audited the literature
([[karpickes-survey-vouches-for-the-effect-and-never-mentions-notes--202609040330]],
p. 434). But that survey is written by the 2006 paper's own second author, and
this base has read none of the three meta-analyses. So the position here is:
two primary papers read in full, four experiments between them, plus the field's
own attributed claim of robustness, plus a named and unclosed route to checking
it independently. That
is a better evidential position than the base holds for anything else about
note-taking, and it is not the same as verified.

**And the scope is narrower than the phrasing invites.** What was measured is
recall of prose passages by undergraduates over five minutes to a week. Nothing
here is about ideas held for years, about connections between them, or about
notes — see
[[having-the-notes-to-hand-buys-present-performance-not-durable-knowledge--202609040345]]
for how far this literature does and does not reach toward this repository's
own practices, which is: barely.
