Put a person's notes on the desk in front of them and they answer questions
about the material much better. Take the notes away a week later and they are
no better off than someone who never had them — the whole of the advantage was
in the moment of consultation, and none of it was retained.

This is the one measurement this repository has found that manipulates access
to written material and then waits. Agarwal and colleagues defined their
open-book condition as one in which students "view notes and textbooks while
taking the test"
([[agarwal-et-al-put-the-notes-on-the-desk-and-measured-what-happened-a-week-later--202609040320]],
p. 861). In their second experiment, with the material available, initial
performance ran .82 against .66 for students working from memory. On the
closed-book final test a week later, the open-book condition finished at .66 and
the retrieve-then-check condition also at .66 — identical destinations from a
sixteen-point head start, and the same pattern held in Experiment 1. Measured
as loss rather than level, the group with the notes fell from .81 to .66 while
the group that had retrieved and then checked went .65 to .66 and forgot
essentially nothing. The authors' account is that having the answer in front of
you raises "the momentary accessibility of some of the information" while
producing "small increments in storage strength" (p. 872). Looking up what you
could have recalled is a transaction that costs nothing and builds nothing.

**Why this is typed `contradicts` against
[[the-note-became-an-external-memory-only-when-memorizing-came-to-be-seen-as-a-cost--202609032115]],
and how narrowly.** That note records Chavigny's 1920 inversion — notes as a
"personal memory on paper", on the ground that "too much memorizing can be
harmful to the higher intellectual qualities" — and Blair's judgement that this
was one of the most significant shifts in the history of note-taking. The
shift's premise is that memory can be handed to paper and the head freed at no
cost to what it knows. That premise is what this measurement contradicts, and
only that: what the paper takes over, it does not consolidate, and a week later
it is as if the transfer never happened. What is *not* contradicted is
Chavigny's recommendation, or his claim about higher intellectual qualities,
which nobody measured then and nobody measured here. Drexel's generation, who
thought a note taker owed memory something, turn out to have been defending a
mechanism that exists — and this study says nothing about whether they were
right about what the freed capacity is worth. The three-century argument is
still not settled; it now has one measured stake in the ground.

**The three cautions that keep this note honest.** First, in the same study
every testing condition beat every exposure-matched study condition on the
delayed test, so nothing here says consulting notes is worse than not
retrieving at all — the comparison that fails is open book against closed book.
Second, the authors themselves note that anticipating an open book changes how
people prepare, citing classroom work reporting students "prepare less
effectively when they will be allowed to use their notes or textbooks during
the test" (p. 872); this base has not read that work, and if it holds the
cost is larger than measured here rather than smaller. Third, and most
limiting: this is a week, a thousand-word passage and a short-answer test. The
claims this repository makes about its own practice are about accumulation and
linking across years
([[the-note-taking-experiments-do-not-reach-the-slip-box-claims--202609040215]]).
A slip-box is not a textbook left open during an exam, and a note written to be
found in five years is not an answer looked up in five seconds.

That last gap is now measured rather than assumed. The field's own 2025 survey
of retrieval-based learning runs 31 pages and never once uses the words
note-taking, notes, external memory or offloading — its single point of contact
with this subject is the study above
([[karpickes-survey-vouches-for-the-effect-and-never-mentions-notes--202609040330]]).
The literature that knows most about what makes knowledge durable has, as of
2025, not turned its attention to the practice this repository is built on. And
in its classroom branch it cannot: consulting notes during retrieval practice
is an explicit exclusion criterion there
([[the-classroom-retrieval-literature-excludes-notes-by-its-own-inclusion-criteria--202609040420]]),
which is what makes the 2008 open-book study, where the notes were on the desk
by design, the exception rather than the first of a series.
