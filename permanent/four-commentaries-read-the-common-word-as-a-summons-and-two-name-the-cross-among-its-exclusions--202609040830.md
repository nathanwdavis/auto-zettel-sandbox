Q 3:64's "come to a word that is *sawaʾ* between us and you" is read by all four
classical commentaries this base has captured as a proposal put to the other
party, whose content is the exclusion of every rival object of worship and whose
alternative is a declaration that the Muslim side stands where it stood. (One
modern commentary complicates the vocabulary; that is recorded at the end rather
than folded into this sentence.) That reading was
drawn from al-Tabari alone
([[the-common-word-is-a-summons-in-its-classical-reading-not-ground-already-shared--202609040720]]).
It now rests on four commentators, and unlike the co-reference gloss, it
travelled.

**The word.** al-Qurtubi glosses `سواء` as `العدل والنصفة` — justice and equity
— and reports a variant reading, attributed in his text simply to `عبد الله`,
`إلى كلمة عدل بيننا وبينكم`, "to a word of justice between us and you"; al-Baghawi as `عدل بيننا وبينكم مستوية`; Ibn
Kathir as `عدل ونصف ، نستوي نحن وأنتم فيها`, justice and fairness in which we
and you stand equal. These are the same gloss al-Tabari gives (`كلمة عدل`), and
the equality asserted is an equality in the fairness of the *terms*, not a
report that the two parties already occupy common ground.

**The content.** Every commentator fills the word with the verse's own "that we
worship none but God and associate nothing with Him", and two of them enumerate
what that excludes. Ibn Kathir: `لا وثنا ، ولا صنما ، ولا صليبا ولا طاغوتا ،
ولا نارا ، ولا شيئا` — no idol, no image, no cross, no *taghut*, no fire,
nothing. al-Muyassar, in the same enumerating style, `من وثن أو صنم أو صليب أو
طاغوت` — image, idol, cross, *taghut*. The cross is named in a list of things
whose worship the proposed common word rules out, in commentaries on the verse
that modern interfaith usage takes as the charter of common ground.

**The exit.** al-Qurtubi glosses "bear witness that we are Muslims" as
professing Islam and submitting to its rulings, `غير متخذين أحدا ربا لا عيسى
ولا عزيرا ولا الملائكة` — taking none as lord, not Jesus, not Ezra, not the
angels; al-Baghawi as `مخلصون بالتوحيد`, sincere in the affirmation of oneness;
Ibn Kathir as having them witness `على استمراركم على الإسلام الذي شرعه الله لكم`
— that you continue in the Islam God legislated for you. What follows refusal is
a restatement of the Muslim position, not a residue of agreement.

**Two independent confirmations of the genre.** al-Qurtubi, al-Baghawi and Ibn
Kathir all quote the Prophet's letter to Heraclius, in which this verse is
embedded in `أسلم تسلم` — submit and you will be safe. And al-Baghawi supplies
an occasion of revelation in which the Najran Christians and the Medinan Jews
each tell Muhammad that he wants only to be taken as a lord himself; the verse
answers a refusal.

**The one complication, recorded rather than smoothed.** al-Sa'di, the modern
commentator among the five captured this cycle, does call the common word
`مشتركة بيننا وبينكم` — shared between us and you — which is the vocabulary of
already-held common ground
([[saadi-turns-the-shared-god-clause-into-a-premise-for-disputation--202609040816]]).
Read in place, he means the word is one all the prophets agreed on and not the
property of either party, and he immediately fills it with the exclusion of
prophet, angel, saint, image and idol and ends with "if they turn away they are
obstinate". So it qualifies the claim without overturning it: the language of
sharing does appear, in a modern commentary, attached to a word whose content is
still a demand. Where the older commentaries use `عدل` and `نصفة` — fair terms —
the modern one reaches for `مشتركة` — shared property. That drift is small,
attested here in one author, and worth watching rather than building on.

## Amendment, 2026-09-04 09:00Z cycle: a fifth commentary, and it sharpens the reading

al-Razi (d. 606/1210) reads Q 3:64 the same way and gives the summons its
clearest statement. "Come to a word `سواء` between us and you" he renders
`هَلُمُّوا إلى كَلِمَةٍ فِيها إنْصافٌ مِن بَعْضِنا لِبَعْضٍ، ولا مَيْلَ فِيهِ
لِأحَدٍ عَلى صاحِبِهِ` — come to a word in which there is fairness from each of
us to the other, with no leaning of one over his fellow — and glosses
`السَّواءُ هو العَدْلُ والإنْصافُ`, equality here being justice and fairness,
which he derives from *insaf* as the giving of a half
([[razi-argues-the-verse-from-what-the-people-of-the-book-already-affirm--202609040910]]).
He then quotes al-Zajjaj that `سواء` describes the word itself, so that `فَإذا
آمَنّا بِها نَحْنُ وأنْتُمْ كُنّا عَلى السَّواءِ والِاسْتِقامَةِ` — if we and
you believe in it, we shall be on level and upright ground. That conditional is
the point: the equality is the *result* of accepting the word, not a state the
two parties are already in. It is the strongest evidence yet against reading
`سواء` as "already shared", and it comes from the commentator most likely to
have noticed if the other reading were available.

The count in this note's title and first paragraph is therefore five
commentaries, not four. Two further things al-Razi adds, neither of which the
other four supply: the setting is the Najran delegation after the *mubahala*
was declined, so the verse is what is said when the harder procedure has
failed; and the address "O People of the Book" is itself part of the fairness,
being among the finest of titles and used, he says, when a man turns from
wrangling to seeking fair terms. The exclusions he attaches are the verse's own
three, expounded against Christian doctrine directly
([[the-classical-exclusion-of-trinitarians-is-made-inside-the-verse-that-carries-the-shared-god-clause--202609040925]]).


## Amendment, 2026-09-04 10:00Z cycle

A fifth commentary reads it the same way, and it is the one that had the best
chance of reading it otherwise. al-Zamakhshari is the only commentator in this
base who says the word is undisputed across the Qur'an, the Torah and the Gospel
— and he says it in the sentence before he glosses the invitation as
`تعالوا إليها حتى لا نقول: عزيز ابن الله، ولا المسيح ابن الله`, come to it so
that we may not say "'Uzayr is the son of God" nor "the Messiah is the son of
God". So the count in this note's title is now five commentaries reading the
verse as a summons, and three naming the sonship or the cross among what the
summons excludes. The reason his agreement is worth more than a fifth tally
mark is set out in
[[the-strongest-classical-claim-of-common-ground-is-made-about-the-word-not-about-the-parties--202609041015]]:
he locates the common ground in the content of the word, not in the parties, and
that is exactly the distinction the summons reading turns on.
