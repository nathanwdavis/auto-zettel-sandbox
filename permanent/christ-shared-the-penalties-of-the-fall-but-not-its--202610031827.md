Aquinas gives the scholastic name for what is now called a sin nature: the
fomes of sin, "an inclination of the sensual appetite to what is contrary to
reason". It "was nowise in Him". The objection he answers had argued that the
fomes and "the passibility and mortality of the body spring from the same
principle", the loss of original justice, so Christ should have both. Aquinas
keeps the passibility and mortality and denies the fomes
([[aquinas-denies-christ-both-original-sin-and-the-fomes-of--202610031818]]).

Scripture draws the same line. Christ is "touched with the feeling of our
infirmities" yet "without sin" (Heb 4:15), and the narrative shows the
weakness: "being wearied with his journey, sat thus on the well" (John 4:6)
([[the-new-testament-passages-call-christ-holy-from-birth-and--202610031824]]).

Irving, who held that Christ took fallen flesh, draws it from the other side.
Christ's flesh was open to temptation, yet he was holy, "seeing wickedness
consisteth not in being tempted, but in yielding to the temptation"
([[irving-holds-that-christ-took-fallen-flesh-and-was-kept--202610031823]]).

So the line runs between infirmity and fault. Hunger, weariness, grief,
mortality and real temptation are on Christ's side of it; inherited guilt and
an inborn inclination to evil are not.
