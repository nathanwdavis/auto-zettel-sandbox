Pico's theses claim that Kabbalah's own principles compel the Catholic Trinity
(see
[[christian-kabbalism-claimed-kabbalah-compels-the-trinity--202609011811]]).
The obvious Jewish reply is to deny the resemblance. The first sustained Jewish
critique to engage the question did the opposite: it granted the resemblance
and turned it against Kabbalah.

Leon Modena's *Ari Nohem* (Venice, 1639) is that critique, written into a
setting Dweck declines to describe as scholarly exchange — Christians "drew on
Kabbalah when preaching to potential Jewish converts", and the appropriation
begun by Pico "was hardly a disinterested intellectual exchange", being
"fraught with conversionary pressures and uneven power relations" (see
[[dweck-the-scandal-of-kabbalah--202609030150]], p. 9). Against kabbalists who
"branded as heretics anyone who denied" the centrality of the sefirot, "Modena
repudiated this claim and leveled a severe countercharge of his own: after an
examination of the sefirot as a concept, he concluded that it pointed to a
plurality within God similar to the Christian doctrine of the Trinity" (same
source, p. 11; see
[[dweck-reports-modena-conceding-the-trinity-parallel-as-an-attack--202609030152]]).

The logic is only available to someone attacking Kabbalah rather than defending
it. A kabbalist answering Pico must show the parallel is superficial. Modena,
who wants the sefirot demoted anyway, can afford to say the parallel is real —
and then let it do his work, since a doctrine that lands a Jew in something
resembling the Trinity is thereby convicted. The same premise serves the
missionary and the anti-kabbalist, because they draw opposite conclusions from
it.

Two consequences for this repository. First, the one-sidedness that note
202609011811 recorded is now partly closed, but not in the direction it
anticipated: what the base can show is a Jewish *concession* of the structural
parallel, not a Jewish refutation of it. Whether kabbalists themselves conceded
or denied it is still uncaptured, and Modena is a hostile witness to the
doctrine he is describing. Second, the base's own claim that Kabbalah answers
yes to the internal-distinction question (see
[[internal-plurality-is-not-what-divides-the-monotheisms--202609011543]], which
is tagged contested for exactly this reason) turns out to share its premise
with a seventeenth-century Jewish critic who thought that answer was Kabbalah's
disgrace. That is corroboration of the reading, and a caution about it: the
premise was contested inside Judaism from early on, by someone who took it to
be true.
