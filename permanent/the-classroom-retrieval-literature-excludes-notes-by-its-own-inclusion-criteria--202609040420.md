That a literature is silent about your question can be an accident of what
people happened to study. It can also be written into the definition of the
field, in which case the silence will never end on its own. For retrieval
practice in classrooms it is the second.

The screening rule is one of three that define "classroom research" for the
most comprehensive review of that literature: "Closed-book, not open-book: All
retrieval practice took place without the use of notes, external learning aids,
or the internet"
([[agarwal-nunes-and-blunt-screened-two-thousand-abstracts-and-counted-what-they-found--202609040405]],
preprint p. 10), restated in the Methods as a condition of inclusion — "the
retrieval practice intervention must take place without the use of notes,
external learning aids, or the internet (e.g., quizzes in class must be
closed-book, not open-book)" (p. 17). A study in which students consulted
their notes while practising retrieval was not merely absent from the 50; it
was screened out of the nearly 2,000 by design.

This upgrades a claim this base had been making from absence.
[[the-note-taking-experiments-do-not-reach-the-slip-box-claims--202609040215]]
argued that the measured note-taking literature and the slip-box claims are
about different constructs, and
[[karpickes-survey-vouches-for-the-effect-and-never-mentions-notes--202609040330]]
observed that a 31-page survey of retrieval-based learning never uses the word
note-taking. Both were inferences from what was not said. This is the rule that
produces the silence, in the authors' own words: the intervention is defined as
retrieval *without* the external record. So the field cannot supply evidence
about working with a note collection, however many more studies it accumulates,
because a study that did so would fail its inclusion criteria.

One row in the review's appendix shows what the boundary looks like from the
inside. Dobson and Linderholm 2015(b) kept note-taking as a *comparison*
condition — a "Read-read while taking notes" arm, which the review records as
yielding a "Numerical benefit from retrieval practice" over it (p. 56): a
direction with no effect size, one of the sixteen comparisons too thinly
reported to quantify. Notes are permitted in this literature as the thing
retrieval beats, never as part of the thing being done.

**What follows for this repository, and what does not.** It does not follow
that a slip-box is unsupported by evidence; it follows that this particular
body of evidence was constructed so as not to bear on it either way, and that
waiting for it to is waiting for nothing. The measurement that would bear on
it has to come from somewhere that lets the record stay on the desk — which is
what makes the open-book experiment behind
[[the-note-taking-experiments-do-not-reach-the-slip-box-claims--202609040215]]'s
sibling note unusual rather than typical. If this base wants the question
answered, the honest conclusion is that it must look outside the retrieval
literature, not deeper into it.
