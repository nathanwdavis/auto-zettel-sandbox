One verse stands at the same structural point in two systems that disagree
about everything around it. Grudem's chapter 12 proves that God knows "all
things possible" from three texts in which God says what would have
happened — David at Keilah, Elisha's arrows, and Jesus on Tyre and Sidon:
"For if the mighty works done in you had been done in Tyre and Sidon, they
would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes"
([[grudem-defines-omniscience-as-one-simple-eternal-act-and-rejects-open-theism--202609061915]],
from [[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]]). Molina's disputation 53 rests the case for middle knowledge on
the same verse: on the hypothesis of the same miracles Christ asserted the
Tyrians' and Sidonians' repentance "futuram fuisse," while that of Chorazin
and Bethsaida "solum permanserit intra limites rei possibilis"; therefore
"sane concedendum est medium quod intendimus inter futurum simpliciter et
mere possibile" — the middle between the simply future and the merely
possible must be conceded
([[molina-distinguishes-three-knowledges-and-makes-the-middle-one-depend-on-the-creature--202609090055]],
from [[molina-concordia-liberi-arbitrii--202609090050]]). Pohle, three centuries on, still gives it as the example:
"Christ, for example, was able to declare with certainty to His obstinate
hearers that the inhabitants of Tyre and Sidon would have done penance"
([[pohle-locates-the-molinist-thomist-dispute-in-the-medium-of-foreknowledge--202609090057]],
from [[pohle-molinism-catholic-encyclopedia--202609090052]]).

The directions are opposite. For Grudem the verse shows that God knows
possibilities, and knowledge of possibilities is then, through Frame, folded
into what God knows by knowing his own power: such knowledge "is already part
of God’s necessary knowledge, or what Craig calls natural knowledge"
([[a-situation-is-made-of-other-peoples-choices--202609081830]]). Tyre and
Sidon become a case of natural knowledge, and middle knowledge is redundant.
For Molina the verse shows precisely that Tyre and Sidon are *not* a case of
mere possibility: what Christ asserts is not that their repentance was
possible but that it "futuram fuisse," would have been — a conditional
future, which natural knowledge of what can be cannot deliver and free
knowledge of what will be does not contain. Reading it as possibility is, for
him, the move of opponents who "Renuunt namque concedere medium inter futurum
simpliciter et possibile merum, idque ut mediam scientiam effugiant," and
referring the certainty to an efficacious help God would have given is to
"enervare atque pervertere verba illa Christi Matthaei 11."

So the verse is not neutral ground but the place where the two mechanisms
part, and the parting is exact: Grudem needs "would have repented" to mean
that God knew the possibility and his own power, Molina needs it to mean that
God knew the outcome of a freedom he did not determine. This sharpens the
earlier finding that chapter 12's scriptural cases are about what creatures
would do while its deduction from self-knowledge is about what God could do,
with the bridge supplied only by Frame's identification
([[grudem-grounds-knowledge-of-the-possible-in-self-knowledge-without-separating-what-creatures-would-do--202609061923]]).
Molina is the author of the position that identification was made against,
and this is the verse on which he made it.

**Limits.** Grudem's chapter 12 does not mention Molina, and Molina's
opponents in disputation 53 are Thomists, not Calvinists; the confrontation
is staged here. Molina's *futurum ex hypothesi* and Grudem's "possible" are
not defined against each other by either author. The ESV wording is Grudem's
quotation; the Vulgate text Molina paraphrases is not on file, only his
paraphrase of it.
