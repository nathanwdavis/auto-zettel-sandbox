Grudem's definition makes God know "all things actual and possible", and the
sub-section on the possible gives that clause two grounds that do not reach the
same things ([[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]], distilled at
[[grudem-defines-omniscience-as-one-simple-eternal-act-and-rejects-open-theism--202609061915]]).

The first ground is scriptural, and every case is about what *creatures* would
do. God tells David that Saul "will come down" to Keilah and that the men of
Keilah "will surrender you"; David leaves, and neither happens (1 Sam.
23:11–13). Jesus says Tyre and Sidon "would have repented" had his works been
done there, and that Sodom "would have remained until this day" (Matt. 11:21,
23); Elisha says what would have followed had Joash struck the ground more
times (2 Kings 13:19). These are statements about how people and cities would
have responded under conditions that did not obtain.

The second ground is a deduction from the first clause of the definition, and
it reaches what *God* could do: "If God fully knows himself, he knows
everything he is able to do, which includes all things that are possible." The
illustration is of the same kind — the thousands of kinds of animals God "could
have created but did not", of each of which he knows "what each of those other
possible creations would have been like and what would have happened in each of
them".

The two are offered as grounds of one thing, the second introduced with "can
also be deduced", and the section does not remark that they differ. But they
do. Knowing everything one is able to do yields knowledge of what one would do
and of what one's creations would be like; it yields knowledge of what a free
creature would choose only if that choice is among the things God is "able to
do", and the section does not say that it is. Read strictly, the deduction
covers the animals and the scriptural cases cover Saul, and the bridge between
them — that what a creature would freely do is settled by, or included in, what
God can do — is exactly the question the open-theism sub-section then opens
without noticing that it was already at issue here. On Grudem's own concession
at the end of the section, that foreknown choices are "not absolutely free",
the bridge holds; but it is supplied there, not here.

**Sources and their limits.** The section is on file in the second edition's
own wording from the owner's export, and every quotation above was checked
against the full text. The distinction drawn is this base's, made on the
section's own examples; Grudem does not draw it, and the note claims only that
the section does not, not that the book nowhere does. The classical vocabulary
for the distinction (knowledge of possibles by knowledge of one's power, as
against knowledge of what free creatures would do) is deliberately not
invoked, because no source stating it is on file in this repository. The link
to [[grudems-simple-act-of-knowing-denies-process-not-composition--202609061920]]
is for the shared premise, God's complete self-knowledge, from which both that
note's "simple act" and this note's deduction start.
