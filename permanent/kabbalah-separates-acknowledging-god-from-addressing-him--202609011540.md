Ramban's most useful contribution to the same-God question is that he refuses
to let it have one answer. On his account a nation can hold a true belief
about who the highest God is and still direct its worship somewhere else --
and it is the second fact, not the first, that makes the case one of idolatry.

Two limits on that claim before it is stated. Ramban is one thirteenth-century
commentator, not "the kabbalistic tradition"; the Leviticus comment is
explicitly his *sod* ("the secret of the matter"), but the Exodus taxonomy this
note leans on hardest is exoteric history of religion, argued alongside
Maimonides. And nothing here is a tolerance: the arrangement is filed under
"three kinds of idol-worship", and on Leviticus 18:25 the nations "go astray
after their princes to worship them".

The claim is explicit rather than inferred. Cataloguing the first and oldest
kind of idol-worship, Ramban says the nations worshipped their appointed
angelic princes "even though they admitted that supreme strength and infinite
power belonged only to G-d the Most High," and cites the rabbis for the
observation that the peoples of the world call the Most High "G-d of gods" (see
[[ramban-lets-the-nations-acknowledge-the-god-they-do-not-address--202609011535]],
on Exodus 20:3). Cosmologically the arrangement is God's own doing: the nations
were allotted to constellations and to angelic lords, Israel alone kept as the
portion over which God "has placed none of the angels as chief, observer, or
ruler" (on Leviticus 18:25). The prohibition, though, does not track the split cleanly, and the note is
worth less if that is hidden. Ramban glosses *elohim acheirim* as "others
besides the Glorious Name" and calls the verse "a prohibition against
believing in any of these beings, accepting them as gods, or saying to them,
'thou art my god'." *Believing in* heads the list, so the offence is not
address alone. What the list does not include is a false cosmology: the
nations' picture of who is highest is, by Ramban's own account, correct.
Belief that a prince is *a god* is condemned; belief that the Most High is
God is granted. And the verse being expounded is the Second Commandment,
addressed to Israel -- Ramban's diagnosis of the nations comes from the
history he attaches to it, not from the commandment's own scope.

This cuts across the reference/description grid the same-God literature works
with (see
[[the-same-god-question-turns-on-reference-versus-description--202608311942]]).
Reference asks which being the traditions pick out; description asks whether
they give the same account of him. Ramban's nations pass both tests fairly
well: they pick out the Most High and describe him as supreme. What they fail
is a third thing the grid has no column for -- where the worship terminates.
Two parties can refer alike, describe alike, and still not be worshipping the
same God, because worship is not exhausted by beliefs about a being; it is
also a movement toward one, and on this account it can be intercepted.

The practical consequence is that Ramban's verdict on any given religion is
not readable off its creed. Nothing a nation professes about the Most High
settles, on his account, whether its liturgy reaches Him.
