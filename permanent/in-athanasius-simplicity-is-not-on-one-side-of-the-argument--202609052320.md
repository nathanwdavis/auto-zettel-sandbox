Divine simplicity is easy to picture as a doctrine with a side: one school uses
the denial of composition to rule internal distinction *out*, another invokes it
to argue distinction *in*. Athanasius is a case where that picture will not hold,
because in his hands the same denial does several different jobs, and they do not
all point the same way.

**It constrains how generation may be conceived.** Against the charge that a
begotten Son must have been begotten the way sons are, Athanasius rules out the
human model by ruling out parts: God "is not composed of parts, but being
impassible and simple, He is impassibly and indivisibly Father of the Son"
(*Contra Arianos* I.28; see
[[athanasius-discourse-i-uses-simplicity-to-explain-impassible-generation--202609052101]]).
Simplicity is not delivering the conclusion that there is a Son. It is fixing the
manner in which whatever generation there is has to be understood — without
partition, effluence, or succession. The opening of *De Decretis* 11 makes the
same move from the other end: human bodies are "in a state of flux and composed
of parts," so human offspring are portions of their fathers, whereas God, "being
without parts, is Father of the Son without partition or passion" (see
[[athanasius-de-decretis-contrasts-human-composite-generation-with-divine-uncompounded-generation--202609052102]]).

**It also works as a premise.** Immediately after, the same section turns the
constraint into a ground: "being uncompounded in nature, He is Father of One Only
Son. This is why He is Only-begotten" — the Son's uniqueness read off the absence
of composition. The clearest instance is *De Decretis* 22: "But if God be simple,
as He is, it follows that in saying `God' and naming `Father,' we name nothing as
if about Him, but signify his essence itself," and therefore "let no one be
startled on hearing that the Son of God is from the Essence of the Father" (see
[[athanasius-de-decretis-22-argues-from-simplicity-to-the-son-from-the-essence--202609052210]]).
Because there is nothing in God that is merely *about* God, naming the Father
names the essence, and what is of the Father is of that essence. Simplicity is
not bounding a conclusion here; it is producing one, and the conclusion is an
internal relation.

**And once the inference runs backwards.** In the human-word analogy of
*De Decretis* 11, Athanasius argues from the fact that not even a human word is
begotten with passion or partition to "the impassible and impartitive nature of
the Father" (see
[[athanasius-de-decretis-contrasts-human-composite-generation-with-divine-uncompounded-generation--202609052102]]).
There simplicity is the output rather than the input.

The upshot is negative and worth having. Where a doctrine stands in an argument
is not a property of the doctrine; it is a property of the passage. So no single
author's usage can settle whether simplicity "belongs" to the exclusion of
internal distinction or to its establishment — inside three sections of one
father it occupies both positions and a third.

Two boundaries. This does not vindicate the report that Athanasius used
simplicity *consistently* to argue that the Son's eternal generation proved his
absolute deity (see
[[the-survey-puts-the-doctrine-in-four-traditions-and-reports-simplicity-used-for-the-trinity--202609042077]]):
simplicity appears once in the fourteen sections read of *Contra Arianos* I, and
*De Decretis* 22 concludes to the Son's being from the Father's *essence*, with
the word "eternal" absent from the section (see
[[athanasius-de-decretis-22-argues-from-simplicity-to-the-son-from-the-essence--202609052210]]).
Nor does it show that report false in kind: the premise-form argument is
genuinely there. And all of it rests on Robertson's 1900 English from a single
host, with no Greek consulted anywhere and no second transcription of the quoted
passages, so a claim about an argument's logical form is here a claim about the
form of a translation ([[athanasius-de-decretis--202609052015]],
[[athanasius-orationes-contra-arianos-i--202609052014]]). Sections read:
*Contra Arianos* I.19–32 of 64, *De Decretis* 8–32 of 32.
