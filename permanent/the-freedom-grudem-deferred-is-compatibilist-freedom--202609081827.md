Chapter 12's section on God's knowledge ended by conceding that "there must be
some sense in which our choices are not absolutely free," adopting Ware's
"freedom of inclination" as the residue, and deferring the rest to the chapter
on providence
([[grudem-defines-omniscience-as-one-simple-eternal-act-and-rejects-open-theism--202609061915]]).
Until now this base had the deferral and not the discharge, and
[[grudem-and-pinnock-accept-the-same-entailment-and-give-up-opposite-sides-of-it--202609061922]]
said as much on its face. Chapter 16 discharges it, and the answer is more
explicit than the deferring passage led one to expect.

Three sentences do it. Grudem states what Scripture denies: it "nowhere says
that we are 'free' in the sense of being outside of God's control or of being
able to make decisions that are not caused by anything." He states what it
affirms: "we are nonetheless free in the greatest sense that any creature of God
could be free—we make willing choices, choices that have real effects." And
having set Ware's *libertarian free will* ("the ability, all things being just
what they are, to choose differently") against Ware's *freedom of inclination*,
he says of the second that "it does not rule out the idea that our choices may
ultimately be determined by God in ways invisible to us"
([[grudem-defines-providence-as-preservation-concurrence-and-government--202609081824]],
from [[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]]). A footnote then supplies the
name the body withholds: this "freedom of inclination" is "also called
'compatibilist freedom.'"

That settles a reading the base had reached by inference. Note 202609061922
argued that Grudem keeps exhaustive foreknowledge and reads freedom down, on the
strength of one concession in chapter 12; chapter 16 says it as a positive
doctrine, with the determination made explicit ("determined by God") and the
technical name attached. The base's inference is confirmed from the primary
text, which is the outcome the standing request for this chapter was filed to
get.

One further sentence is worth keeping because it changes the *kind* of claim
being made. Grudem argues that the freedom his opponents want is not merely
unscriptural but incoherent given preservation: since Christ is "continually
carrying along all things," to be outside providential control "would simply be
not to exist!" That is not an appeal to a text about freedom; it is an argument
that libertarian independence is unavailable to a creature whose existence is
moment-by-moment upheld. The doctrine of preservation, in other words, is doing
work in the doctrine of freedom.

**Limits.** "Compatibilist freedom" appears in a footnote glossing Ware's term,
not as Grudem's self-description, and he elsewhere declines the label for
himself — a refusal that is its own subject at
[[grudem-takes-positions-whose-standard-names-he-declines--202609081828]], and
that should stop anyone citing this note as "Grudem calls himself a
compatibilist." Ware's two definitions are quoted from *God's Greater Glory*,
which this repository has not read; Ware is now cited at second hand in three
separate places here. And chapter 16 no more explains how a determined choice is
a willing one than chapter 12 did: Grudem lists that as one of the two questions
his own position cannot answer.
