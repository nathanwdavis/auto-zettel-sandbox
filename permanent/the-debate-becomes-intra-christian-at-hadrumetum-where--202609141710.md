A debate needs two parties inside the same community. Justin's opponent is
pagan fate and Irenaeus' is the Valentinian doctrine of natures; even Origen,
who assembles the sovereignty texts as objections, supplies no advocate for the
side those texts would support
([[the-earliest-preserved-christian-writings-on-free-will--202609141706]]).
*De gratia et libero arbitrio*, written about 426, is the earliest work on file
in which both positions are held by Christians.

The addressees settle it. Augustine writes to "my brother Valentinus" and the
monks of Hadrumetum serving God with him, because "there are dissensions among
you on this subject" — a quarrel inside a monastery, not a reply to an outside
school. And the two errors he names are not one heresy and its orthodox answer
but a matched pair. The first is the Pelagian: those "who so preach and defend
man's free will" as "as boldly to deny, and endeavour to do away with, the
grace of God". The second is the one that occasioned the book: "there are some
persons who so defend God's grace as to deny man's free will", "or who suppose
that free will is denied when grace is defended"
([[augustine-writes-to-monks-who-took-his-case-for-grace-to-be--202609141705]]).

The second group is the historically decisive one, because they are his own
readers. They had taken his anti-Pelagian writings to entail that the will is
not free, and Augustine's task is to deny the entailment while keeping the
doctrine that provoked it. That is the first appearance of the problem in its
enduring form: not whether grace or freedom is real, but whether asserting one
in full strength leaves the other anything to be.

The answer he gives is the one the later confessions institutionalise. He
concedes both bodies of Scripture, arguing free will from the form of God's
commands and then warning that these same testimonies "in the maintenance of
free will" must not "be understood in such a way as to leave no room for God's
assistance and grace". Twelve centuries later the Westminster divines put the
same refusal to choose into a single sentence, ordaining whatsoever comes to
pass in a clause that also establishes rather than removes the liberty of
second causes
([[the-westminster-confession-asserts-sovereign-ordination-and-creaturely-liberty-as-a-single-proposition--202609120650]]).

**Limits.** Earliest *on file here*, not earliest simply: the Pelagian
controversy opens around 411, and Augustine's *Ad Simplicianum* (396) is
usually given as where his own position turned, while the semi-Pelagian phase
runs past his death to the Second Council of Orange in 529. None of those texts
is captured in this repository, so the claim that Hadrumetum is the first
intra-Christian instance is a claim about the sources read, and the safer claim
is that by 426 the dispute is demonstrably internal. Pelagius' own writings
survive only partly and are not on file at all, so both parties are here
described from Augustine's report of them.
