What makes a note collection produce combinations nobody stored is not the
notes but a particular kind of link: the shortcut that jumps from one region
of the web to a distant one, "completely different (both in terms of subject
and location)"
([[schmidt-shows-how-luhmanns-file-fabricated-serendipity--202609010303]]).
Schmidt's network-theoretic reading of Luhmann's actual card index locates the
"fabrication of serendipity" in these cross-region references — an estimated
50,000 across the two collections, one on nearly every card of the first and
every second card of the second — together with hub cards whose
collected references open access to large tracts of the file
([[schmidt-niklas-luhmanns-card-index--202609010302]]).

This gives the critical-mass threshold a mechanism
([[a-slip-box-surprises-only-after-critical-mass--202609010113]]): what
accumulates over the container years is not card count but shortcut density,
and it corroborates from the archive side Luhmann's own claim that a note
"receives its quality only from the network of links and back-links"
([[luhmann-frames-the-slip-box-as-a-communication-partner--202609010112]]).
The contrapositive is empirical, not hypothetical: Schmidt found regions of
the real index that were poorly linked and apparently untouched since their
compilation — "black holes" that fade away. Two practical corollaries follow.
Placement and classification can be almost arbitrary so long as links are
added, so effort spent on taxonomy is better spent on linking; and linking is
upkeep, not an afterthought — Luhmann kept retrofitting references onto old
cards as new ones arrived. Atomic, individually addressable notes are what
give such links sharp endpoints to bind to
([[one-idea-per-note-enables-reuse--202701010002]]) — though the evidence
here remains one practitioner's file, described by one archivist.
