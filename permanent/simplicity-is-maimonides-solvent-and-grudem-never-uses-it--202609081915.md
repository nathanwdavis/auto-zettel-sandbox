This base has tracked divine simplicity across three traditions as a constraint
on what may be said about God's *being*
([[divine-simplicity-is-the-question-the-monotheisms-actually-divide-on--202609042081]]).
Hilchot Teshuvah 5:5 shows it doing something else entirely: load-bearing work in
the doctrine of *providence*, as the premise that disarms the argument from
foreknowledge to necessity.

Maimonides' move is compact. The objection assumes God knows the way we know —
a knower and a known thing standing apart, so that a fixed item of knowledge
fixes its object. He denies the assumption at the root: God "does not know with
a knowledge that is external from Him as do men, whose knowledge and selves are
two [different entities]. Rather, He, may His name be praised, and His knowledge
are one." That is the identity of God with his attributes, the doctrine this base
already holds from Yesodei HaTorah and the *Guide*, applied here to knowledge in
particular. If God's knowing is not a distinct act bearing on an object but is
God himself, then the inference from "known" to "necessary" — which trades on
knowledge being the sort of thing that could match or fail to match its object —
never gets started. The cost is stated at once: we cannot comprehend it, any
more than we can comprehend God's essence
([[maimonides-makes-free-will-a-pillar-of-the-torah-and-refers-the-paradox-upward--202609081912]]).

Grudem holds a version of the same doctrine and does not use it here. The base
already recorded that his "one simple and eternal act" of knowing denies process
in God — counting, recalling, inferring, learning — rather than composition
([[grudems-simple-act-of-knowing-denies-process-not-composition--202609061920]]),
and that partlessness recurs across his attribute definitions. He therefore has
the materials for something like Maimonides' answer. What he does instead, at
the point where the objection arises, is concede the entailment: if God knows
our actions in advance "there must be some sense in which our choices are not
absolutely free." Simplicity is affirmed in the doctrine of God and then not
recruited in the doctrine of providence.

Why not is worth stating carefully, because this note is not a claim that Grudem
overlooked an available argument. His simplicity is thinner than Maimonides': it
denies stages in the knower, not the distinction between a knower and what is
known, and it is nowhere developed into the identity of God with his knowledge
that Maimonides needs. And where Maimonides is willing to say that God's knowing
is not knowledge in any sense we possess, Grudem's whole method resists that —
his God is described in terms taken from creatures and qualified downward, which
is what "communicable attributes" means. The two positions on simplicity that
this base had filed as a difference about God's *being* turn out to have
different consequences for what can be said about human *freedom*. That is the
finding: an axis the base built for the same-God question does work in the
free-will question too.

**Limits.** Maimonides' argument is stated in the Mishneh Torah in a few
sentences and referred to Yesodei HaTorah ch. 2, which is not on file; the
*Guide* III, where it is developed, is unread here. Whether the move succeeds is
contested inside Judaism — Ra'avad's gloss on this very halacha is the classic
objection, and no commentary is captured. On the Christian side, this note
compares Grudem to Maimonides, not Christianity to Judaism: the Thomist
tradition uses divine simplicity and eternity in a way much closer to
Maimonides', and none of it is on file here.
