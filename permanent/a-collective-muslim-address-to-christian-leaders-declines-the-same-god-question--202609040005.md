*A Common Word Between Us and You* (2007) is a letter from Muslim scholars,
clerics and intellectuals to the leaders of the Christian churches, addressed
to Christians specifically, on the relation between the two faiths. It never
asks whether Muslims and Christians worship the same God, and it never answers
that question in those terms — the phrase does not occur in the captured text,
nor does the question in any other wording
([[a-common-word-grounds-common-ground-in-shared-commandment--202609040001]],
[[a-common-word-between-us-and-you--202609032358]], part III). What it offers
instead is a different basis for the relation: "love of the One God, and love of
the neighbour," which it calls "the common ground between Islam and
Christianity."

The substitution is deliberate, not evasive, and the letter says what it is
doing. Difference and commonality are asserted in one sentence: "Whilst Islam
and Christianity are obviously different religions—and whilst there is no
minimising some of their formal differences—it is clear that the Two Greatest
Commandments are an area of common ground and a link between the Qur'an, the
Torah and the New Testament." The commonality claimed is between commands and
between texts. It is not a claim about a shared object of worship, and the
letter declines to convert it into one.

That bears directly on
[[the-same-god-question-turns-on-reference-versus-description--202608311942]],
which is the most-linked note in this base. That note's thesis — that the
dispute is two questions wearing one sentence, one about reference and one about
description — is not shown false here; it remains a good account of the
disputants it was built from. What is false is its implicit further claim to be
the *shape of the dispute as such*. Its four sources are Gjelten, Nostra Aetate,
Tachin and Vroom: a secular journalist, a Catholic council, a Reformed
theologian, and a philosopher of religion. Every one of them accepts that the
question "do they worship the same God?" is the question, and disagrees about
how to answer it. Put a collective Muslim statement addressed to Christian
leaders beside them and a third position appears that the grid has no cell
for: the question is not the one to ask, and what should be settled instead is
what each side is commanded to do. Reference and description are not exhaustive
because declining to choose between them is available, and was chosen.

The nearest thing already in this base is halakhic rather than Muslim, and the
resemblance is structural.
[[shituf-grades-the-worshipper-instead-of-identifying-the-worshipped--202609011542]]
also refuses the identification question and answers a different one — there,
what a gentile may permissibly do; here, what both communities are commanded to
do. Two traditions, asked whether the other's God is theirs, both change the
subject to obligation. That is worth more than either instance alone, because
it suggests the reference/description framing may be characteristically the
framing of those *outside* the practical question rather than the framing of
practitioners.

A note on how this source is described, since the temptation is to reach for a
superlative. This letter is commonly called the most prominent statement of its
kind and the letter of 138 scholars. Neither claim is made here and neither is
needed. What the capture establishes is that the statement is collective, that
it is addressed to the leaders of the Christian churches, and that it was
published in 2007 by The Royal Aal al-Bayt Institute for Islamic Thought. That
is enough for the argument above: one party to the dispute, speaking
collectively and in its own name, declined the question. A ranking would add
rhetorical weight and no evidence.

Where the letter does approach divine identity it stops short on purpose. It
places the Shema (Deut. 6:4), Jesus' citation of it (Mark 12:29) and Surah
al-Ikhlas (112:1-2) side by side and concludes that "the Unity of God, love of
Him, and love of the neighbour form a common ground upon which Islam and
Christianity (and Judaism) are founded." That is a claim that the same
*doctrine* stands at the head of both. It does not take the further step to the
same *being*, and the letter is careful enough elsewhere that the omission
should be read as chosen. Its treatment of Christ is the evidence that it is not
simply being diplomatic: "Muslims recognize Jesus Christ as the Messiah, not in
the same way Christians do."

ON THE `contested` TAG, which this note was first given and then had removed.
FR-12 requires a note tagged `contested` to rest on three independent
references, and this one rests on a single document. The tag was dropped rather
than the requirement bent, because on reflection it was the wrong tag: this note
does not take a side in the same-God dispute. Its claim is documentary — that a
particular collective statement declines the question — and its interpretive
extension is fenced by the limits below. The note it contradicts,
[[the-same-god-question-turns-on-reference-versus-description--202608311942]],
is correctly tagged `contested` and keeps that tag. If a later run adds Ibn
Taymiyya and al-Ghazali and this note grows into a claim about Muslim theology
generally, it will need the tag and will then have the sources for it.

TWO LIMITS, both real. First, this is one document, and a collective ecumenical
address with a stated peacemaking purpose is a genre with its own reasons for
setting a divisive question aside — so what is established is that this
statement declines the question, not that Muslim theology declines it. Ibn
Taymiyya's *al-Jawab al-Sahih*, which argues the question rather than dissolving
it, is exactly the hostile witness that would test this, and it is not yet in
this base. Second, the letter is captured as an excerpt, not in full. Both
limits bear on how far this note travels; neither touches the claim that the
question is absent from what was captured, which was checked directly.
