The arithmetic of compound growth is certain; what is uncertain, over
horizons long enough for the arithmetic to matter, is that the reinvestment
loop keeps running. Money must actually be relent, managers must actually
exist, and nothing may divert the principal — for every period of a horizon
measured in centuries. So the binding constraint on long-horizon compounding
is not the rate or the formula but the survival and fidelity of the
institutions executing the loop.

The designer of the canonical long-horizon scheme says this himself.
Franklin's 1789 codicil projects £1,000 into £131,000 and then £4,061,000,
but each projection is wrapped in its own conditional — "without
interruption", "if no unfortunate accident has prevented the operation" —
and he concedes the whole plan may be a "vain fancy" given "the accidents to
which all human affairs and projects are subject in such a length of time"
([[franklin-stakes-a-two-century-bequest-on-uninterrupted-compounding--202609010508]],
[[franklin-last-will-and-testament-and-codicil--202609010507]]). His hedges
are institutional, not financial: staged disbursement at year one hundred,
volunteer management, fallback clauses for either city refusing the trust.

This bounds [[compounding-favors-horizons-longer-than-one-life--202609010205]]
and [[time-horizon-dominates-rate--202701010013]] from the other side: if
horizon dominates rate, then the scarce input is not patience in intent but
a structure that survives long enough to deliver it, and claims about
century-scale returns inherit the mortality statistics of institutions.
(Reading that as a general law, beyond Franklin's own framing of his scheme,
is this note's inference.) It is the dark twin of
[[a-trusted-structure-frees-attention-from-willpower--202608311142]]: there
a durable structure replaces willpower; here the structure's durability is
itself the load-bearing, failure-prone part.
