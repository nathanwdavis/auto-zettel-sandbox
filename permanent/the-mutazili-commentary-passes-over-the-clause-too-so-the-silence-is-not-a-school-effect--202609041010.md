> **AMENDED 2026-09-04 11:30Z — the title claim is too wide, and the limit this
> note named for itself is what caught it.** The last paragraph below says the
> survey is not exhaustive and that the Shi'i tradition has not been touched.
> It has now been touched, and the clause is glossed there: al-Tusi (d. 460) and
> al-Tabrisi (d. 548) both gloss `وإلهنا وإلهكم واحد` as `لا شريك له`
> ([[the-shii-commentaries-do-gloss-the-clause-so-the-classical-silence-is-a-sunni-fact--202609041130]]).
> So the silence IS a school effect — at the Sunni/Shi'i boundary, which this
> note did not test, rather than at the Mu'tazili/Ash'ari one, which it did.
> What survives unchanged is the claim about the sample: within the Sunni
> commentaries, the widest theological gap available does not explain the
> silence. What fails is the generalisation from that sample to "the tradition".
> Read the note below as bounded to the Sunni commentaries throughout.

al-Zamakhshari (d. 538/1144) comments on Q 29:46 and never reaches
`وإلهنا وإلهكم واحد`. That closes the last standing explanation for the silence
of the commentaries after al-Tabari: that it belongs to one theological school
rather than to the tradition.

**The objection this note answers was, again, the base's own.** When
[[the-kalam-commentary-is-silent-on-the-clause-too-so-the-silence-is-not-a-genre-effect--202609040915]]
disposed of the genre explanation by adding al-Razi, the same cycle filed the
next objection against its own result: al-Tabari, al-Qurtubi, Ibn Kathir,
al-Baghawi and al-Razi are all, in one sense or another, Sunni traditionalists,
and a shared silence among them may be a fact about that family and not about
Islamic exegesis. On that explanation the identification might be live somewhere
outside it, and the base would simply not have looked.

**Why al-Zamakhshari is the test.** *al-Kashshaf* is the classical Mu'tazili
commentary — read for centuries by scholars who rejected its theology and had to
write around it — and its author's doctrinal commitments are the furthest in the
group from the others'. Nor is *tawhid* a subject he is reticent about in this
very passage: he glosses "if they turn away" at Q 3:64 as turning away *from
tawhid*, supplying the word himself where the verse does not
([[zamakhshari-calls-the-common-word-undisputed-across-the-three-scriptures-and-never-glosses-the-shared-god-clause--202609041005]]).
If any classical commentator outside the traditionalist family would stop at a
clause asserting that the God of two parties is one, he is the one.

**The evidence, and it is an absence, so it was checked as one.** Neither
`معبود` — the word in which al-Tabari's identification consists — nor `واحد` in
any form occurs anywhere in al-Zamakhshari's comment on Q 29:46. The counts were
taken on two independently served copies, tafsir.app and quranpedia.net, under a
normaliser written out by code point and run with its positive controls in the
same pass, and both come out zero
([[zamakhshari-al-kashshaf-on-3-64-and-29-46--202609041000]]). `واحد` occurs
twice in his comment on Q 3:64, so the word is one the tool can see. His comment
runs from `بالتي هي أحسن` through the exception and the *dhimma* readings to
`وقولوا آمنا بالذي أنزل إلينا`, then to a hadith, and stops — one clause short of
the shared-God formula.

**What this does and does not license.** It licenses dropping the school
qualification along with the genre one: five commentaries, four centuries, two
theological families, and the clause is left alone by all of them. It does not
license reading the silence as denial, and it does not make the survey
exhaustive — the base has not touched al-Zamakhshari's own commentators, the
Shi'i tradition, or the modern Arabic commentaries other than the two it holds.
The claim that survives is still the narrow one: the identification of the
shared God as a shared object of worship is al-Tabari's own move, and the
commentary tradition after him neither repeats nor disputes it.
