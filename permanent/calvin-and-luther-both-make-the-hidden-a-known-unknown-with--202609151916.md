Neither Reformer subdivides what God has not made known. For both it is one class, and what each adds to the bare partition is a duty toward it. The duties differ, and the difference is the finding.

**What they share.** Both affirm that the hidden tier exists and that its existence is all that can be known of it. Luther: "It is enough to know only, that there is in God a certain will inscrutable: but what, why, and how far that will wills, it is not lawful to inquire, to wish to know, to be concerned about, or to reach unto — it is only to be feared and adored!" ([[luther-divides-god-preached-from-god-hidden-grants-that-the--202609151912]]). Calvin: "it is not lawful for men to pry into the secret things of God", which the Lord "has been pleased to conceal within himself" and which "it is his pleasure that we should not apprehend but adore" ([[calvin-fences-the-doctrine-of-election-with-deuteronomy-29--202609151907]]). In the owner's terms both treat the hidden as a known unknown at the level of the class, with the members placed under a prohibition rather than merely out of reach. Both also give the revealed tier a criterion: for Luther it is the word, "as far as He is clothed in, and delivered to us by, His Word"; for Calvin it is the word bounded by welfare, "revealed in so far as he knew to be conducive to our interest and welfare".

**Where they part.** Calvin's rule is two-sided and he uses Deut. 29:29 once for each side: "the secret things of God are not to be scrutinized, and that those which he has revealed are not to be overlooked, lest we may, on the one hand, be chargeable with curiosity, and, on the other, with ingratitude". The hidden is adored; the revealed is owed study; and the posture toward the hidden is given a name, an ignorance that is learning. Luther's rule is one-sided severance. The hidden God is "not preached, not revealed, not offered unto us", and "In whatever, therefore, God hides Himself and will be unknown by us, that is nothing unto us". Adoration survives in Luther too, but alongside "nothing unto us" and "What is above us, does not concern us", which Calvin never says. Calvin fences the hidden and keeps it in view; Luther walls it off and turns away.

**Whose partition it is.** Luther's line runs through God's *will*, not through knowledge: God preached against God hidden is the word against the decree, and "God does many things which He does not make known unto us in His word: He also wills many things which He does not in His word make known unto us that He wills." That is, in 1525, the distinction this base holds from Grudem as the secret and revealed will ([[grudem-splits-gods-will-into-necessary-free-secret-and-revealed--202609081904]]), and it is the reason the base's earlier note found Grudem's proof text partitioning disclosure while his doctrine partitions will ([[deut-29-29-partitions-knowledge-and-grudem-partitions-will--202609081906]]): the doctrine is Luther's, and the verse was Calvin's fence around a different thing, curiosity about election. The two were joined later, by whom this base cannot yet say.

**The posture the base already had.** Helm's refusal to shrink the mystery of God and evil, keeping it where Augustine and Calvin left it ([[helm-leaves-the-mystery-of-god-and-evil-where-augustine-and-calvin-left-it--202609111824]]), is Calvin's rule applied: the hidden is to be adored and not mechanised. Luther's harder version, that the hidden God is nothing to us, has no representative in the base's modern sources, and it would treat the whole foreknowledge debate as an inquiry into what "is not lawful to inquire".

**Limits.** One section of Luther and four of Calvin, both in nineteenth-century English. Whether Calvin read *De servo arbitrio* on this point is not established here; the chapter as transcribed carries a note referring the reader to it, whose authorship, Calvin's or an editor's, this base has not settled. That Grudem's two wills descend from Luther's is a resemblance this base observes, not a lineage any source on file asserts.
