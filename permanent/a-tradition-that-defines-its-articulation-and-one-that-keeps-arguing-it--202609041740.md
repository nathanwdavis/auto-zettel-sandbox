The Trinity and the sefirot differ not only in what they assert but in what
kind of thing the assertion is, and that difference is visible in the two
sources side by side. Christianity's articulation of God passed through an
event that fixed its terms; Kabbalah's did not, and its central question about
what the sefirot are stayed open for centuries with no mechanism that could
close it.

Hodge's account of Nicaea makes the fixing explicit and even procedural. The
first of the council's three objects was "to remedy the confusion which
prevailed in the use of several important words employed in discussions on the
doctrine of the Trinity" — he singles out *hupostasis*, which was being used
both for substance and for person — and the second was to condemn errors. The
creed ends in an anathema, and the losing readings did not remain live options
afterwards; "Sabellianism was, therefore, soon almost universally rejected"
(see [[hodge-on-the-two-readings-the-councils-ruled-out--202609041715]]).

Nothing of that shape happened to *atzmut* and *kelim*. Cordovero's fourth
gate opens by reporting that "a great confusion has fallen among the later
kabbalists" and closes by granting both parties standing — "these and those are
the words of the living God, and the law is as they say, though not for their
reasons" — before offering a third construction of his own (see
[[cordovero-states-the-essence-vessel-dispute-and-answers-it-with-both--202609041710]]).
The formula he uses to keep both is one that leaves a disagreement standing as
a disagreement: it awards the practical ruling while conceding that the losing
side is also speaking truly. Nothing in the gate excludes Recanati from the
tradition, condemns him, or claims any authority that could. What closes a
question in the one tradition is a body with power to anathematise; in the
other, on the evidence of this gate, what closes it is only whether a later
reader is persuaded. (How much *Pardes Rimmonim* was in fact read, and what
became of the instrumentalist reading after it, is a reception question no
source in this base answers; nothing above depends on it.)

The upshot for comparing the two doctrines is a caution about how they are
compared. A Christian doctrinal formula and a kabbalistic one are not two
answers of the same type: the first is a boundary drawn against named
alternatives and enforced, the second a reading that won an argument still
formally open. So a resemblance found between them is weaker evidence than it
looks — the kabbalistic side of any such pairing is one position among
tolerated others, and the same tradition contains a position that the pairing
would have to disown. That is a reason to state which reading of the sefirot a
comparison uses, every time, rather than to abandon the comparison.

This is a difference in the mode of the doctrines, not a ranking of them. It
says nothing about which articulation is true, and nothing about whether a
tradition is better for having settled its question or for having kept it.
