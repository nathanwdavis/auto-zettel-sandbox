A filing system built around topic heads answers exactly one question —
"where did I put what I filed under this word?" — and can answer it very
well without ever recording how one idea bears on another. Locke's
commonplace method is the clean historical case
([[locke-a-new-method-of-making-common-place-books--202609010602]]): a
two-letter index guarantees that any head is findable again, and the
method's one cross-reference primitive, the reciprocal page numbers,
points a topic's pages only at *each other*, re-joining a single head "as
if they were join'd together"
([[locke-files-each-entry-under-a-head-keyed-by-two-letters--202609010603]]).
The linking machinery existed by 1686; it was aimed inward, at
continuation, not outward, at association. Nothing in the design relates
head to head, so what comes back out is what was put in, sorted.

That is precisely the axis on which the zettelkasten tradition later
diverged, which is why the contrast earns a note: Luhmann held that a note
gains its quality from its web of links and back-links
([[luhmann-frames-the-slip-box-as-a-communication-partner--202609010112]]),
and the archive-side finding that productivity rides on cross-region
shortcut links — classification being nearly arbitrary so long as links
are added ([[serendipity-in-a-note-web-rides-on-shortcut-links--202609010304]])
— inverts Locke's allocation of effort, which spends everything on the
classification key and nothing on association. The unit of storage moves
too: Locke files prose under a shared topic word, while linkable notes
must each hold one addressable idea
([[one-idea-per-note-enables-reuse--202701010002]]). Retrieval and
connection are therefore separable goods in a note system, and a design
can max out the first while providing none of the second — this note's
generalization from the one Locke case, offered as the reason
"searchable" is not the same property as "generative".
