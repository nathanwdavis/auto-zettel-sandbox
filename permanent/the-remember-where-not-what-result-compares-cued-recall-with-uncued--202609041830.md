The most quoted finding in the Google-effect literature is that people who
expect information to stay available remember *where* it is rather than *what*
it says. In the experiment behind it, folder recall came in at 0.49 and
statement recall at 0.23, `t(31) = 6.70, P < 0.001`. The two numbers are not
comparable, and the paper says so on the same page it reports them.

Participants recalled the statements freely, with no prompt. They recalled the
folders when handed an identifying feature of the statement — asked, for a
statement about an ostrich, "What folder was the statement about the ostrich
saved in?" One task is uncued recall and the other is cued recall, which is
easier for reasons that have nothing to do with the internet. The authors are
explicit: "Participants did have a cue to memory (a word from the trivia
statement) with the folder recall that the statements themselves did not have.
We were not able to counterbalance the trivia and the folders trials such that
the folders were as numerous as the statements, which would be necessary to
counterbalance the uncued and cued recall tasks." They call the finding
"preliminary evidence"
([[sparrow-liu-and-wegner-ran-one-priming-experiment-and-three-offloading-ones--202609041810]]).

What survives the confound is thinner and more interesting than the headline.
The authors also ran an if/then analysis over the same data, and there the
comparison is internal: participants recalled the folder without the statement
about as often as they recalled nothing at all (0.30 against 0.38), and both
far more often than they recalled the statement without the folder (0.11) or
both together (0.17). That pattern does not depend on the cueing asymmetry
between tasks, because every cell of it comes from the same pair of tasks. It
says that when the content is gone the location can still be there, which is a
real claim about how the two are stored. It does not say that location is
remembered *better than* content, which is what the headline says and what the
cueing asymmetry was needed to produce.

The distinction matters here specifically. A slip box is a claim about
addresses: that what you must keep in your head is where things are and how
they connect, not what they say. If "we remember where, not what" were an
established finding about human memory, the method would have a psychological
warrant. It is not one — it is an uncounterbalanced comparison its own authors
flagged, never replicated
([[the-google-effects-replication-record-reaches-only-its-priming-experiment--202609041820]]).
The warrant a slip box actually has is architectural rather than psychological,
and this repository already holds the distinction:
[[topic-head-filing-optimizes-retrieval-not-connection--202609010604]] argues
about what a filing scheme makes *findable*, and makes no claim about what its
user remembers. That is the kind of claim the evidence supports.

A note about how this was caught, because it generalises. Nothing here required
data the paper withheld or an audit by a third party. The caveat is two
sentences long, sits directly beneath the result, and is written by the people
who ran the experiment. It disappeared somewhere between the paper and the
citations — which is a reason to read the paragraph after a famous number
before citing it, and a reason this repository captures the caveat alongside
the result in `raw/` rather than the result alone.
