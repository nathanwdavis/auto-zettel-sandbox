The paper the sovereignty/free-will outline argues for — that God's sovereign
will and human free will are both true — has its sharpest one-sentence
statement in Westminster Confession 3.1, and the point of that sentence is
that the two are not two claims held in tension but one claim. God "did ...
freely, and unchangeably ordain whatsoever comes to pass," and the very same
sentence carries the consequence "nor is the liberty or contingency of second
causes taken away, but rather established"
([[westminster-confession-of-faith--202609120640]], WCF 3.1). The grammar does
the work the systematician usually has to argue for: the ordination is the
subject and the establishment of creaturely liberty is a subordinate clause of
the same decree, so that on the Confession's telling sovereignty is what
grounds liberty rather than what competes with it. Chapter 9's definition of
that liberty — a will "neither forced, nor, by any absolute necessity of
nature, determined to good, or evil" (WCF 9.1) — makes the freedom compatibilist:
it is freedom from compulsion, not freedom from the decree, which is why the
same document can say both without qualification
([[the-freedom-grudem-deferred-is-compatibilist-freedom--202609081827]]).

This is a stronger move than balancing two doctrines: it denies that they
were ever two. The companion paragraph 3.2, which states that God has "not
decreed anything because he foresaw it as future ... upon such conditions,"
then fixes the order of dependence — the decree grounds the foreknowledge, not
the reverse — and is where the single proposition does polemical work against
middle knowledge
([[the-reformed-answer-to-the-grounding-objection-is-the-decree-and-the-confession-runs-opposite-to-molina--202609111825]]).
