Q 2:221 forbids two marriages in two clauses. Do not marry *al-mushrikat* until
they believe; do not give in marriage to *al-mushrikin* until they believe.
Al-Sabuni's two rulings on this one verse assign the word two different
extensions.

In the first clause, `المشركات` excludes the women of the Book: the *mushrika* is
"she who worships idols and has no revealed religion", and a Muslim man may marry
a Jewish or Christian woman. In the second, `المشركين` includes their men — "every
unbeliever who does not profess the religion of Islam, so it takes in the
idolater, the Magian, **the Jew, the Christian** and the apostate."

The same author, on the same page, on the same word, in one verse.

What decides the difference is not a re-assessment of what Jews and Christians
believe. Nothing between the two rulings revises the theology. What changes is
the direction the marriage runs, and the reason given is
`أن الإسلام يعلو ولا يُعلى عليه` — Islam is exalted and is not exalted over —
spelled out as household authority: the husband holds `سلطة وولاية` over the
wife, and children follow the father.

This is the sharpest available evidence that *mushrik* in the juristic literature
is not functioning as a stable metaphysical classification of whom someone
worships. It is functioning as a legal category whose boundary is set by what the
ruling has to accomplish. Where the ruling protects a Muslim household from
being governed by a non-Muslim, the term stretches to whoever is not a Muslim;
where the ruling has to leave Q 5:5's explicit permission standing, it contracts
to idolaters proper.

Which is why this note is marked `refutes` against
[[the-qurans-own-syntax-is-the-classical-proof-that-the-people-of-the-book-are-not-mushrikun--202609041320]]
rather than merely qualifying it. That note reports the grammatical argument
that the Qur'an's conjunction fixes the extension of *mushrikun* and keeps the
People of the Book outside it. If the extension were fixed that way, it could not
be one thing in the first half of a verse and another in the second half, in the
work of an author who states the grammatical argument himself in the ruling
immediately before — proof (a) of the first ruling and the second ruling sit on
consecutive served pages of the same section. The syntax argument is real and it
is what the majority cites, but it is not doing the work it claims to do: it
settles the first clause and is simply not applied to the second.

The analogy to *shituf*
([[shituf-grades-the-worshipper-instead-of-identifying-the-worshipped--202609011542]])
is close and instructive. There, a halakhic category grades what a gentile may be
held to rather than deciding what a gentile's God *is*. Here, a Qur'anic category
tracks what a ruling needs rather than deciding what a Christian worships. Both
traditions, at the point where the law has to act, decline to make the same-God
question do the work — and both leave that question exactly where it was.

One counterweight, from the same section, kept because it cuts against the tidy
reading above: al-Sabuni also justifies the asymmetry by shared *content*, saying
the Muslim husband "meets her on faith in God and veneration of His messengers"
(`لأنه يلتقي معها على الإيمان بالله، وتعظيم رسله`). That is a same-God argument,
offered in the same breath as the hierarchy argument, and he does not reconcile
the two. This base should not tidy them either.
