The cost this map recorded for Molinism is feasibility: God chooses among
whole orders of things, but there may be worlds he cannot actualize, because
what free creatures would do in them is not up to him
([[weak-actualization-claims-meticulous-providence-without-determination-and-concedes-infeasible-worlds--202609090103]]).
Laing, summarising Basinger in uncaptured text, states the contrast the
compatibilist wants: a God limited only by logical possibility against a God
limited also by counterfactuals he does not control. Then he asks whether a
compatibilist middle knowledge gets the first God. Take Adam and the fruit,
with (5) the counterfactual that he would compatibilistically eat and (6)
that he would fast: "According to Tiessen, God can actualize a world where
(6) is true and (5) is false. But how can he, apart from Adam’s choice being
in some way dependent upon the will of God? If it is dependent upon the will
of God, then the truths of (5) and (6) are not part of God’s middle
knowledge. If it is not dependent upon the will of God, then it seems that
God is just as constrained in terms of the kind of world he can actualize in
the Calvinist Molinist model as he is in the traditional (libertarian)
Molinist model". The one escape is to hold "that any possible free action
can be elicited from any given creature, if the proper circumstances and
influences obtain", which Laing finds "hard to believe" and tests with
Lamentations 2:20: more likely that "At least one mother could never be
influenced to freely eat her own child" than that every mother could
([[laing-argues-that-calvinist-middle-knowledge-is-caught-in-a-dilemma--202609111818]],
from [[laing-the-compatibility-of-calvinism-and-middle-knowledge--202609111813]]).

Ware takes that horn, at least for evil. In the passage Van Horn quotes (GGG
123-24), God "controls whether evil is done, what evil is done, and in any
and every case he could prevent the evil from being done", by "regulating
the factors of a situation"; and in Van Horn's summary of the whole picture,
God "chose a world in which he knew his creatures would freely do what he
wanted done"
([[van-horn-records-the-concession-and-argues-that-counterfactuals-can-be-natural-or-free-but-not-middle--202609111817]],
from [[van-horn-on-incorporating-middle-knowledge-into-calvinism--202609111812]]).
On that account there is no feasibility residue: every outcome God wants is
available through some arrangement of factors, because the factors, and the
natures that respond to them, are his. That is the first horn of Laing's
dilemma accepted in full, and its price is the one the previous notes
record: the counterfactual is true by what God makes and arranges, so the
knowledge is not middle.

Two consequences for the comparison with Grudem. His strongest objection to
middle knowledge, that a situation is itself made of other people's choices
([[a-situation-is-made-of-other-peoples-choices--202609081830]]), does not
trouble Ware at all: on Ware's account the other people's choices are
outputs of natures and factors God arranges, so regulating a situation is
regulating them. And the residue Grudem's tie-breaker weighs, which position
leaves God greater
([[the-dispute-is-decided-by-which-residue-honours-god-more--202609081831]]),
comes out the way Grudem wants only because Ware pays nothing on
feasibility; the Molinist pays it in full, and Laing's point is that a
compatibilist cannot have Molina's freedom from the charge of determinism
and Grudem's freedom from the feasibility limit at once.

**Limits.** Basinger's passages are summarised from uncaptured text. Ware's
"could prevent" is said of evil; whether he claims that every good act can
be elicited from every creature is not in the captures, and Laing's mother
is an intuition offered against Tiessen, not an argument against Ware, whom
the article never quotes. That Grudem's objection does not trouble Ware is
this base's inference from the two accounts; neither author addresses the
other.
