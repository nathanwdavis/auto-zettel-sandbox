[[maimonides-denies-any-composition-in-gods-unity--202608311932]] ends by issuing
a challenge — "any account on which the one God contains real internal
distinctions has to answer it" — and this base never answered it. The answer is
that the challenge is shared and the conclusions are not, because "no
composition" is not one claim.

**The argument is common property.** Maimonides: what has a cause has existence
"superadded to its essence"; God has no cause, so "existence and essence are
perfectly identical." Aquinas: "every composite is posterior to its component
parts, and is dependent on them; but God is the first being," and "every
composite has a cause, for things in themselves different cannot unite unless
something causes them to unite." Same inference, from dependence to simplicity,
in both. The survey confirms the reach: the doctrine "is represented not only in
classical Christian theology, but also in Jewish, Greek, and Islamic thought"
(see [[the-survey-puts-the-doctrine-in-four-traditions-and-reports-simplicity-used-for-the-trinity--202609042077]]).

**What it excludes is not common property.** Maimonides runs the conclusion into
predication and denies attributes outright: God "exists without possessing the
attribute of existence... knows, without possessing the attribute of knowledge,"
and even unity is "an accident supervening" (see
[[maimonides-denies-god-has-attributes-at-all-not-merely-that-they-divide-him--202609042075]]).
Aquinas builds his by enumerating six kinds of composition — quantitative parts,
matter and form, nature and *suppositum*, essence and existence, genus and
difference, subject and accident — and a plurality of persons is not on the list,
nor raised in the question (see
[[aquinas-argues-simplicity-from-dependence-and-stops-short-of-the-attributes--202609042076]]).

So the two traditions do not hold one doctrine with a disagreement appended.
They hold one *argument* whose scope each fixes differently: Maimonides extends
it until it consumes the attributes, Aquinas terminates it at metaphysical
composition and leaves the persons to a later question. Asking which is "the"
doctrine of divine simplicity is asking a question neither of them is answering.
