When the classical law needs to decide whether Jews and Christians fall under the
Qur'anic term *mushrikun*, the argument it reaches for is not theological but
syntactic. Twice the Qur'an names them alongside the associators and joins the
two with a conjunction:

- Q 2:105 — "those who disbelieve **from among the People of the Book** *nor*
  **the associators**"
- Q 98:1 — "those who disbelieved **from among the People of the Book** *and*
  **the associators**"

From which: `ظاهرُ العطف يقتضي مغايرةً بين المعطوف والمعطوف عليه` — the apparent
force of the conjunction requires a distinction between the conjoined term and
the term it is conjoined to. If the People of the Book were already inside
*al-mushrikin*, naming both would be naming the same people twice. So the
apparent sense of *mushrikat* in Q 2:221 does not reach the women of the Book,
and the permission of Q 5:5 conflicts with nothing.

Al-Qurtubi (d. 671/1273) reports the argument as the view of "some of the
scholars"; al-Sabuni gives it seven centuries later as proof (a) of the
*jumhur*'s case, from the same two verses, reaching the same conclusion. The two
are not independent witnesses to the argument — al-Sabuni is compiling — but
they are independent witnesses to its standing across that span.

Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions.

**It is a real answer to the question this cluster has been circling.** Every
commentary this base captured on Q 3:64 and Q 29:46 attaches the *jizya* and the
sword to the shared-God verses without registering any tension with
`وإلهنا وإلهكم واحد`. The tension is registered — in the *ahkam* literature, on a
marriage verse, where the law is forced to say who a word covers. And the answer
the majority gives is that the Qur'an's own grammar keeps the People of the Book
out of the category of associators.

**And it is a thinner answer than it looks.** The argument establishes a fact
about the *word*, not about the parties. It says the Qur'an does not use
*mushrikun* as a label for them; it does not say they do not associate. That gap
is exactly where the counter-position lives — see
[[whether-the-people-of-the-book-are-mushrikun-is-a-disputed-question-of-law--202609041325]]
— and it is why the same tradition can hold this argument and still produce
al-Zamakhshari extending *mushrikun* to the Jews and Christians at Q 3:67
([[a-classical-commentary-applies-mushrikun-to-the-people-of-the-book-inside-the-common-word-passage--202609041020]])
and al-Razi excluding trinitarians as associators inside Q 29:46 itself
([[the-classical-exclusion-of-trinitarians-is-made-inside-the-verse-that-carries-the-shared-god-clause--202609040925]]).
Those are marked as `contradicts` above and the contradiction is real, but it is
a contradiction between a claim about a term's legal extension and claims about
what the People of the Book in fact do. A tradition can hold both without
inconsistency, and this one does.

The limit worth stating plainly: this base has no independent measure of how the
classical field actually divided. "The majority" is the sources' own word.
