Judaism and Christianity hold the Pentateuch in common, so the interesting
question is not which texts each cites but what kind of evidence each takes a
commandment to be. On the free-will question they differ at exactly that point,
and the difference is systematic rather than a matter of proof-texts traded back
and forth.

For Maimonides a command is evidence about the commanded creature. Deut. 30:15
and 11:26 — "Behold, I have set before you today life [and good, death and
evil]" — are quoted to establish "that the choice is in your hands," and the
argument is generalised: if God decreed a person righteous or wicked, "how could
He command us through [the words of] the prophets: 'Do this,' 'Do not do this'…?
What place would there be for the entire Torah? According to which judgement or
sense of justice would retribution be administered to the wicked or reward to
the righteous?" Free will is therefore "a fundamental concept and a pillar [on
which rests the totality] of the Torah and mitzvot" — not one doctrine among
others but the condition on which the commandments are intelligible
([[maimonides-makes-free-will-a-pillar-of-the-torah-and-refers-the-paradox-upward--202609081912]]).

Grudem's apparatus takes the same texts as evidence about God's *precept*, which
is compatible with his decreeing otherwise. His secret/revealed distinction
assigns commands to the revealed will, "God's declared will concerning what we
should do," while the secret will "includes most of his hidden decrees by which
he governs the universe and determines everything that will happen"
([[grudem-splits-gods-will-into-necessary-free-secret-and-revealed--202609081904]]).
Once that frame is in place, a commandment tells you what God requires and
nothing about what the creature is able to do independently of God's decree — so
the inference Maimonides makes is blocked before it starts. The two wills are
not a reply to Maimonides; they are a general instrument, and the blocking is a
side effect of how it works.

The consequence is that the two positions cannot meet on the commandment texts.
Maimonides argues *from* the existence of commands *to* an unconstrained
capacity to obey them; Grudem's frame classifies commands as one aspect of a will
whose other aspect determines whether they are obeyed. Each is coherent, and
neither can be refuted by producing more commandment texts, because they disagree
about what such a text is evidence of. Grudem's own handling shows the frame is
doing the work rather than the verses: he sorts 1 Tim. 2:4 to the revealed will
because "the New Testament is clear that … not all will be saved," which is an
argument from a doctrine to a classification
([[grudems-two-wills-are-sorted-from-outside-the-verse--202609081905]]).

**Limits.** Maimonides is not thereby the "Jewish position": rabbinic Judaism
contains determinist strands and this base has captured none of them, and
Ra'avad objected to Hilchot Teshuvah 5:5 in the standard printings. Grudem is
one Reformed voice, and the Arminian writers his own chapter quotes argue much
as Maimonides does here — the divide is not cleanly between religions. The
Samaritans, who hold the Pentateuch and nothing else and so are the sharpest test
of a Pentateuch-only argument, are entirely absent from this base. Whether
Maimonides' inference from command to capacity is sound is a real question this
note does not settle; the Reformed reply that a command can express obligation
without implying ability is exactly what the two-wills frame formalises.
