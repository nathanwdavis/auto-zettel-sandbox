"The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom" — Grudem's rendering — will
carry several readings. *Beginning* can be first in order of content (the proposition a wisdom
curriculum opens with), or an epistemic precondition (the stance without which
the rest cannot be understood), or a chief part standing for the whole, or the
starting point of a practice. Grudem picks one, and the because-clause he
attaches to the verse says which: "if we fear dishonoring God or displeasing
him, and if we fear his fatherly discipline, then we will have the motivation
that makes us want to follow his ways and live according to his wise commands"
(2nd ed., pp. 232-233,
[[grudem-closes-gods-wisdom-in-the-readers-own-life--202609081645]]). Every term
in the explanation is motivational — fear as an aversion, its object as a
consequence, its output named outright as *motivation* and *wanting*. Beginning
is what gets the practice going, not what is known first.

That reading is not arbitrary; it is what the definition two sentences earlier
calls for. Grudem has just glossed the wisdom in question as "skill in living a
life pleasing to God"
([[grudem-describes-a-communicable-attribute-by-route-and-fruit--202609081647]]).
A skill is an acquired competence exercised in conduct, and the thing that
begins a competence is wanting to exercise it, not a first premise. Had wisdom
been defined as a body of knowledge about God, the epistemic reading of
"beginning" would have been the natural one and the same verse would have been
glossed the other way. The exegesis follows the definition.

Two details of the passage confirm the fit rather than merely allowing it.
First, the fear is specified in kind. What is feared is "his fatherly
discipline" — *fatherly* is Grudem's word, not an emphasis added here. It is a
son's fear, operating inside a relation already granted — which matches the one
condition Grudem attaches to the comfort earlier on the same page, "if we are
his children." It is not the fear that would drive someone toward God from
outside, and so it cannot be functioning as a precondition of entry. Second,
the passage's test for possessing wisdom is behavioural too: humility rather
than pride, a gentle and peaceable spirit rather than arrogance, a continual
walk in dependence. A practical definition predicts that a skill will be
verified by performance, and that is the check given.

**Limits.** This is one gloss in a compressed closing paragraph, not an
exegesis. Grudem does not canvass the alternative readings, does not discuss
the Hebrew *re'shit* behind "beginning", and does not say whether he takes Prov.
1:7, Prov. 9:10 and Ps. 111:10 to be making one point or three; the three are
cited together with "cf." doing the only work of distinguishing them. Whether
he sustains the motivational reading in his chapters on Scripture, sanctification
or the fear of God is unknown here, since none of them is on file. The verses
themselves have not been independently captured and are known only as he cites
them.
