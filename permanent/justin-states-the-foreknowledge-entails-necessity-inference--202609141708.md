The inference that if an act is known in advance it cannot be otherwise is the
axis on which the modern dispute turns: open theism asserts it and gives up
exhaustive foreknowledge, Grudem and Ware assert the foreknowledge and reject
the inference, Molina denies the inference outright
([[ware-states-the-foreknowledge-entailment-as-open-theisms-own-reasoning-and-answers-it-as-grudem-does--202609111827]],
[[the-foreknowledge-entailment-is-a-weapon-in-chapter-16--202609081829]]).

Justin has it in the First Apology, about 155, and has it in the form of an
objection he expects his own argument to provoke. Having argued through
chapters 40-42 that Christ's life was foretold, he breaks off: "lest some
suppose, from what has been said by us, that we say that whatever happens,
happens by a fatal necessity, because it is foretold as known beforehand, this
too we explain". The inference is stated exactly — being foretold as known
beforehand is taken to imply fatal necessity — and it is stated so that it can
be refused.

His refusal has two parts. The first is the argument from desert: if "all
things happen by fate", then "neither is anything at all in our own power",
and neither praise nor blame attaches to anyone, so that "unless the human race
have the power of avoiding evil and choosing good by free choice, they are not
accountable for their actions, of whatever kind they be". The second is the
distinction that the later tradition lives on, that foreknowing is not
necessitating: "God foreknowing all that shall be done by all men" foretells
what he foreknows, but "what we say about future events being foretold, we do
not say it as if they came about by a fatal necessity"
([[justin-answers-the-fatalist-reading-of-prophecy-by-making--202609141702]]).

What is old here is the inference and its denial; what is not old is the use
made of them. Justin denies the entailment against pagan fate, with divine
foreknowledge as the premise he and his readers share. The moderns argue the
same entailment with a Christian doctrine of sovereign decree on the other
side. The proposition is continuous and the controversy is not.

**Limits.** "Earliest preserved" is a claim about what survives. Justin's
formulation is also not a technical one: he neither distinguishes necessity of
the consequence from necessity of the consequent, as the scholastics later do,
nor addresses the grounding of God's knowledge, which is the question that
divides Molina from the Reformed. Reading his denial as an anticipation of any
particular modern position would go beyond the text.
