Warfield's cessationism is at root a claim about what the link between
miracle and faith was. The link was never the believer's faith. The
miraculous gifts "were not the possession of the primitive Christian as
such" but "distinctively the authentication of the Apostles", and "part of
the credentials of the Apostles as the authoritative agents of God in
founding the church" (pp. 5-6, in
[[warfield-makes-the-miraculous-gifts-the-credentials-of-the--202609230044]]).
His key evidence shows faith and the gifts coming apart. The Samaritans'
faith is genuine under Philip, yet the gifts arrive only through the hands
of Peter and John. The episode teaches the church "to distinguish between
the gifts of grace and the gifts of power" (pp. 22-23). When the
commissioning office ended, the gifts it authenticated ended with it.

Faith keeps a role, but Warfield moves it from miracle to prayer. He
accepts the strict definition of a miracle as "the immediate action of God,
as distinguished from His mediate action through natural laws". His
complaint against the faith-healers is that they count "every instance of
answer to prayer" as a miracle. Denying miraculous healing today denies
only "the continuance through all time of the specific mode of answer to
prayer which comes by miracle" and not "any real, answer to prayer"
(pp. 160-161). The praying believer is heard, but through God's mediate
action.

On the question of cause, Warfield agrees with Aquinas. Both make a miracle
a confirming sign from God rather than a product of the believer's faith
([[aquinas-ascribes-miracles-to-faith-as-their-end-and-their--202609230047]]).
They differ on who is confirmed and for how long. For Aquinas the thing
confirmed is "truth declared", and he lets even a wicked preacher of true
doctrine work true miracles. For Warfield it is the Apostles as founders,
and the gifts are confined to their age.
