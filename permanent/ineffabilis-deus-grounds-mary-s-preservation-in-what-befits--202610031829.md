The Immaculate Conception can look like the Catholic answer to how Christ
escaped original sin: a mother free of it passes none on. The
defining text does not argue that way. Ineffabilis Deus defines that Mary "was
preserved free from all stain of original sin" and grounds it in what befits
the Mother of God: it was "wholly fitting that so wonderful a mother" should be
"so completely free from all taint of original sin". The privilege comes "in
view of the merits of Jesus Christ", so Mary is presented as redeemed by her
Son, not as the condition of his purity
([[ineffabilis-deus-defines-mary-s-preservation-from-original--202610031822]]).

Aquinas, the standard Catholic account, explains Christ's freedom from
original sin without reference to Mary's state at all: Christ was "not
conceived from the seed of man. Thus it did not contract original sin"
([[aquinas-derives-christ-s-flesh-from-adam-s-matter-but-not--202610031819]]).

The mechanism reading shows up in a Protestant critic. Irving argued that
Christ must have taken fallen flesh "unless God had created the Virgin in
Adam's first estate", which he called "a figment of Romish superstition"
([[irving-holds-that-christ-took-fallen-flesh-and-was-kept--202610031823]]).
That treats the doctrine as a rival account of where Christ's unfallen
flesh came from, a role its own definition does not give it.
