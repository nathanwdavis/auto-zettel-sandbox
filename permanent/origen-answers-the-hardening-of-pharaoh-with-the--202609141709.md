Ware's proposal is that God causes good acts directly but only arranges the
factors that occasion evil ones, so that "without causing a person to do evil,
he nonetheless controls the evil they do", and yet "in no case does he cause
the evil to be done"
([[ware-makes-gods-agency-asymmetrical-causing-good-and-occasioning-evil-where-grudem-has-god-cause-all-things--202609111823]]).
The structure is older than the vocabulary.

Origen faces the hardest sovereignty text in the canon, that God hardened
Pharaoh's heart, and answers it by making the divine operation single and the
outcomes double. His illustration is the sun: one heat melts wax and dries mud,
so the same action of God meets two dispositions and produces opposite effects.
The hardening is then not a distinct divine work upon Pharaoh but the effect, in
Pharaoh, of a work that is good in itself. He adds a second consideration of the
same shape, that delay in curing is sometimes the physician's kindness rather
than his malice, since "a rapid cure is not for the advantage of those who are
healed" when the disease would otherwise rage inwardly
([[origen-treats-the-hardening-of-pharaoh-and-romans-9-as-the--202609141704]]).

Both writers are buying the same thing: an account on which God governs the evil
act without being its cause, so that sovereignty over the outcome survives and
authorship of the sin does not attach. Both pay in the same coin, a distinction
between what God does and what the creature's own constitution makes of it —
Ware's natures and factors, Origen's wax and mud.

The two are not the same doctrine. Ware's asymmetry is stated inside a
compatibilism, where the creature's nature and circumstances jointly settle the
choice and God arranges both; Origen's is stated inside a libertarian defence of
freedom, and the disposition the divine action meets is one the creature has
made for itself. The mechanism is shared and the account of the will underneath
it is opposed, which is why the same move can serve a Reformed and an
anti-determinist purpose.

**Limits.** This is a structural analogy, not a line of influence: nothing on
file shows Ware drawing on Origen, and the resemblance is claimed for the shape
of the answer only. Origen is read here in Rufinus' Latin as rendered in
Crombie's English, and the *De Principiis* survives chiefly in that Latin
translation, whose fidelity on contested points was disputed from Jerome
onward — the Greek fragments printed alongside in the same source are the check
available, and they carry the same argument at III.1.10-11.
