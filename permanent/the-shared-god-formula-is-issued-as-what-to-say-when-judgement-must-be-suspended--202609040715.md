The sentence in which the Qur'an comes closest to settling the same-God
question is transmitted, in the tradition's foundational commentary, as an
answer to a different question: what a Muslim should say when he cannot tell
whether what a Jew or a Christian has just told him is true.

al-Tabari sets Q 29:46's `وَإِلَهُنَا وَإِلَهُكُمْ وَاحِدٌ` inside exactly that
case. When the People of the Book relate things from their books "about which it
is possible they are truthful and possible they are lying, and you do not know
their case in that" — then say to them: we believe in what was revealed to us
and revealed to you, and our God and your God is one. He adduces the prophetic
tradition he takes to agree, from Abu Hurayra, on the People of the Book reading
the Torah in Hebrew and expounding it in Arabic to the Muslims: `لا تُصَدِّقُوا
أهْلَ الكِتابِ وَلا تُكَذّبُوهُمْ` — *do not believe the People of the Book and
do not disbelieve them* — and say the verse
([[tabari-on-the-common-word-and-the-shared-god-clause--202609040705]], ad Q
29:46).

**Every English rendering here is this repository's own working translation of
the captured Arabic**, with the Arabic printed beside each rendering that
carries weight
([[tabari-jami-al-bayan-on-3-64-and-29-46--202609040700]]).

**The formula's job, in this setting, is to make agnosticism sayable.** A Muslim
who can neither endorse nor reject a piece of scriptural testimony needs
something to say that commits him to no verdict on it. The formula supplies
one, and it does so by naming the widest thing the two parties share — the
revealed source, and the God who revealed. It is an exit from an epistemic
bind, and the co-reference claim is the exit's load-bearing part.

**This is why the form is a command and the content is still a proposition, and
both halves matter.** The base has argued that the major modern Muslim
intervention in this dispute makes its common ground out of shared obligation
rather than shared reference, taking neither horn of the reference/description
grid
([[the-muslim-consensus-statement-makes-common-ground-out-of-command-not-reference--202609040115]]).
This source corroborates the *form* of that observation from a thousand years
earlier and in a wholly different register: here too the shared-God material
arrives as an instruction about what to do, not as a thesis with an argument
attached. But it removes one explanation of the modern silence. The 2007 letter
could not have been silent about co-reference because the tradition had nothing
to say about it; the tradition's foundational commentary says it flatly
([[the-foundational-tafsir-glosses-the-shared-god-clause-as-a-shared-object-of-worship--202609040710]]).
Whatever the letter's silence is, it is not an inherited absence.

**The convergence worth noticing, and its limit.** Asked who is being
worshipped, the Jewish category of *shituf* answers by grading the worshipper
instead
([[shituf-grades-the-worshipper-instead-of-identifying-the-worshipped--202609011542]]);
here the tradition answers by ruling what the questioner may say. Both relocate
the question onto conduct. The difference is that *shituf* declines the identity
claim and al-Tabari makes it — so this is a convergence of form and not of
doctrine, and calling it more than that would overstate what either source
says. The observation is this base's own; neither source draws it.

**What this note does not claim.** Not that the formula's only use in the
tradition is this one — al-Tabari records Mujahid restricting the instruction to
those People of the Book who do not say the offending things, which is already a
second use, and later commentators are uncaptured here. And not that a rule of
speech is insincere: al-Tabari plainly takes the co-reference claim to be true.
The claim is about how the tradition *delivers* it, which is as a directive, in
a hard case, without argument.
