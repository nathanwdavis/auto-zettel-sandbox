Grudem defines God's knowledge as knowledge of "himself and all things actual
and possible in one simple and eternal act", and when he comes to explain the
word *simple* he gives it the same gloss the unity definition carries: "Here
again the word simple is used in the sense 'not divided into parts'"
([[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]], distilled at
[[grudem-defines-omniscience-as-one-simple-eternal-act-and-rejects-open-theism--202609061915]]).
What the gloss is then made to mean is not anything about the relation of
God's attributes to his being. It is that God "is always fully aware of
everything": he would not have to count the grains of sand "like some kind of
giant computer", nor "call the number to mind because it was something he had
not thought about for a time"; he "does not have to reason to conclusions or
ponder carefully before he answers"; "he never learns and never forgets
anything"; and his knowledge "never grows dim or fades into his nonconscious
memory". The *eternal* of the definition is explained the same way, as a denial
of growth: "If he were ever to learn something new, he would not have been
omniscient beforehand."

Every one of those is a denial of a *process* in the knower — counting,
recalling, inferring, forgetting, learning — and none of them is a denial of a
*composition* in the known or the knowing. "Not divided into parts" is here
applied to an act and cashed out as the act's having no stages and no
succession. That is a different job from the one the phrase does in the unity
definition, where "God is not divided into parts" is about God's being, and
where the chapter outline goes on to say that the attributes are "not
additions to his real being" and that each qualifies every other (see
[[grudems-chapter-11-outline-lists-five-incommunicable-attributes-with-unity-glossed-as-simplicity--202609050646]]).
One word, one gloss, two denials: of parts in the being, and of stages in the
knowing.

This matters for the axis in
[[divine-simplicity-is-the-question-the-monotheisms-actually-divide-on--202609042081]],
which sorts positions by how much of what is said about God the refusal of
composition is allowed to eliminate. The setting Grudem's incommunicable
attributes gave that axis was fixed by what the denial preserves — that God
acts and feels (see
[[grudems-incommunicable-attributes-are-denials-fenced-where-god-acts--202609050612]]).
The knowledge section shows the other face of the same setting. Where
Maimonides lets the argument consume the attribute of wisdom itself, Grudem
keeps a real attribute of knowledge, with a content that can be itemised
(himself, the actual, the possible), and applies "simple" not to the question
whether knowledge is distinct from God but to the question whether God's
knowing has a before and after. Simplicity of the act is temporal and
psychological before it is metaphysical. That is consistent with the fence the
earlier note found — a God who "acts and feels differently" in time is
compatible with a God whose *knowing* has no succession, and the eternity
definition's "sees all time equally vividly" is the same thought — but it is
not derived from it, and the section makes no reference to the attributes'
relation to the essence at all.

**Sources and their limits.** The section is on file in the second edition's
own words, from the owner's licensed export, with every quotation above
verified against the full text; this is the first Grudem note in the base not
dependent on first-edition pages quoted by third parties. The section's "here
again" refers back to an earlier use of the gloss that the export does not
include, so the identification of that earlier use with the unity definition
rests on the first-edition wording of that definition (witnessed at p. 177 of
the 1994 text) and on the phrase's exact recurrence, not on a second-edition
witness to the unity chapter. The claim that the section says nothing about the
attributes' relation to the essence is a claim about this section only; the
unity section, which is where Grudem treats that relation, is not on file in
the second edition. Whether the two denials are connected by an argument
elsewhere in the book — for instance in the eternity section, whose definition
already denies "succession of moments" in God's being — cannot be settled from
what is captured.
