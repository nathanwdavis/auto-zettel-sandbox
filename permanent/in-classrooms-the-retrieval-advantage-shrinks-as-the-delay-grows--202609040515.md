In the laboratory, the advantage of testing yourself over reading again *grows*
with the delay to the final test — that crossover is the reason the effect is
described as producing durable learning at all. Across fifty experiments run in
real schools and classrooms, the gradient points the other way: "Effect sizes
were larger following a 1-3 day delay, while smaller following an
end-of-semester delay"
([[agarwal-nunes-and-blunt-audited-the-classroom-half-and-declined-to-meta-analyse--202609040510]],
accepted manuscript p. 35).

The reviewers do not soften this or bury it. They state the conflict in the next
sentence — "in laboratory research, the opposite effect has been shown—longer
delays lead to a larger benefit" (p. 35) — cite Roediger and Karpicke 2006 as
the laboratory case, and make it the first of their eight recommendations for
future research (p. 43). The two bodies of evidence were not in dispute about
whether retrieval helps; they disagree about when it helps most, which is
precisely the question a claim about durability turns on.

**What this does and does not contradict.** It does not touch the finding that
retrieval beats re-exposure: 46 of 49 classroom effect sizes still favour
retrieval practice, and the review's verdict on its own question is "an
unequivocal 'yes'" (p. 42). What it contradicts is the extrapolation *from*
that finding — the step in
[[retrieval-not-re-exposure-is-what-makes-learning-last--202609040340]] that
reads the one-week crossover in a psychology laboratory as telling you what
happens at a semester's distance. That step now has evidence against it from the
only setting anyone actually cares about the answer in.

**Why it is weaker evidence than it sounds, and this has to be said in the same
breath.** Delay was never *manipulated* here. It is a variable coded across
studies that differ in everything else at once — subject, age, quiz format, how
often retrieval happened, whether the teacher was also the researcher — so the
comparison is between studies, not within one, and no causal claim about delay
survives it. The reviewers say as much by asking for the experiment that would
settle it: research "where a range of delays between retrieval practice and
final tests are directly manipulated, ideally ranging from days to weeks to
months" (p. 43). Their own candidate explanation, that benefits "increase in the
first few days, and then taper off as delays approach weeks or months" (p. 43),
is offered as a hypothesis and is not measured anywhere in the review.

**Why it matters here rather than only in education.** Everything this base has
about externalised memory concerns intervals of years — a slip-box's claim is
that a note written now pays out decades later. The retrieval literature's
strongest measurements sit at one week
([[roediger-and-karpicke-measured-retrieval-against-re-exposure-and-let-time-decide--202609040310]]),
and the only measurements taken at semester length find the effect at its
smallest. Whatever the resolution turns out to be, the direction of the
correction runs against the confident version of the claim, and this base should
not cite retrieval practice for anything at long horizons until the manipulation
the reviewers asked for exists.
