The Gospels give two answers to the question of faith's role in miracles,
and they point opposite ways. At Nazareth faith comes first. Jesus "could
there do no mighty work, save that he laid his hands upon a few sick folk",
and "marvelled because of their unbelief" (Mark 6:5-6, in
[[mark-6-1-6-at-nazareth-jesus-could-do-no-mighty-work-and--202609230045]]).
The unbelief belongs to the would-be beneficiaries, and it limits what
happens without stopping it altogether. John reverses the order. The signs
are written "that ye might believe" and, believing, have life (John
20:30-31, in [[john-20-24-31-the-signs-are-written-that-ye-might-believe--202609230046]]).
There faith comes after the miracle and is produced by it.

John also qualifies his own order in the same passage. Thomas believes
because he saw, and the blessing goes to "they that have not seen, and yet
have believed" (20:29). The signs therefore reach later readers as
testimony, not as sight. Mark's Nazareth scene shows the reverse limit:
seeing mighty works did not produce faith in people who already had reasons
to take offence (6:2-3).

The two directions are not contradictory, and Aquinas holds both in one
sentence. He cites the Nazareth unbelief (Matthew's parallel, 13:58) as a
case of miracles being "ascribed to faith", and he names "the confirmation
of faith" as their end
([[aquinas-ascribes-miracles-to-faith-as-their-end-and-their--202609230047]]).
Faith can be the setting in which a miracle happens and also the outcome
the miracle aims at. What the Gospel texts on file do not settle is whether
it is ever the cause.
