At pp. 232-233 Grudem told his reader that "God causes all things to move us
toward his ultimate goal for our lives" and argued nothing for it; the sentence
was a consolation resting on a verse
([[grudem-closes-gods-wisdom-in-the-readers-own-life--202609081645]]). Chapter
16 is where the causing is specified, and the specification is a single move:
divine and creaturely causation are not two shares of one quantity. "It is not
as though the event was partly caused by God and partly by factors in the
created world" — rather, "it is possible to affirm that in one sense events are
fully (100 percent) caused by God and fully (100 percent) caused by the creature
as well. However, divine and creaturely causes work in different ways"
([[grudem-defines-providence-as-preservation-concurrence-and-government--202609081824]],
from [[grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158]]).

The two causes are then distinguished by office rather than by portion. God is
the "primary cause" that "plans and initiates everything that happens," working
"as an invisible, behind-the-scenes, directing cause"; the creature's properties
are the "secondary causes," and — the detail that does the epistemic work — they
are "the causes that are evident to us by observation." So the botanist's
account of why grass grows and the claim that God makes it grow are not
competing for the same explanatory space, and finding the natural cause never
subtracts from the divine one. That is what licenses "all things" to range over
every event in a life without exception, which is exactly the scope the wisdom
section needed and did not defend.

Two consequences for how this base should read the earlier note. First,
[[grudems-romans-8-28-comfort-is-bought-with-the-freedom--202609081646]] argued
that the comfort at pp. 232-233 requires the arriving things to be under God's
ordering, and that most of what arrives comes through other people's choices.
Chapter 16 confirms the first half outright and, in its objection to middle
knowledge, makes the second half Grudem's own argument rather than this base's
inference (see
[[a-situation-is-made-of-other-peoples-choices--202609081830]]). Second, the
consolation is not the whole of what the doctrine is for: chapter 16 devotes a
section to blocking the inference from providence to passivity, and it does so
by putting means inside the decree — "God has ordained that our actions have
effects. God has ordained that events will come about by our causing them" — so
that "a hearty belief in God's providence is not a discouragement but a spur to
action." The wisdom section's reader, told to quiet discouragement, is the same
reader chapter 16 tells to study, to pray, and to keep the sailors on the ship.

**Limits.** The mechanism is a distinction, not an explanation, and Grudem says
so in his own voice: "Exactly how God combines his providential control with our
willing and significant choices, Scripture does not explain to us." On evil the
concession is stronger still, taken from Berkhof — "the problem of God's
relation to sin remains a mystery." So what the base has gained is the shape of
the answer and its refusal-point, not an account of how one event has two total
causes; the Macbeth analogy illustrates the shape and is offered as an analogy,
with an explicit caution against reading God as the doer of sin. The connection
drawn here between the two chapters is this base's, made across two separate
owner captures; Grudem does not cite his wisdom section in chapter 16, and the
chapter's own back-reference to chapter 12 is to the treatment of knowledge, not
of wisdom.
