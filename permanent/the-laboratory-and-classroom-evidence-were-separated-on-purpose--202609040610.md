The retrieval-practice literature appears to contradict itself about delay: in
the laboratory the advantage of testing grows the longer you wait, and in
classrooms it shrinks. That is not a disagreement about shared evidence. The two
reviews that report it excluded each other's studies by explicit criterion, and
neither has read the other's data.

Rowland's meta-analysis kept a study only if it had "not assessed participant
learning of information for a real or simulated class in which they were
enrolled", and records the count of what that removed: thirteen studies, with
readers referred to a different meta-analysis for them
([[rowland-meta-analysed-the-laboratory-half-and-excluded-the-classroom--202609040605]],
advance online p. 8). Agarwal, Nunes and Blunt screened in the opposite
direction and for the symmetrical stated reason — that "what works in the
laboratory does not necessarily work in the classroom—and vice versa"
([[agarwal-nunes-and-blunt-audited-the-classroom-half-and-declined-to-meta-analyse--202609040510]],
accepted manuscript p. 5). So the 159 laboratory effect sizes and the 49
classroom effect sizes are disjoint sets, assembled by people who each thought
the other set would contaminate theirs.

**The second half of the separation is the one that matters more, and it is
about range rather than setting.** Rowland's delay moderator has exactly two
levels, split at one day: under a day, `g = 0.41`; a day or more, `g = 0.69`
(advance online p. 15). The literature it summarises is described in the paper's
own framing as running from "short retention intervals on the order of minutes"
to "long retention intervals (of days or weeks)" (p. 6). The classroom review's
gradient begins roughly where Rowland's ends, and its two levels are stated
precisely enough to see that: larger at a one-to-three-day delay (k = 20),
smaller at the end of a semester, which its authors gloss as "approximately 6-15
weeks" (202609040510, p. 35).
Put the two side by side and the effect rises from minutes to a few days and
falls from a few days to a semester. Those are not contradictory findings. They
are two adjacent segments of one curve, each measured by people who could not see
the other segment.

**Credit where it is due: the classroom reviewers proposed this reading
themselves, as a guess.** Immediately after reporting the conflict they write
that "One possible explanation for this discrepancy is that the delays in
classroom settings are much longer than in laboratory settings", support it with
a second-hand statistic — in Adesope et al.'s review, "the majority of studies
(64%) had a delay of six or fewer days" — and offer the taper hypothesis
(202609040510, p. 43). What is new here is not the idea but its check. They were
inferring the laboratory range from a third review they cite rather than from the
laboratory meta-analysis itself; reading Rowland directly confirms it from the
inside, and adds the fact they do not mention — that his review and theirs
excluded each other's studies by explicit criterion, so the two gradients could
not have been in evidential conflict in the first place.

**What this does to the note it elaborates.**
[[in-classrooms-the-retrieval-advantage-shrinks-as-the-delay-grows--202609040515]]
was written from the classroom reviewers' own framing, in which their result runs
against the laboratory pattern — their words are that "in laboratory research,
the opposite effect has been shown—longer delays lead to a larger benefit"
(202609040510, p. 35). Having now read the laboratory meta-analysis rather than
their description of it, that framing is too strong: the
patterns are reverse only if you assume both were measured over the same
interval, and they were not. The correction runs in the direction of *less*
conflict between the two literatures and *no more* support for the long-horizon
claim — because the peak, on this reading, sits at a few days, and everything
past it is downhill.

**Why the reconciliation is not reassuring.** It would be, if this base were
interested in learning for a quiz. It is interested in whether writing something
down and coming back to it years later does anything, and on this reading the
whole measured literature — both halves, disjoint, roughly 200 effect sizes —
covers minutes to a semester and stops. The best-measured region is also the
least relevant one. Neither review's authors would claim otherwise; the classroom
reviewers ask for exactly the missing experiment, one in which delays are
"directly manipulated, ideally ranging from days to weeks to months"
(202609040510, p. 43), and it has not been run.

A caution this note has to carry about itself: the curve described above is
assembled across two reviews with different settings, different subjects,
different materials and different outcome measures, and delay was coded rather
than manipulated in both. It is a way of reading two results without forcing them
into conflict, not a measurement. What would falsify it is the within-study
manipulation nobody has done.
