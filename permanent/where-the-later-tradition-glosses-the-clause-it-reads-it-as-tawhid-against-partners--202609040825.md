> **AMENDED 2026-09-04 11:30Z — the reading is not "later"; it is classical, and
> there is now a third modern witness.** This note drew the no-partner reading
> from two modern commentaries and framed it as what the *later* tradition does.
> The framing was an artefact of the sample: al-Tusi (d. 460) and al-Tabrisi
> (d. 548) give the same reading in the same words, `لا شريك له`, three centuries
> before the earliest commentary cited below
> ([[the-no-partner-reading-of-the-clause-is-classical-not-modern--202609041135]]).
> Separately, Tantawi's *al-Wasit* — held back by the 08:00Z cycle because it
> stood on one host — is now attested on two independent hosts, altafsir.com and
> quranpedia.net, reading `لا شريك له لا فى ذاته ولا فى صفاته`
> ([[tantawi-extends-the-denial-of-partnership-into-the-essence-and-the-attributes--202609041125]]).
> The cross-check also corrects a detail this base had recorded: his lemma does
> NOT drop `وإلهنا`; that truncation belonged to the quran.com copy.

The classical commentaries after al-Tabari leave `وَإِلَهُنَا وَإِلَهُكُمْ
وَاحِدٌ` without comment
([[the-maabud-identification-is-al-tabaris-own-and-the-commentaries-after-him-do-not-repeat-it--202609040820]]).
Two widely used modern Arabic commentaries do gloss it, and neither reads it as
al-Tabari did. Both convert a clause whose surface says *the same one* into an
assertion that God has no partner.

**al-Tafsir al-Muyassar**, the official paraphrase distributed with the Qur'an
by the King Fahd Complex, renders the clause `وإلهنا وإلهكم واحد لا شريك له في
ألوهيته، ولا في ربوبيته، ولا في أسمائه وصفاته` — one, with no partner in His
divinity, nor in His lordship, nor in His names and attributes
([[the-muyassar-gloss-adds-no-partner-clauses-to-the-shared-god-statement--202609040818]]).
The Qur'anic clause ends at "one"; everything after it is the commentary's
addition, and it is the standard tripartite division of *tawhid* — divinity,
lordship, names and attributes. That trinitarian predication is classified under
the third of these is this repository's own gloss on why the expansion runs the
way it does; no source captured in this base states it, and a run that captures
a work of *ʿaqida* should either ground it or strike it. What the capture itself
shows is only that the expansion is spent entirely on what may not be predicated
of God.

**al-Sa'di** (d. 1376/1956) prints the clause inside a longer lemma and glosses
the whole as a rule for conducting disputation: let your argument with the
People of the Book be built on belief in what was sent down to you and to them,
on belief in your messenger and theirs, `وعلى أن الإله واحد` — and on *that the
deity is one*
([[saadi-turns-the-shared-god-clause-into-a-premise-for-disputation--202609040816]]).
The grammar is the finding. What the clause supplies, on this reading, is a
premise the Muslim disputant must not undercut, and the purpose he states for
proceeding this way is `إلزام لهم بالإقرار بالقرآن` — obliging the other party
to acknowledge the Qur'an.

**The distinction this turns on is the one the base already uses.** An
identification says *the thing you worship and the thing we worship are the
same thing*; it is about reference
([[the-same-god-question-turns-on-reference-versus-description--202608311942]]).
An assertion of oneness-without-partner says *what is true of God is this and
not that*; it is about description, and it is compatible with holding that the
addressee's description is so wrong that his worship misses its object. Both
modern glosses are of the second kind, and both are aimed, in the immediate
context, at the party whose doctrine the commentary regards as ascribing
partners.

**So the shape of the tradition's treatment of this clause, on what is now
captured, is: one identification, three silences, two doctrinal expansions.**
Nobody contradicts al-Tabari and nobody repeats him. That is a weaker and more
interesting result than either "the tradition affirms co-reference" or "the
tradition denies it", and it is the result the evidence carries.

**What this does not establish.** Two modern commentaries are not the modern
tradition, and neither is a work of argument — al-Muyassar is a paraphrase with
no dissent reported, and al-Sa'di's remark is made in passing inside advice
about disputation. Neither author is asked here what he would say if the
co-reference question were put to him directly; what is recorded is what each
does with the clause when he reaches it. A third modern commentary read this
cycle, Tantawi's *al-Wasit*, glosses the clause the same way, but no host
independent of Quran.com was found serving it, so it is left out of the count
rather than used to make the pattern look broader than its witnesses.
