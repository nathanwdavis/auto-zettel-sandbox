The dissociation between what people expect to remember and what they actually
remember is not a quirk of one laboratory's participants — it reappeared on an
independent sample that had never met the original experimenters. When the Social
Sciences Replication Project reran Karpicke and Blunt's Experiment 1 at the
University of Virginia, it collected the students' predictions alongside their
recall, and the predictions ran backwards: the concept-mapping group expected to
remember 69% while the retrieval group expected only 57.8%, `p = 0.025`
([[ssrp-replication-report-for-karpicke-and-blunt-2011--202609042135]]). The
group that would go on to remember *less* was the more confident one — the same
reversal the original paper reported
([[karpicke-and-blunt-retrieval-practice-produces-more-learning-than-concept-mapping--202609042130]]),
now on a fresh sample in a new setting.

That matters more than a single replicated significance test, because the
metacognitive reversal is doing separate work from the retention result. It is
the finding that people cannot feel this effect operating: the strategy that
performs worst is the one that feels most productive. The base already holds that
as a claim from within the Roediger–Karpicke line
([[confidence-tracks-re-exposure-while-retention-tracks-retrieval--202609040350]]),
built on studies that share authors and a laboratory. This adds the piece that
claim most wanted — an independent group measuring the same misprediction and
getting it — which is exactly the kind of outside confirmation the base withholds
judgement for. The scope stays narrow: one prediction elicited before a one-week
test, undergraduates, short science prose. It confirms that the illusion travels,
not how far.
