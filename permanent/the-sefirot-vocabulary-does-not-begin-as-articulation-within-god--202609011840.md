When this repository argues that Kabbalah is a Jewish position affirming real
internal distinction in God (see
[[internal-plurality-is-not-what-divides-the-monotheisms--202609011543]]), it
reads the ten sefirot of *Patach Eliyahu* as articulation *within* the One. That
reading is available for the zoharic text. It is not available for the source
that text cites. In the oldest work to use the word, the ten are a
series running from God's spirit outward into the world.

*Sefer Yetzirah* enumerates them in order, and the order is a descent: one is
"the Wind of the Living GOD"; two is "Wind from Wind", carved into the
twenty-two letters; three is "Water from Wind", from which mud and clay are
hewn; four is "Fire from Water", from which the throne of glory and the angels
are made; and five through ten are the six directions, above and below and the
four points of the compass (see
[[sefer-yetzirah-counts-ten-from-the-spirit-out-to-the-six-directions--202609011830]]).
Nine of the ten are elements, formed things, or dimensions of space. Whatever that
list is, it is not ten aspects of a divine essence.

The single ambiguity is the first member, and it should not be papered over:
*ruach elohim chayyim*, the spirit or wind of the living God, does sit inside
the count, and a reader who takes it as God's own spirit has one intra-divine
item in a list of ten. But one is not the pattern, and the text itself puts the
whole list under something outside it. Its only direct statement about the One
is a denial of any second, offered as the *reason* the series closes on itself:
the end is bound to the beginning "like the flame-tongue is bound to the coal",
"for the Lord is unique, He has no second — before one, what can you count?"
(1:7). The parallel line at 1:5 has "the Unique Lord EL, the faithful king"
governing all ten "from His Holy Abode". A God who governs the ten from
elsewhere is not being described *as* the ten.

So the later doctrine is a development of the vocabulary, not its original
sense, and the translator of the version read here says as much in refusing to
gloss this book's ten by the Zohar's. Two consequences follow for the arguments
this repository has already built. First, the internal-articulation reading of
the sefirot cannot claim the antiquity its vocabulary suggests: the word is old,
the intra-divine sense is not demonstrably so, and no note here may treat the
first appearance of the term as the first appearance of the doctrine. Second,
and cutting the other way, the earliest text of that vocabulary spends its one
theological sentence insisting that the Lord has no second — which is evidence
that counting ten of *something* in the divine order was never, in this
tradition, felt to be in tension with strict oneness. The tension the later
comparison with trinitarian doctrine turns on had to be introduced later, by
moving the ten inside.

What would settle how and when that move happened is the literature between the
two texts captured here — *Sefer ha-Bahir* is the obvious next thing to look
for — and this repository has captured none of it. Until it does, the claim above is a claim about two
captured texts and the distance between them, not a history of the doctrine.
