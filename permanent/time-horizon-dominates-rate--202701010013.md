Over long horizons the number of compounding periods matters more than the rate per period, because periods multiply while the rate only scales each step.

## Grounding

Added 2026-09-04. This note was one of six genesis fixtures carrying no source
at all, and it is not a leaf: it is joint-fourth by in-degree in this base, so
grounded notes were leaning on an ungrounded one. The source it needed was

Housel states the claim directly and as advice: "If you want to do better as an
investor, the single most powerful thing you can do is increase your time
horizon," and his Buffett case turns on duration rather than rate — "the real
key to his success is that he's been a phenomenal investor for three quarters of
a century"
([[housel-on-compounding-as-time-in-the-loop--202608311037]],
[[housel-the-psychology-of-money--202608311036]], ch. 4, from the verbatim
excerpt at raw/202608311119-housel-ch4-confounding-compounding-excerpt.txt).

Two honest qualifications, because a citation that makes a note look sourced
without supporting what it says is the failure mode these fixtures invite.

First, only half of this note is what Housel supports. The claim *that* horizon
dominates rate is his, on evidence. The *because* clause — that periods multiply
while the rate only scales each step — is arithmetic, and no source is cited for
it because none is needed: it is a property of exponentiation, not an empirical
finding. It is stated here as definitional rather than left looking unsupported.

Second, "dominates" is Housel's practical claim over investing horizons, not a
theorem. Any fixed horizon has a rate high enough to beat a longer one at a
lower rate; what the evidence supports is that over the horizons people actually
face, and with the rates actually available, duration is the lever with more
room in it. Housel's own grounding is a small verified excerpt of ch. 4 rather
than the full chapter, which is recorded in the literature note.
