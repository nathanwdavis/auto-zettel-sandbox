Over long horizons the number of compounding periods matters more than the rate per period, because periods multiply while the rate only scales each step.

Grounded 2026-09-03, having sat uncited since genesis while carrying the
joint-fourth-highest in-degree in the base — a hub nothing supported.
[[housel-on-compounding-as-time-in-the-loop--202608311037]] states the same
claim from a source already verified in this base, and states it about as
plainly as it can be stated: "If you want to do better as an investor, the
single most powerful thing you can do is increase your time horizon" (Housel,
*The Psychology of Money*, ch. 4, quoted verbatim in that note's capture).

Two limits, so the citation is not read as more than it is. Housel is a
practitioner writing for a general audience, not a proof; what he supplies is
an independent statement of the claim and a worked case, not a derivation. And
the arithmetic half of this note — that periods multiply while the rate only
scales — is definitional and needs no source at all. The citation covers the
empirical half: that over the horizons people actually face, the multiplying
term is the one that decides the outcome.
