Bibliographic record. Benjamin Franklin's last will and testament (dated
July 17, 1788) together with the codicil of June 23, 1789, as printed in
the standard Smyth edition of Franklin's writings, vol. 10, pp. 493–509 —
the codicil (pp. 501–509) is the primary text establishing the two
200-year compound-interest loan funds for Boston and Philadelphia, and the
second primary source behind this base's "compound growth" topic after
Price 1772.

The raw capture is the contiguous Chapter XIII region of the Internet
Archive OCR plain text of the University of Toronto scan of Smyth vol. 10
(public domain; no access restriction), with running heads and page
numbers left in place so quotations can cite Smyth's pagination. The OCR
carries scattered character noise ("hi" for "in", "WELL" for "WILL" in
the chapter heading); quotations in notes built on this reference
normalize such artifacts only after cross-checking the quoted wording
word-for-word against an independent transcription (constitution.org's
"The Last Will and Testament of Benjamin Franklin") and, where the two
disagree, against the Internet Archive OCR of Jared Sparks's edition
(The Works of Benjamin Franklin, vol. I, 1856 printing, item
worksofbenjaminf01franiala) as a third witness. One divergence was found
and resolved this way: in the sentence directing repayments to be re-lent,
constitution.org drops the clause ", which sums of principal" (an
eye-skip); Smyth and Sparks agree on the fuller reading, which is the one
quoted. The work predates ISBN and carries no DOI, so verification rests
on the raw capture; edition, editor, publisher, place, and year are
transcribed from the captured volume's own title page.

The codicil itself names no source for the compounding scheme — it credits
no one, and any influence claim (e.g. Price, or Mathon de la Cour's
"Fortuné Ricard") would need a separate documented source; notes built on
this reference therefore assert none.
