Bibliographic record. The nine hundred theses Pico offered for public
disputation at Rome in 1486. (Peterson's editorial introduction to the base
text below reports that the disputation never took place and that Pico was
condemned by Innocent VIII, then absolved by Alexander VI; that is editorial
apparatus, outside the captured section, and is recorded here as his statement
rather than as a claim this repository has verified.) This repository cites one
section of the theses: the *Conclusiones cabalisticae numero LXXI, secundum
opinionem propriam,
ex ipsis Hebreorum sapientum fundamentis Cristianam Religionem maxime
confirmantes* -- the section in which Pico turns kabbalistic method to
Christian apologetic ends. (It is standardly described as the founding
document of Christian kabbalism; that priority claim is not verified from any
source captured here, and no note in this repository rests on it.) Tiered
`primary-text`: a 1486 work, in the public domain, cited in Pico's own words.

The capture is the complete cabalistic section, not an excerpt, taken from two
independent digitizations and set against each other thesis by thesis. The base
text is Joseph H. Peterson's digital edition of the whole *Conclusiones*
(Esoteric Archives, 2001, updated 2025-07-12), which is released under CC-BY
4.0 and so is explicitly reusable. The cross-check witness is the Pico Project
edition of Brown University's Center for Digital Scholarship with the
University of Bologna -- a diplomatic transcription of the 1486 editio princeps
that keeps the original line breaks and leaves the Hebrew words as gaps. Brown
carries a notice that the collection is a no-longer-maintained web archive; it
resolves over https and was fetched live for this capture.

What the collation found is recorded in the capture header rather than
smoothed away, because it bears on how far either edition may be trusted alone.
After normalising for orthography only, 21 of the 71 theses Peterson prints are
word-for-word identical to Brown and the other 50 differ only in single words
or word division. But Peterson omits thesis 27 outright, so his edition holds
71 theses under numbers running to 72; and Peterson carries scanning slips
Brown does not (`signicat` for `significat`, `Quillbet` for `Quilibet`, `suit`
for `fuit`). Brown is accordingly the better witness to the wording, Peterson
the better licensed and the only one supplying the Hebrew. Every phrase this
repository quotes from Pico is attested by BOTH editions, and the capture's
Section C prints the two side by side for the theses actually quoted.

One curiosity belongs to the 1486 text itself and not to either editor: both
editions head the section "numero LXXI" (71) while numbering the theses to 72.

Open Library corroborates the work, its title and its 1486 Rome date at
`OL1456981W` (the record is for Kieszkowski's 1973 critical edition). Its
`author_name` there reads "Giovanni Francesco Pico della Mirandola", conflating
Giovanni (1463-1494) with his nephew Gianfrancesco; the author of the
*Conclusiones* is Giovanni, and this record does not propagate Open Library's
error.
