## 2026-10-08

Answered 2026-10-08. Yes, on two writers' terms, by two routes. Both Irving and Meyendorff give Christ a fallen human nature and deny that he is a human person, so guilt, which belongs to persons, has no subject in him (Irving credits Christ's actual sinlessness to the Spirit). Meyendorff denies inherited guilt to everyone (what passes from Adam is mortality), so Christ needs no exemption; Irving keeps original sin passing by ordinary generation and blocks it at the manner of Christ's conception, bearing our guilt only by imputation. They differ on what 'fallen' contains: mortality and the innocent passions for Meyendorff (with John of Damascus), the evil propensities as well for Irving, which is where Westminster's 'corruption of nature' bites. Open: Meyendorff's 1987 SVTQ essay, the Greek fathers on Romans 5:12 in their own words (on their face Meyendorff and Sanday and Headlam place Theodoret on opposite construals), Maximus, and the modern literature (INBOX 2026-10-08). ## 2026-10-08

Added 2026-10-08, from an owner-supplied source: Grudem, Systematic Theology 2nd ed., ch. 26 sec. 4, 'Could Jesus Have Sinned?' (reconstructed excerpts on the Grudem reference). Grudem gives the evangelical foil: Christ's human nature taken by itself is like Adam and Eve's, free from sin but able to sin (permanent 202610081554); Christ could not have sinned because a sin would have been the divine person's, where Irving holds a Christ not liable to sin would not be a man (202610081555); and Grudem has Christ's human nature resist temptation while depending on the Father and the Spirit, where Irving has the Father and the Spirit restrain his fallen flesh and Meyendorff says he could overcome because he was God (202610081556).
