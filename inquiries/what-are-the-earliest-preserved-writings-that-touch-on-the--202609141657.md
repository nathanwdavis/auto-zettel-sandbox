What are the earliest preserved writings that touch on the debate in the Christian church between the doctrine of the sovereignty of God and the doctrine of human free will?

## 2026-09-14

**Answered 2026-09-14.** Four primary works captured and verified, each in its
public-domain nineteenth-century English translation (ANF/NPNF via New Advent):
Justin's *First Apology* 43-44 (c. 155), Irenaeus' *Against Heresies* IV.37
(c. 180), Origen's *De Principiis* III.1 (c. 229) and Augustine's *De gratia et
libero arbitrio* 1 and 6 (c. 426).

The finding that matters is that "earliest" has three answers, because the early
texts are not arranged as the later debate is. Free will is argued explicitly
from Justin onward, but against Stoic/astrological fate and the Valentinian
doctrine of fixed natures; divine sovereignty is a premise those writers share
with their readers, not the position across from them. Origen is the hinge: his
chapter title concedes that the threat has moved inside Scripture, and he is the
first to assemble Pharaoh, Ezekiel's stony heart and Romans 9 as a body of
objections needing systematic answer. The dispute between *Christian* parties is
first documented at Hadrumetum in 426, where Augustine answers monks who read
his own doctrine of grace as a denial of freedom.

Two forward links into existing base material were made rather than left
implicit: Justin states and denies the foreknowledge-entails-fixity inference
that organises the Grudem/Ware/open-theism dispute, and Origen's sun-melting-wax
answer to Pharaoh's hardening is structurally Ware's occasioning-not-causing
asymmetry, though reached inside a libertarian rather than a compatibilist
account of the will.

**What is not on file, in rough order of value.** Tertullian, *Adversus
Marcionem* II.5-8 and *De Anima* 21, which carry the Latin *liberum arbitrium*
vocabulary the West inherits; Augustine's *Ad Simplicianum* (396), standardly
given as where his own position turned, and *De praedestinatione sanctorum* /
*De dono perseverantiae* (428-429), the semi-Pelagian phase; Pelagius' own
surviving writings (the *Letter to Demetrias*, the *De natura* fragments), so
that both parties need not be described from Augustine's report; Methodius,
*De Autexousio* (c. 300), a whole treatise on the title question; Clement of
Alexandria's *Stromateis*; the canons of the Second Council of Orange (529),
which close the controversy in the West. The New Testament material both sides
quote — Romans 9 above all — is earlier than any of these and is deliberately
not treated here as a witness to the debate, being the disputed text rather
than a party to it; whether the base should hold it as a reference in its own
right is a question for the owner.
