**Answer (2026-09-03):** Partly, and not in the direction the question
assumed. The first sustained Jewish critique to engage the point --- Leon
Modena's *Ari Nohem* (Venice, 1639) --- did not deny that the sefirot resemble
the Trinity. It granted the resemblance and used it as a charge: Modena
"leveled a severe countercharge of his own: after an examination of the sefirot
as a concept, he concluded that it pointed to a plurality within God similar to
the Christian doctrine of the Trinity." That reply is available to him because
he is attacking Kabbalah, not defending it --- the same premise serves the
missionary and the anti-kabbalist, who draw opposite conclusions from it. The
setting is captured too: Christians "drew on Kabbalah when preaching to
potential Jewish converts", and the appropriation Pico began "was hardly a
disinterested intellectual exchange", being "fraught with conversionary
pressures and uneven power relations".

Answered to the extent one grounded source allows, and no further. Three limits,
all recorded in the notes rather than left implicit. (1) The source is Dweck's
scholarly report; *Ari Nohem* itself is not in this repository, in Hebrew or in
translation, so nothing here establishes HOW Modena argued from the sefirot to
the parallel. (2) Modena is a hostile witness to the doctrine he describes and
an anti-kabbalist rather than a kabbalist, so the reply of someone DEFENDING
the sefirot against Pico is still uncaptured --- the dispute is no longer
one-sided, but it is still not represented from the side Pico was arguing
against. (3) Dweck names Pico as the setting and does not say Modena answered
him by name. All three are filed in INBOX with the routes to close them.
