# Fallen Nature Without Original Guilt: Irving and Meyendorff — Paper Outline

## What this document is

This is the outline for a paper of about 2,500 words, roughly eight to nine pages
double-spaced, for a Systematic Theology class. It explores one claim about the person of
Christ: that the Son of God took a *fallen* human nature, the same nature every child of
Adam has, without inheriting original *guilt*. It uses two writers who hold versions of the
claim from opposite ends of Christendom: Edward Irving, a pastor whose sermons on the incarnation
carry a dedication dated 1828, and John Meyendorff, an Orthodox theologian at St
Vladimir's Seminary, writing in 1974 and 1989. The outline assumes Turabian
notes-bibliography citations; check your syllabus.

## The terms this outline uses, each defined once

- **Fallen nature** (or fallen humanity, fallen flesh) means human nature in the condition
  it has had since Adam's sin, as opposed to the condition Adam had before it. The whole
  paper turns on the fact that writers who use this phrase do not all put the same things
  into that condition.
- **Original sin**, in the Westminster standards, means the sinfulness of the estate into
  which humanity fell. The Larger Catechism (Q. 25) lists three parts: "the guilt of Adam's
  first sin", the want of the righteousness Adam was created with, and "the corruption of
  his nature", by which a person is "wholly inclined to all evil" [repo].
- **Original guilt** is the first of those three parts: Adam's guilt counted against each of
  his descendants. The view this paper explores denies that Christ had it.
- **Corruption** has two senses, and the paper must keep them apart. In the Westminster
  sense it is *moral*: an inclination to evil. In the Greek sense (*phthora*) that John of
  Damascus and Meyendorff use, it is *physical*: liability to suffering, death and decay.
- **Innocent (or blameless) passions** are John of Damascus's name for the weaknesses that
  came into human life after the fall but are not sins: hunger, thirst, weariness, tears,
  fear of death, agony.
- **Person** and **hypostasis** name the one who acts, wills and is responsible, as opposed to
  the *nature* that person has. Chalcedonian Christology holds that Christ is one person, the
  eternal Son, with two natures. In later dogmatic vocabulary, the claim that Christ's human
  nature has no human person of its own is called *anhypostasia*; neither Irving nor
  Meyendorff uses that word in the pages on file, but both make the claim.
- **Imputation**, in Irving, means that the sins and guilt of others are laid on Christ and
  he is treated as guilty for their sake, though they are not his own.
- **Aphthartodocetism** is the sixth-century view of Julian of Halicarnassus that Christ's
  body was incorruptible from conception, because he never sinned. The church rejected it.
- ***Eph' hō*** is the four-word Greek phrase at the end of Romans 5:12, rendered 'in whom all have sinned' by Augustine's Latin and "for that all have sinned" by the KJV.

## The thesis, in one paragraph

The claim that Christ took a fallen human nature without original guilt is coherent once two
distinctions are drawn. The first is between *nature* and *person*: fallenness belongs to a
nature, but sin and guilt belong to a person, and the only person in Christ is the eternal
Son. Irving and Meyendorff both draw this distinction, and both rest Christ's freedom from
sin on it. The second is between the two senses of *corruption*. Meyendorff's fallen nature
is corrupt only in the physical sense: mortal, passible, tempted, but with no inclination to
evil, and on his account nobody inherits Adam's guilt in the first place, so Christ needs no
special exemption. Irving's fallen nature is corrupt in the moral sense too, carrying "the
evil propensities of the fallen manhood", and since Irving keeps a doctrine of original sin
that passes from Adam he has to explain how Christ escaped it. The paper argues that
Meyendorff's version of the view is the stronger, because it can say "fallen" without saying
'inclined to evil' and 'without original guilt' without inventing an exemption, and that
Irving's version stands or falls on whether an inclination to evil can belong to a nature
without belonging to the person whose nature it is.

If your professor wants a descriptive paper rather than an argued one, keep everything up to
the word "Irving's" and replace the last two sentences with: "The paper sets out how each
writer reaches the claim and where each meets the standard objections."

## Section I. Introduction (about 250 words)

1. Open with the two texts that create the problem. Christ "was in all points tempted like
   as we are, yet without sin" (Heb 4:15), and God sent his Son "in the likeness of sinful
   flesh" (Rom 8:3). He had to be made like his brethren "in all things" (Heb 2:17), and he is
   "holy, harmless, undefiled, separate from sinners" (Heb 7:26). [repo]
   Source: [Scripture predicates sinlessness of Christ's condition from birth, and calls his likeness to sinners a likeness](../permanent/scripture-predicates-sinlessness-of-christ-s-condition-from--202610031831.md).
2. State the two usual answers. The mainstream Western answer is that Christ took the
   *penalties* of the fall (weariness, suffering, mortality, real temptation) but not its
   disordered inclination, and inherited no original sin. Aquinas, for example, keeps
   passibility and mortality in Christ and denies him the *fomes*, "an inclination of the
   sensual appetite to what is contrary to reason". [repo]
   Source: [On the mainstream account Christ took the penalties of the fall but not its disordered inclination](../permanent/christ-shared-the-penalties-of-the-fall-but-not-its--202610031827.md).
   The minority answer is that he took a fallen nature. Name its best-known modern
   advocates, Karl Barth and T. F. Torrance, but say that this paper works from two earlier
   and less-read sources instead. [Barth and Torrance: not read]
3. Say what the paper will do: show that the minority answer can be held without saying that
   Christ inherited original guilt, by looking at how Irving and Meyendorff each do it, and
   then test both against the objections.

## Section II. Why the claim sounds contradictory: the Western frame (about 300 words)

The reader needs to see first why 'fallen but not guilty' sounds like nonsense to anyone
raised on the Westminster standards.

1. **In Westminster's terms the fallen estate includes guilt.** The Larger Catechism puts
   "the guilt of Adam's first sin" first in its list of what makes the fallen estate sinful,
   and says original sin is conveyed "by natural generation, so as all that proceed from
   them in that way are conceived and born in sin" (Q. 25-26). On those terms, to share the
   fallen estate is to share its guilt. [repo]
   Source: [The Larger Catechism defines sin as want of conformity or transgression and calls the corrupted estate original sin](../literature/the-larger-catechism-defines-sin-as-want-of-conformity-or--202610032109.md).
2. **The Western tradition therefore exempts Christ from the inheritance altogether, and
   argues about how.** Augustine and Aquinas locate the exemption in the manner of Christ's
   conception; Calvin says Christ was pure "because he was sanctified by the Spirit"; the
   Larger Catechism names both without ranking them. [repo]
   Source: [Augustine and Aquinas block original sin at the manner of Christ's conception, Calvin at the Spirit's sanctifying of it](../permanent/augustine-and-aquinas-block-original-sin-at-the-manner-of--202610031828.md).
3. **Nothing about this makes Christ less than human.** The tradition counts sin out of human
   nature: Adam before the fall was fully human and sinless. So the Western answer does not
   need a fallen nature to make Christ one of us. [repo]
   Source: [The tradition counts sin out of human nature, so a humanity without original sin is complete, not reduced](../permanent/the-tradition-counts-sin-out-of-human-nature-so-a-humanity--202610031826.md).
4. Close the section with the question the rest of the paper answers: can the fall's effects
   on human *nature* be separated from Adam's *guilt*, so that Christ shares the first without
   the second?

## Section III. Irving: fallen flesh, no original sin (about 550 words)

Irving's *Sermons on the Incarnation* (Collected Writings, vol. 5) [repo]. Use the 1866
edition's page numbers, which are the ones the repository's notes give.

1. **Christ took fallen flesh because there was no other.** Irving argues "That Christ took
   our fallen nature" and that this "is most manifest, because there was no other in existence
   to take" (pp. 115-16). Christ was of "the seed of the woman after she fell, and not before
   she fell". The opposite view he calls "a virtual denial of His humanity".
   Source: [Irving holds that Christ took fallen flesh with the evil propensities of fallen man, which the indwelling Spirit restrained](../literature/irving-holds-that-christ-took-fallen-flesh-and-was-kept--202610031823.md).
2. **The flesh carried the inclination itself, and the Spirit restrained it.** Irving will
   maintain "that Christ's flesh was as rebellious as ours, as fallen as ours", and credits
   the Holy Ghost with "subduing, restraining, conquering, the evil propensities of the fallen
   manhood" (pp. 169-70). Christ was holy throughout, "seeing wickedness consisteth not in
   being tempted, but in yielding to the temptation" (p. 127). The whole dispute, he says, is
   "Whether Christ's flesh had the grace of sinlessness and incorruption from its proper
   nature, or from the indwelling of the Holy Ghost. I say the latter" (appendix, after p. 566).
   Source: the same literature note, and [Irving gave Christ's flesh the evil propensities of fallen man and credited the Spirit with restraining them](../permanent/irving-s-fallen-flesh-christology-disputes-the-source-of--202610031830.md).
3. **Original sin is avoided by the manner of conception.** This is the passage your paper
   most needs, and it is the one most summaries of Irving leave out: "You have original sin
   taken away in Him by the manner of His conception. He is not, as it were, an individual of
   the sinful individuals; He is not a human person; He never had personal subsistence as a
   mere man". He took part with "the fallen children", but "not through connexion with Adam,
   but by His own free will, and His Father's free will, and the free will of the Holy Ghost;
   and thus original sin is avoided, though yet the body He took is in the fallen state, and
   liable to all temptations" (pp. 159-60). The body was formed "not by ordinary generation"
   but by a creative act.
   Source: [Irving holds that original sin is kept from Christ by the manner of his conception](../literature/irving-holds-that-original-sin-is-kept-from-christ-by-the--202610081436.md).
4. **The only guilt on Christ is ours, carried for us.** Irving does put guilt on Christ, but
   as imputation: "His humiliation and suffering were not for anything which He had done, but
   for what mankind in Adam had done", and in covenant language the Father "imputed it to Him;
   treated Him as the guilty one" (pp. 153-54). In the same sense "all the infirmities, sin,
   and guilt, of all flesh was gathered into one" in Christ's flesh, which is how Irving reads
   Romans 8:3: "not in the likeness of sinless flesh, but in the likeness of sinful flesh"
   (p. 174). Make the distinction explicit in the paper: *original* guilt (Adam's guilt as
   Christ's own) is denied; *imputed* guilt (our guilt borne by Christ as substitute) is
   affirmed, as every substitutionary account affirms it.
   Source: the same literature note.
5. **Summarise Irving in one sentence**: the flesh is fallen, inclination included; the
   person is the Son, not a human person; original sin, which for Irving passes by ordinary
   generation from Adam to human persons, therefore has nowhere to land.
6. A historical note, if you want one: Irving was deposed from the ministry of the Church of
   Scotland in 1833 over this teaching. [not on file: confirm from a biography, for example
   Margaret Oliphant's *Life of Edward Irving* (1862), before you state it]

## Section IV. Meyendorff: inherited mortality, not inherited guilt (about 550 words)

Two texts: *New Life in Christ: Salvation in Orthodox Theology* (Theological Studies, 1989)
[repo, excerpts: pp. 490, 492-95] and *Byzantine Theology* (1974; 2nd ed. 1979), pp. 143-46
[repo, excerpts: fourteen sentences]. Supplement with John of Damascus, *Exact Exposition of
the Orthodox Faith* III [repo].

1. **Sin and guilt belong to persons, not to natures.** "Now, in Greek patristic thought,
   only this free, personal mind can commit sin and incur the concomitant "guilt"", and sin
   is "never an act of nature". So Adam's rebellion "could be conceived only as their
   personal sin; there would be no place, then, in such an anthropology for the concept of
   inherited guilt, or for a "sin of nature," although it admits that human nature incurs the
   consequences of Adam's sin" (*Byzantine Theology*).
   Source: [Meyendorff finds a Greek patristic consensus that the inheritance of the Fall is mortality rather than sinfulness](../literature/meyendorff-finds-a-greek-patristic-consensus-that-the--202610081438.md).
2. **What is inherited is mortality.** "There is indeed a consensus in Greek patristic and
   Byzantine traditions in identifying the inheritance of the Fall as an inheritance
   essentially of mortality rather than of sinfulness, sinfulness being merely a consequence
   of mortality" (*Byzantine Theology*). In 1989: "What is being transmitted from parents to
   children is not sin but mortality and slavery", and Eastern patristic thought "ignores the
   notion of a transmission" of guilt from Adam, though "it does not ignore the very fact of
   cosmic fallenness" (p. 493).
   Source: [Meyendorff holds that Eastern patristic thought knows no transmission of guilt from Adam, only of mortality](../literature/meyendorff-holds-that-eastern-patristic-thought-knows-no--202610081437.md).
3. **The exegetical root is Romans 5:12.** The Latin *in quo omnes peccaverunt* was "used in
   the West to justify the doctrine of guilt inherited from Adam", "But such a meaning cannot
   be drawn from the original Greek". Meyendorff gives two readings the Greek allows: as
   "because", the verse "defines death as the punishment for individual sins of any human
   (not "original" sin)"; with "death" as antecedent, death "becomes the cause of individual
   human sins" (1989, n. 45). Either way, Adam's guilt is not in the clause.
   Source: [Meyendorff's two Greek readings of Romans 5:12 both keep Adam's guilt out of the clause](../permanent/meyendorff-s-two-greek-readings-of-romans-5-12-both-keep--202610081443.md),
   and for the Greek itself, [In the Greek, Romans 5:12's last clause names no one](../permanent/in-the-greek-romans-5-12-s-last-clause-names-no-one-so-in--202610061559.md).
4. **Christ assumed exactly that inheritance.** "It is the mortal, corruptible, and fallen
   humanity which was assumed by the Logos" (p. 494). God "willed precisely that, as man,
   Jesus, since his conception in the womb of Mary, would be fully conditioned by what our
   human, fallen existence is: he lived in time, "grew in wisdom," did not know, suffered, and
   died"; "mortality and corruptibility were assumed by the Son of God from the beginning of
   his human life" (p. 494 and n. 47).
   Source: the 1989 literature note above.
5. **The boundary on the other side is Julian of Halicarnassus.** Julian argued that "since
   death and corruption (phthora) are consequences of human sin, they could not have been
   present in Jesus, who did not sin" (p. 494). The church rejected this. John of Damascus
   explains why with his two senses of corruption: Christ's body was corruptible in the sense
   of "all the human sufferings, such as hunger, thirst, weariness, the piercing with nails,
   death", but never in the sense of "the complete resolution of the body into its constituent
   elements" (III.28). He also says Christ "assumed all the natural and innocent passions of
   man", the ones that "have entered into the life of man owing to the condemnation by reason
   of the transgression", "save sin" (III.20).
   Source: [John of Damascus and Meyendorff both reject Julian of Halicarnassus's incorruptible Christ](../permanent/john-of-damascus-and-meyendorff-both-reject-julian-of--202610081444.md)
   and [John of Damascus has Christ assume the natural and innocent passions](../literature/john-of-damascus-has-christ-assume-the-natural-and-innocent--202610081439.md).
6. **Why Christ did not sin.** "Christ was not a human hypostasis" (p. 492). Sin is "a
   conscious, personal act of rebellion against divine will", and "It is because he was God,
   not as "mere man," that Jesus was able to overcome the temptations inherent in fallen
   humanity" (p. 495).
7. **Summarise Meyendorff in one sentence**: no one inherits Adam's guilt; everyone inherits
   mortality; Christ took that mortal, passible, tempted humanity, and the person who lived
   in it was the Son.

## Section V. One move, two contents (about 400 words)

This is the analytical heart of the paper. Make three points, one paragraph each.

1. **The shared move: fallenness belongs to the nature, guilt to the person.** Set Irving's
   "He is not a human person" beside Meyendorff's "Christ was not a human hypostasis". Both
   writers deny that Christ's humanity has a human person of its own, and both rest his
   freedom from sin on that denial. This is what makes 'fallen but not guilty' more than a
   contradiction: if guilt attaches to persons and the only person in Christ is the Son, a
   fallen nature does not produce a guilty subject.
   Source: [Irving and Meyendorff both pair a fallen human nature in Christ with the denial that he is a human person](../permanent/irving-and-meyendorff-both-pair-a-fallen-human-nature-in--202610081440.md).
2. **Two routes to 'no original guilt'.** Meyendorff needs no exemption, because nobody
   inherits guilt; Christ born of Mary inherits what every child inherits, which is mortality.
   Irving keeps original sin passing from Adam, so he has to block it, and he blocks it where
   Augustine and Aquinas do, at "the manner of His conception". The difference is that they
   exempt the flesh as well and Irving exempts only the person.
   Source: [Meyendorff needs no special means to keep inherited guilt from Christ](../permanent/meyendorff-needs-no-special-means-to-keep-inherited-guilt--202610081441.md).
3. **Two contents for "fallen".** Meyendorff's fallen humanity is mortal, corruptible and
   passible, the same list the mainstream Western account calls the penalties of the fall;
   nothing in the Meyendorff pages on file puts an inclination to evil in Christ's flesh.
   Irving's fallen flesh carries "the evil propensities". So the two writers share a word
   more than a claim, and the Orthodox form of the view is closer in substance to Aquinas
   than to Irving.
   Source: [Meyendorff's fallen humanity is the mortal, corruptible condition, while Irving's fallen flesh also carries the evil propensities](../permanent/meyendorff-s-fallen-humanity-is-the-mortal-corruptible--202610081442.md).

## Section VI. Objections and replies (about 350 words)

Take four. Give each one sentence of objection and two or three of reply.

1. **"A fallen nature *is* original sin."** The Larger Catechism counts "the corruption of his
   nature" as part of what is "commonly called original sin". Reply: in Westminster
   "corruption" is moral ("wholly inclined to all evil"); in Meyendorff and John of Damascus
   it is physical (*phthora*). Meyendorff's version therefore meets Westminster at the
   *guilt* item, which it denies for everyone, not at the corruption item. Irving's version
   does meet it at the corruption item, and his only answer is that the inclination is in the
   flesh and never in the one who wills. Say plainly that this is where Irving's view is
   weakest.
   Source: [The Larger Catechism's corruption of nature is an inclination to all evil and Meyendorff's corruptibility is liability to death](../permanent/the-larger-catechism-s-corruption-of-nature-is-an--202610081445.md).
2. **"Romans 5:12 says all sinned in Adam."** Reply: the Greek clause names no one, and both
   of Meyendorff's readings exclude inherited guilt. But do not lean on his preferred reading,
   "because of death": Sanday and Headlam (1905) list it and call it "even more impossible"
   than "in whom", and they place Theodoret on the "because" side where Meyendorff places him
   on the "death" side. The safe claim is the weaker one: the verse does not *state* inherited
   guilt.
   Source: [Meyendorff's two Greek readings of Romans 5:12](../permanent/meyendorff-s-two-greek-readings-of-romans-5-12-both-keep--202610081443.md).
3. **"Gregory of Nazianzus settled this: what is not assumed is not healed."** This is the
   proof text fallen-nature writers usually cite, and you should *not* rest the paper on it.
   In its own letter the maxim is aimed at a Christ "without a human mind". It proves that
   Christ's humanity is complete, which both sides affirm, not that it was fallen. [repo]
   Source: [Gregory's maxim that the unassumed is unhealed was aimed at a Christ without a human mind](../literature/gregory-s-maxim-that-the-unassumed-is-unhealed-was-aimed-at--202610031816.md)
   and the closing paragraph of the Irving permanent note linked in Section III.
4. **"If Christ is sinless only because he is God, his temptation was not real."** This is
   the hardest objection and neither writer fully answers it. Irving puts the victory in the
   Spirit's work in Christ's flesh; Meyendorff says Christ overcame the temptations "because he
   was God". Note that Calvin also credits the Spirit, so Irving is not alone on the
   mechanism. Acknowledge the objection, and say that the paper's claim concerns guilt, not
   impeccability, which is a separate question.

## Section VII. Conclusion (about 150 words)

1. Restate the two distinctions: nature and person; moral and physical corruption.
2. Restate the result: with both distinctions in place, 'fallen nature without original guilt' is coherent. Meyendorff's version holds without strain. Irving's version needs the
   further claim that an inclination to evil can belong to a nature without belonging to the
   person who has it.
3. End on Hebrews 2:17 and 4:15: the paper's view is one way of taking "in all things ...
   like unto his brethren" with full seriousness while keeping "yet without sin".

## If your professor wants you to take a side

The outline already takes one: Meyendorff's form of the view over Irving's. If you want to
defend Irving instead, your argument has to be that the Larger Catechism's "corruption of
his nature" is a property of persons ("whereby *he* is ... wholly inclined to all evil"), so a
nature without a human person can carry the flesh's propensities without being "inclined"
in the catechism's sense. That is a real argument, but it is the paper's hardest move, and
you would need Irving's appendix and *The Orthodox and Catholic Doctrine of Our Lord's Human
Nature* (1830) to make it [not read].

  between them, "Christ was not 'passionate' in a sinful sense" ... "but became subject to
  the 'irreproachable passions'" (p. 29 n. 39). Kimel's quotations of the
  1989 article match the captured text exactly, which is a good sign, but check these against
  the journal before you use them. This is the single most useful source the repository lacks.
- John Meyendorff, *Christ in Eastern Christian Thought* (first published in English 1969;
  St. Vladimir's Seminary Press edition 1975), p. 149 in the edition Kimel cites, on Maximus
  the Confessor's "gnomic will" and why Christ had none; known only from Kimel's post.

**Correctly cited but not read [not read].**

- Oliver D. Crisp, "Did Christ Have a *Fallen* Human Nature?," *International Journal of
