# Divine Sovereignty and Human Free Will: Paper Outline

## What this document is

This is the outline for a paper of about four pages, double-spaced, which comes to roughly
1,100 to 1,300 words, for an evangelical Systematic Theology class. The outline assumes
Turabian notes-bibliography citations, which is the usual seminary default; check the
syllabus in case your professor wants something else.

This version is dated 2026-09-10. It is written in plain English and full sentences so that
nothing has to be guessed at. Its aim is descriptive: it states, as precisely as the sources
allow, where Wayne Grudem's view and Luis de Molina's view agree and where they differ. It
does not try to add a new argument to the debate. Two earlier versions of this outline, a
broad survey of every position and a version framed as a debate with a verdict, are in this
file's git history and can be mined if you want them.

**How to use the links.** Every time a point rests on a source, the outline says so in a
sentence and gives a link to the note in this repository that holds the evidence. Each note
begins with a "Grounding" line that names the file in the `raw/` folder holding the actual
captured text. When you quote in the paper, quote from that captured text, not from the note.

## The terms this outline uses, each defined once

- **Sovereignty** means that God rules over everything that happens, so that nothing takes
  place outside his will and control.
- **Providence** is the doctrine that describes how God's rule actually works: he keeps
  created things in existence, he works with them in everything they do, and he directs them
  to the ends he has chosen. Grudem calls these three parts preservation, concurrence, and
  government.
- **The decree** is God's eternal plan. Grudem defines the decrees of God as "the eternal
  plans of God whereby, before the creation of the world, he determined to bring about
  everything that happens."
- **Concurrence** is Grudem's name for the second part of providence: God cooperates with
  every created thing in every action, so that every event is fully caused by God and, at the
  same time, fully caused by the creature. In this view God is the **primary cause**, the
  unseen cause that plans and initiates everything, and the creature is the **secondary
  cause**, the cause we can actually observe.
- **Foreknowledge** is God's knowledge of what is going to happen before it happens. Both
  views in this paper affirm that God's foreknowledge is complete.
- **Compatibilist freedom**, which Grudem prefers to call "freedom of inclination," means
  that a person is free when he does what he most wants to do, even if God has ultimately
  determined what he wants. Under this definition, a choice can be both determined by God
  and genuinely the person's own.
- **Libertarian freedom** means that a person, in exactly the same circumstances, could
  really have chosen otherwise. Under this definition, a choice that God has determined is
  not free.
- **Middle knowledge** is Molina's term for God's knowledge of what every free creature
  *would* do in any set of circumstances God might place it in. It is called "middle" because
  it sits between two other kinds of knowledge the older theologians already recognized.
- **Natural knowledge** is what God knows simply by knowing his own nature and power: every
  necessary truth and everything that could possibly happen.
- **Free knowledge** is what God knows once he has decided what to create: everything that
  will actually happen. It is called "free" because it depends on God's free decision.
- A **counterfactual of creaturely freedom** is a statement of this form: if this person
  were in this situation, he would freely do this. Middle knowledge is God's knowledge of
  all the true statements of that form.
- **The grounding objection** is the standard objection to middle knowledge. It asks what
  makes such a statement true before the person exists and before God has decided anything.

## The thesis, in one paragraph

God's sovereign will and human free will coexist, and both are true doctrines. Wayne Grudem
and Luis de Molina both hold this. They affirm the same four things: that God knows
everything that will happen, that God's providence reaches every event, that God is never
the author of sin, and that people make real choices for which they are responsible. What
separates them is not whether both doctrines are true but how the two fit together, and the
difference comes down to the order in which God's knowing and the creature's choosing are
arranged. Grudem puts God's decree first and has God's knowledge follow it. Molina puts the
creature's would-be choice first and has God's knowledge follow that, with God's decree
coming after. The paper's job is to state that difference clearly, and to show how small it
is against the size of what the two views share.

## Section I. Introduction (about 150 words)

1. Open with Acts 2:23, which puts both truths in one sentence: Jesus was delivered up
   according to the "definite plan and foreknowledge of God" and was killed "by the hands of
   lawless men." Say that the paper will compare two evangelical-compatible explanations of
   how both halves of that sentence can be true at once.
   Source: Grudem uses this verse in the section of chapter 13 that defines God's will. The
   repository's note on that section is [Grudem splits God's will into necessary and free, then secret and revealed](../literature/grudem-splits-gods-will-into-necessary-free-secret-and-revealed--202609081904.md).
2. Name the two views. The first is Grudem's doctrine of concurrence, from chapter 16 of his
   *Systematic Theology*, which is the Reformed position. The second is Molina's doctrine of
   middle knowledge, from his 1588 work the *Concordia*, which is defended today inside
   evangelicalism by William Lane Craig and Kenneth Keathley.
   Source for Grudem: [Concurrence is the mechanism the wisdom section assumed](../permanent/concurrence-is-the-mechanism-the-wisdom-section-assumed--202609081826.md).
   Source for Molina: [Molina distinguishes three knowledges in God](../literature/molina-distinguishes-three-knowledges-and-makes-the-middle-one-depend-on-the-creature--202609090055.md).
   Craig and Keathley are cited from memory at book level and have not been checked; see the
   source list at the end.
3. State the thesis in the form given above, and say in one sentence what the paper will do:
   first show what the two views share, then describe each view on its own terms, then state
   the differences one at a time.
4. Optionally, note that Grudem himself classifies Molinism as "a third Arminian viewpoint,"
   but that he does so because of how it explains salvation, not because it denies God's
   sovereignty. This matters because a reader who knows Grudem's chapter may assume Molina is
   on the low-sovereignty side, and he is not.
   Source: [Grudem answers the Arminian by comparing unanswered questions](../literature/grudem-answers-the-arminian-by-comparing-unanswered-questions--202609081825.md).

## Section II. What the two views share (about 220 words)

This section establishes that the thesis is common ground. Give one witness from each side
for each of the four agreements.

1. **Both affirm that God knows everything that will happen, and both prove it from
   prophecy.** Grudem rests his case on Isaiah 40 to 48, where God makes his ability to
   declare the future the test that distinguishes him from false gods. Molina, one paragraph
   before he defines middle knowledge, argues that if God did not know future free acts,
   prophecy would perish and most of Scripture would collapse with it.
   Source for Grudem: [Grudem answers open theism with Isaiah's test of deity](../permanent/grudem-answers-open-theism-with-isaiahs-test-of-deity-not-with-the-definition-of-omniscience--202609061921.md).
   Source for Molina: the sentence is on the scanned page of the *Concordia* that the
   repository holds as `raw/202609090050-molina-p317-leaf334.jpg`, directly above the
   transcribed passage. It has not been transcribed yet, so read it from the image before you
   cite it.
2. **Both affirm that God's providence reaches every event.** Grudem says that God "works
   all things according to the counsel of his will" and that no event falls outside his
   providence. Molina, in the repository's translation of his Latin, says that nothing is in
   the creature's power that is not also in God's power, and that God can bend our free will
   in any direction he chooses, with one exception, sin.
   Source for Grudem: [Concurrence is the mechanism the wisdom section assumed](../permanent/concurrence-is-the-mechanism-the-wisdom-section-assumed--202609081826.md).
   Source for Molina: [Molina's own list of what coheres keeps sovereignty whole](../permanent/molinas-own-list-of-what-coheres-keeps-sovereignty-whole--202609090102.md).
3. **Both deny that God is the author of sin.** Grudem, writing about Acts 2:23, says that
   "we must never understand it to imply that we are freed from responsibility for evil or
   that God is ever to be blamed for sin." Molina says that God is in no way the cause of
   sins, and that God may permit sin but never commands it or incites anyone to it.
   Source for Grudem: [Grudem splits God's will into necessary and free, then secret and revealed](../literature/grudem-splits-gods-will-into-necessary-free-secret-and-revealed--202609081904.md).
   Source for Molina: the same note as in point 2.
4. **Both affirm that people make real choices.** Grudem says that "we make willing choices,
   choices that have real effects." Molina says that a free will placed in any situation
   could, if it willed, do the opposite of what it does.
   Source for Grudem: [The freedom Grudem deferred is compatibilist freedom](../permanent/the-freedom-grudem-deferred-is-compatibilist-freedom--202609081827.md).
   Source for Molina: [Middle knowledge sits between natural and free knowledge](../permanent/middle-knowledge-sits-between-natural-and-free-knowledge-because-it-is-prevolitional-yet-contingent--202609090100.md).
5. Close the section by saying plainly what these four agreements prove: the thesis of the
   paper is not one side's claim against the other; it is what both sides hold, and the
   disagreement lies inside it.

## Section III. Grudem's view, stated on its own terms (about 220 words)

Describe the view in the order Grudem builds it, so that a reader can see each step.

1. God's decree comes first. Before creation, God determined everything that would happen.
   His knowledge of the future is knowledge of what he has decreed.
   Source: [Grudem defines providence as preservation, concurrence and government](../literature/grudem-defines-providence-as-preservation-concurrence-and-government--202609081824.md).
2. God carries out the decree through concurrence. Grudem's key sentence is that "in one
   sense events are fully (100 percent) caused by God and fully (100 percent) caused by the
   creature as well. However, divine and creaturely causes work in different ways." God is the
   primary cause, working invisibly behind every event, and the creature's own nature and
   choices are the secondary cause, which is what we observe. The two are not two shares of
   one cause; they are two different kinds of cause of the same event.
   Source: [Concurrence is the mechanism the wisdom section assumed](../permanent/concurrence-is-the-mechanism-the-wisdom-section-assumed--202609081826.md).
3. Human freedom, in this view, is freedom of inclination. Grudem borrows Bruce Ware's
   definition: "we are free when we choose according to our strongest inclination or deepest
   desire. In short, we are free when we do what we most want to do." He adds that this kind
   of freedom "does not rule out the idea that our choices may ultimately be determined by
   God in ways invisible to us." He knows the other definition, libertarian freedom, which he
   quotes as "the ability, all things being just what they are, to choose differently," and
   he rejects it, saying that Scripture nowhere describes us as free in the sense of being
   outside God's control or of making decisions that nothing causes.
   Source: [The freedom Grudem deferred is compatibilist freedom](../permanent/the-freedom-grudem-deferred-is-compatibilist-freedom--202609081827.md).
4. Grudem draws one consequence that matters for the comparison. He argues that if God
   knows a future choice, that choice is already fixed: "If our future choices are known,
   then they are fixed. And if they are fixed, then they are not 'free' in the Arminian sense
   (undetermined or uncaused)." He uses this against anyone who wants God to foreknow free
   choices without having ordained them.
   Source: [The foreknowledge entailment is a weapon in chapter 16](../permanent/the-foreknowledge-entailment-is-a-weapon-in-chapter-16--202609081829.md).
5. Grudem is candid about what his view cannot explain. He writes that "Exactly how God
   combines his providential control with our willing and significant choices, Scripture
   does not explain to us," and at the end of the chapter he names two questions his position
   cannot answer: "Exactly how can God cause us to choose something willingly?" and "Exactly
   how can God not be blamed for evil when he ordains that we do evil willingly?"
   Source: [The dispute is decided by which residue honours God more](../permanent/the-dispute-is-decided-by-which-residue-honours-god-more--202609081831.md).

## Section IV. Molina's view, stated on its own terms (about 220 words)

Describe the view in the order Molina builds it. Note for the reader that Molina wrote in
Latin, that the repository holds the Latin transcribed from the 1876 edition, and that the
English sentences below are the repository's own translations. If you quote Molina in the
paper, quote the Latin from the repository or use Alfred Freddoso's published translation,
which is listed in the sources.

1. Molina says there are three kinds of knowledge in God, and that unless we distinguish
   them we will go badly astray in reconciling human freedom with God's foreknowledge. The
   first is natural knowledge, which could not have been otherwise, by which God knows
   everything his power could bring about. The second is free knowledge, by which God knows,
   after the free act of his will, what will actually happen. The third is middle knowledge,
   by which God sees, in his own essence and by a comprehension of each free will that Molina
   himself calls inscrutable, what that will would do if it were placed in this or that or
   any of infinitely many possible orders of things, even though it could do the opposite.
   Source: [Molina distinguishes three knowledges in God](../literature/molina-distinguishes-three-knowledges-and-makes-the-middle-one-depend-on-the-creature--202609090055.md).
2. Middle knowledge comes before God's decision and does not depend on it. Asked whether it
   should be called free or natural, Molina answers that it is neither: it is not free,
   because it precedes every free act of God's will, and it was not in God's power to know
   anything by it other than what he in fact knew; and it is not natural, because if the
   creature had been going to choose otherwise, God would have known that instead.
   Source: [Middle knowledge sits between natural and free knowledge](../permanent/middle-knowledge-sits-between-natural-and-free-knowledge-because-it-is-prevolitional-yet-contingent--202609090100.md).
3. God uses middle knowledge to choose which order of things to create. Knowing what every
   free creature would do in each possible arrangement of circumstances, God chooses one
   arrangement and, in the same decision, decides to cooperate in this or that way with the
   free choices he foresees in it. Providence over free creatures then works by placement and
   response: God exercises a person by events, permits his failures, calls him, and helps
   him. The modern name for this is weak actualization: God brings about what he wants by
   placing free creatures in circumstances in which they will freely do it, rather than by
   causing the choice itself.
   Source for Molina: [Weak actualization claims meticulous providence without determination](../permanent/weak-actualization-claims-meticulous-providence-without-determination-and-concedes-infeasible-worlds--202609090103.md).
   Source for the modern vocabulary: [Laing defines middle knowledge as God's prevolitional knowledge of counterfactuals](../literature/laing-defines-middle-knowledge-as-prevolitional-knowledge-of-counterfactuals-of-freedom--202609090056.md).
4. Molina states the principle that runs his whole system in a marginal note: future
   contingent things are not future because God knows them; rather, God knows them because
   they will be. For free acts he says that God foreknows what a creature will do because the
   creature will freely do it, and not the other way around.
   Source: [Molina reverses the direction of dependence](../permanent/molina-reverses-the-direction-of-dependence-god-foreknows-the-free-act-because-the-creature-would-do-it--202609090101.md).
5. Molina is also candid about what his view leaves unexplained. The comprehension by which
   God knows what a will would do is, in his own word, inscrutable. The modern form of the
   difficulty is the grounding objection: nothing seems to make a statement about what a
   person would freely do true before that person exists, and the five standard Molinist
   replies each move the difficulty somewhere else rather than removing it.
   Source: [The grounding objection is Molinism's own unanswered question](../permanent/the-grounding-objection-is-molinisms-own-unanswered-question--202609090104.md).

## Section V. The differences, stated one at a time (about 300 words)

This is the heart of the paper. State each difference in two or three sentences, and say in
each case what the two views still share around it, because that is what makes the
difference subtle.

1. **They define freedom differently.** Grudem says a choice is free when it is what the
   person most wants, even if God determined the want. Molina says a choice is free only if
   the person could really have done the opposite in the same circumstances. Both agree that
   the choice is genuinely the person's and that the person is responsible for it.
   Sources: the Grudem note on freedom of inclination and the Molina note on the three
   knowledges, both linked in sections III and IV.
2. **They order God's knowing and God's willing differently.** For Grudem, God's decree
   comes first and God knows the future because he has decreed it. For Molina, God's
   knowledge of what each creature would freely do comes first, God's decision comes second,
   and God's knowledge of what will actually happen comes third. Both agree that God knows
   the entire future with certainty before any of it happens.
   Sources: [The foreknowledge entailment is a weapon in chapter 16](../permanent/the-foreknowledge-entailment-is-a-weapon-in-chapter-16--202609081829.md)
   and [Molina reverses the direction of dependence](../permanent/molina-reverses-the-direction-of-dependence-god-foreknows-the-free-act-because-the-creature-would-do-it--202609090101.md).
3. **They explain how God gets what he wants differently.** Grudem's God works inside every
   choice as its primary cause. Molina's God chooses the whole set of circumstances, knowing
   what each person would freely do in it, and then cooperates with the choice. Both agree
   that God gets exactly what he intends and that nothing happens outside his plan.
   Sources: the concurrence note in section III and the weak actualization note in section IV.
4. **They locate the limit on God's options differently.** For Grudem there is no limit
   other than God's own character; every possible world is open to him. For Molina, since
   middle knowledge is true before God decides and God has no control over its content, there
   may be arrangements of circumstances, and so possible worlds, that God cannot bring about.
   The modern survey of the doctrine concedes this in its own voice. Both agree that God is
   omnipotent, meaning that he can do everything consistent with his nature.
   Source: [Weak actualization claims meticulous providence without determination](../permanent/weak-actualization-claims-meticulous-providence-without-determination-and-concedes-infeasible-worlds--202609090103.md),
   including the sentence from the IEP survey that "there may be some possible worlds that
   God cannot actualize."
5. **They read the same verse in opposite directions.** Both cite Jesus' words about Tyre
   and Sidon in Matthew 11:21: "if the mighty works done in you had been done in Tyre and
   Sidon, they would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes." Grudem uses the verse to
   show that God knows all possibilities, and then, following John Frame, treats that
   knowledge as part of God's natural knowledge of his own power, so that a separate middle
   knowledge is unnecessary. Molina uses the same verse to show that God knows something that
   is more than a possibility: not merely that Tyre and Sidon could have repented, but that
   they would have. On his reading, a bare possibility would not have let Jesus say "they
   would have repented." Both agree that the verse is true and that God knew what Tyre and
   Sidon would have done.
   Source: [Grudem and Molina both argue from Tyre and Sidon](../permanent/grudem-and-molina-both-argue-from-tyre-and-sidon-and-read-it-in-opposite-directions--202609090105.md).
6. **They admit a mystery in different places.** Grudem admits that he cannot explain how
   God causes a choice that is still willing, or how God ordains evil acts without being to
   blame for them. Molina admits that the way God comprehends what a creature would freely do
   is inscrutable, and his modern defenders have not settled what makes such knowledge true.
   Both admit, then, that the point where God's knowing meets the creature's choosing is not
   fully explained; they differ on which side of that point the unexplained part sits.
   Sources: [The dispute is decided by which residue honours God more](../permanent/the-dispute-is-decided-by-which-residue-honours-god-more--202609081831.md)
   and [The grounding objection is Molinism's own unanswered question](../permanent/the-grounding-objection-is-molinisms-own-unanswered-question--202609090104.md).

A one-line summary the paper can use: the two views agree about what is true and disagree
about the order in which God's knowledge, God's decision, and the creature's choice stand to
one another.

If you want a table in the paper, this is the comparison in six rows.

| Question | Grudem | Molina |
|---|---|---|
| What makes a choice free? | The person does what he most wants, even if God determined the want. | The person could have done otherwise in the same circumstances. |
| What comes first, God's decree or God's knowledge of free choices? | The decree; God knows the future because he decreed it. | The knowledge of what creatures would freely do; the decree follows it. |
| How does God secure the outcome he wants? | By working inside every choice as its primary cause. | By choosing the circumstances, knowing what each person would freely do in them, and cooperating with the choice. |
| Is any possible world closed to God? | No. | Possibly yes, because middle knowledge is not under God's control. |
| What does Matthew 11:21 show? | That God knows all possibilities. | That God knows what would have happened, which is more than a possibility. |
| What is left unexplained? | How a choice God causes is still willing, and how God is not to blame for the evil he ordains. | How God knows what a will that does not yet exist would freely do, and what makes that knowledge true. |

## Section VI. Conclusion (about 90 words)

1. Restate the thesis: both doctrines are true, and both of the views examined hold both of
   them.
2. Restate the difference in one sentence: the views disagree only about the order in which
   God's knowledge, God's decision, and the creature's choice are arranged.
3. End on the point that both sides confess a mystery at the same joint. If you want a
   historical note, add that when the Catholic Church examined this very dispute between
   Molina's followers and their Dominican opponents, it ended the matter in 1607 by allowing
   each side to teach its view and forbidding either to condemn the other.
   Source for the historical note: [The Congregatio de Auxiliis ended by permitting both schools](../permanent/the-congregatio-de-auxiliis-ended-by-permitting-both-schools-and-condemning-neither--202609090106.md).

## If your professor wants you to take a side

The outline does not require a verdict, because the paper's aim is to state the difference
clearly. If a verdict is required, the shortest defensible one is this: Grudem's view keeps
every possible world open to God and puts the unexplained part in the manner of God's
causing; Molina's view keeps libertarian freedom and puts the unexplained part in the reach
of God's knowledge. Which cost is lower is a judgment, and Grudem himself admits that his
closing comparison of the two sets of unanswered questions is a judgment about which leaves
God greater, not a proof.
Source: [The dispute is decided by which residue honours God more](../permanent/the-dispute-is-decided-by-which-residue-honours-god-more--202609081831.md).
