Own-words summary of [[edward-irving-collected-writings-vol-5-sermons-on-the--202610031814]].

The second part of Irving's third sermon on the incarnation turns from the
composition of Christ's person to reconciliation, and on the way it says
what becomes of original sin in a Christ whose flesh is fallen.

First, imputation (pp. 153-54). Nothing constrained the Son: of his own free
will he took the body the Spirit quickened of the Virgin's substance, and in
it did no sin. "By imputation I understand, that His humiliation and
suffering were not for anything which He had done, but for what mankind in
Adam had done." To suffer for the kind and not for individuals of it, "He
came not by ordinary generation": the Spirit took a portion "from all the
fallen substance before Him" to make his body. So he came under the load of
"a fallen world's infirmity and sin" freely, "without any obligation of
whatever kind"; in covenant language the Father "imputed it to Him; treated
Him as the guilty one". Guilt is on Christ, in this account, as something
borne for others, not as his own.

Second, the summary (p. 157): there were united in Christ "the Godhead, in
the person of the Son, and the manhood, in its fallen state", in one person
"in suchwise as that He was wholly without sin, holy and blameless in the
sight of God."

Third, the mechanism (pp. 159-60). "You have original sin taken away in Him
by the manner of His conception. He is not, as it were, an individual of the
sinful individuals; He is not a human person; He never had personal
subsistence as a mere man". The Spirit forms the body from the existing
flesh by a creative act, "not by ordinary generation". He took part with
"the fallen children", but "not through connexion with Adam, but by His own
free will, and His Father's free will, and the free will of the Holy Ghost;
and thus original sin is avoided, though yet the body He took is in the
fallen state, and liable to all temptations." God and fallen man are then
"brought together without any original sin" in the person of Christ.

Fourth, the representative sense (p. 174). Later in the same part he says
"that in the flesh of Christ, all flesh stood represented;—that, in the flesh
of Christ all the infirmities, sin, and guilt, of all flesh was gathered into
one", and that sin was condemned there by his holy life. He reads Romans 8:3
the same way: the Son came "not in the likeness of angels, not in the
likeness of sinless flesh, but in the likeness of sinful flesh". "He took the
weakness, He took the sinfulness; in one word. He took the fallenness of
flesh" (so the 1866 text reads), which he glosses with "God made Him sin for us." The guilt here is
the guilt of all flesh borne representatively, the imputation of pp. 153-54,
not an original sin of Christ's own.

Irving therefore holds two things apart: the fallen state of the flesh,
which Christ has, and original sin, which he does not. What keeps them apart
is that Christ is not a human person and did not come by ordinary
generation from Adam. What guilt there is on him is ours, carried for us.
