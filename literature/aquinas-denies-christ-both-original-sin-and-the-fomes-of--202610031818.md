Own-words summary of [[aquinas-summa-theologiae-iii-q-15-of-the-defects-of-soul--202610031809]].

Article 1 asks whether there was sin in Christ. Aquinas recalls why Christ
took on human defects at all: "Christ assumed our defects that He might
satisfy for us, that He might prove the truth of His human nature, and that
He might become an example of virtue to us." Sin serves none of the three. It
hinders satisfaction; it sets no example of virtue; and it proves nothing
about his humanity, "since sin does not belong to human nature, whereof God is
the cause". So "Christ nowise assumed the defect of sin", and Aquinas adds
"either original or actual", citing 1 Peter 2:22.

Article 2 asks about the fomes of sin, the scholastic name for the inborn
tinder of sin, defined here as "an inclination of the sensual appetite to what
is contrary to reason". The more perfect a person's virtues, the weaker the
fomes; Christ's virtues were perfect, so the fomes of sin "was nowise in Him".
The first objection had argued that the fomes and "the passibility and
mortality of the body spring from the same principle", the loss of original
justice. Aquinas keeps passibility and mortality in Christ and denies him the
fomes: he took the penalties of the fall, not its disordered inclination.
