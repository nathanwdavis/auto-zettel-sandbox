## On Q 29:46 — "our God and your God is one"

**The clause is glossed, not argued.** al-Tabari's whole comment on the clause
is a substitution of one phrase for another: on `وَإِلَهُنَا وَإِلَهُكُمْ
وَاحِدٌ` he says `ومعبودنا ومعبودكم واحد` — *and the one we worship and the one
you worship is one*. The gloss moves from *ilah* (god) to *maʿbud* (object of
worship), which is a move onto the thing worshipped rather than onto the
description under which it is worshipped. There is no supporting report, no
dissenting opinion recorded, and no argument. In a comment on the same verse
that canvasses three competing positions on a different clause — al-Tabari
marks them off himself with `فقال بعضهم` … `وقال آخرون` … `وقال آخرون` — this
clause gets a synonym.

**Its setting is a protocol for unverifiable testimony.** The gloss does not
stand in an ecumenical passage. al-Tabari frames the whole sentence as
instruction for a specific situation: when the People of the Book relate things
to you from their books "about which it is possible they are truthful and
possible they are lying, and you do not know their case in that" — then say to
them: *we believe in what was revealed to us and revealed to you*, and *our God
and your God is one*. He then reports the prophetic tradition he says agrees
with his reading, from Abu Hurayra: the People of the Book used to read the
Torah in Hebrew and expound it in Arabic to the Muslims, and the Prophet said
`لا تُصَدِّقُوا أهْلَ الكِتابِ وَلا تُكَذّبُوهُمْ` — *do not believe the People
of the Book and do not disbelieve them* — and say the verse. A second report
through ʿAtaʾ ibn Yasar gives the same instruction. So the shared-God sentence
arrives as what a Muslim is to **say** when judgement must be suspended.

**Two hard edges in the same passage, which is why the affirmation cannot be
read as irenic on its own.** First, the trailing clause `وَنَحْنُ لَهُ
مُسْلِمُونَ` is glossed as submission in obedience "regarding what He commanded
us and forbade us" — obligation, not fellowship. Second, and much sharper: on
`إِلا الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا مِنْهُمْ` ("except those of them who do wrong")
al-Tabari canvasses three positions and endorses the most juristic of them —
that the exception means those who refuse to pay the *jizya* and make war, and
whom one disputes with by the sword. (The first position carries several
reports, so a reader counting reports rather than positions will count more
than three; the three are the ones al-Tabari's own `وقال آخرون` divides.) He defends it against the obvious
objection (are the People of the Book not all wrongdoers, by unbelief?) by
distinguishing wronging *themselves* through unbelief, which he grants of all
of them, from wronging *the believers*, which is what the verse's exception
picks out. He also rejects Qatada's view that the verse is abrogated by Q 9:29,
on his general principle that no ruling of God's Book may be declared abrogated
"except by evidence that must be accepted, from report or from reason."

The composite is worth stating plainly, because it is not the shape either side
of the modern debate expects: one and the same passage affirms that Muslims and
the People of the Book worship the same object, restricts polite disputation to
those who submit to the *jizya*, and treats the two as raising no tension.

## On Q 3:64 — the "common word"

**"Sawaʾ" is glossed as "ʿadl", and the word's content is tawhid plus
disavowal.** On `كَلِمَةٍ سَوَاءٍ` al-Tabari writes `يعني: إلى كلمة عدل بيننا
وبينكم` — *meaning: to an equitable word between us and you* — and then fills it
in: `والكلمة العدل، هي أن نوحِّد الله فلا نعبد غيره، ونبرأ من كل معبود سواه،
فلا نشرك به شيئًا` — *and the equitable word is that we affirm God's oneness and
worship none besides Him, and disavow every object of worship other than Him,
associating nothing with Him*. The clause about not taking one another as lords
he reads as not rendering obedience to a human in what God has forbidden, and
not magnifying him by prostration as one prostrates to one's Lord. He later
grounds the address to both communities on a general principle rather than on
any report: devoting worship to God alone is obligatory "upon every one of God's
creation who is commanded and forbidden", and the term *ahl al-kitab* covers
people of the Torah and people of the Gospel alike, with nothing in the verse
singling out either.

**The occasion reports make it a demand, and record its refusal.** al-Tabari
gives two families of report on whom the verse addresses. One puts it at the
Jews of Medina (Qatada, al-Rabiʿ, Ibn Jurayj), the last of these adding that the
Prophet called them to it "and they refused him." The other puts it at the
Christian delegation of Najran (Ibn Ishaq via Muhammad ibn Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr,
al-Suddi, Ibn Zayd). Ibn Zayd's version is the most pointed: after the delegation
refused on the question of Jesus, the instruction was to call them to something
easier — and 3:64 is that easier thing — `فأبوا أن يقبلوا هذا ولا الآخر`, *and
they refused to accept this or the other*.

## What this source does and does not settle

It settles that the classical Sunni tradition has a position on the co-reference
question and that the position is affirmative: the object of worship is one. It
settles that the position is delivered as a rule about what to say, in a setting
of suspended judgement, rather than as a thesis defended.

It does not settle what the tradition as a whole holds. This is one commentator,
however foundational, on two verses; al-Qurtubi, al-Razi and Ibn Kathir are
uncaptured, and so is every juristic treatment of whether Christians count as
*mushrikun*. Nor does it settle anything about the modern debate's terms:
al-Tabari is not answering the reference/description question, because nobody
had put it to him. What he supplies is a datum that any account of how Muslim
theology handles the question has to fit — and one that the base's single
previous Muslim-voice source, an irenic collective letter, could not supply on
its own.

The grounding limit that matters most: the argumentative weight here rests on

solid; it is the *English* that is this base's own and could be bettered by a
