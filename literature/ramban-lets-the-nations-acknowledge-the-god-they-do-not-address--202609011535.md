Own-words summary of [[ramban-commentary-on-the-torah--202609011530]].

**The allotment (Leviticus 18:25).** Ramban explains a law about sexual
morality by way of a cosmological "secret," and the secret is a map of who
belongs to whom. God "placed the power of the lower creatures in the higher
beings, giving over each and every nation in their lands... some known star or
constellation," and "higher above them are the angels of the Supreme One whom
He placed as lords over them" -- the princes of Persia and Greece from Daniel
10. He reads Deuteronomy 4:19, "which the Eternal thy G-d hath allotted unto
all the people," as the warrant. The Land of Israel is the exception: it is
"the inheritance of the Eternal designated to His Name," over which God "has
placed none of the angels as chief, observer, or ruler." Elsewhere, "although
it all belongs to the Glorious Name, yet its purity is not perfect, because of
'the servants' who hold sway there, and the nations go astray after their
princes to worship them as well."

**The taxonomy (Exodus 20:3).** Ramban then classifies idolatry into three
kinds, and the first is the one that bears on this repository's question:
peoples who "began to worship the angels, who are the Separate Intelligences,
because it is known that some of them have rulership over the peoples." The
decisive sentence is his next: "They worshipped the angels **even though they
admitted that supreme strength and infinite power belonged only to G-d the
Most High**," and he cites the rabbinic observation that the peoples of the
world "call G-d the Most High 'G-d of gods'." The second kind worships the
visible host -- sun, moon, constellations; a variant worships outstanding men
such as Nebuchadnezzar or Pharaoh, on the theory that binding one's thought to
an ascendant person raises one's own star.

**What the prohibition prohibits.** On *elohim acheirim* Ramban is precise:
"wherever Scripture says *elohim acheirim*, the meaning is 'others besides the
Glorious Name'," and the verse is "a prohibition against believing in any of
these beings, accepting them as gods, or saying to them, 'thou art my god'."
The offence is an act of acceptance and address, not a failure of cosmology.

**And whom it binds.** Ramban restricts divine jealousy to Israel, on the
ground that "Israel is the treasured possession of the Glorious Name": "if His
people, His servants, turn to other gods, G-d is 'jealous' of them even as a
man is jealous of his wife when she goes to other men." Then, flatly:
"Scripture uses no such term of jealousy with reference to other peoples to
whom He has allotted the hosts of heaven."
