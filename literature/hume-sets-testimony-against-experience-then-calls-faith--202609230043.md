Own-words summary of [[an-enquiry-concerning-human-understanding--202609230038]].

Part I of "Of Miracles" ends in a maxim: "no testimony is sufficient to
establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its
falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to
establish". Faced with a report of a man raised from the dead, Hume asks
whether it is more probable that the witness should "either deceive or be
deceived" or that the event happened: "I weigh the one miracle against the
other ... and always reject the greater miracle" (para. 91). Part II argues
that no actual testimony has ever met that bar.

The closing paragraphs apply the conclusion to religion. Hume claims to be
pleased that the argument will "confound those dangerous friends or
disguised enemies to the Christian Religion, who have undertaken to defend
it by the principles of human reason", because "Our most holy religion is
founded on Faith, not on reason" (para. 100). He tests the Pentateuch by
this standard as a "mere human writer and historian", and it fails. The
last paragraph turns the whole relation around: the Christian religion "not
only was at first attended with miracles, but even at this day cannot be
believed by any reasonable person without one. Mere reason is insufficient
to convince us of its veracity: And whoever is moved by Faith to assent to
it, is conscious of a continued miracle in his own person, which subverts
all the principles of his understanding, and gives him a determination to
believe what is most contrary to custom and experience" (para. 101).

The irony is plain from the preceding argument. Paragraph 101 does not
grant faith a miraculous source. It makes the believer's assent the one
"miracle" left, because on Hume's reckoning nothing else could produce it.

Scope: Section X only. The rest of the Enquiry is in the capture but is not
summarized here.
