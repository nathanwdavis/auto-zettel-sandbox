Own-words summary of [[laporte-rigid-designators-sep--202609041950]].

The entry's definition is austere: "A rigid designator designates the same
object in all possible worlds in which that object exists and never designates
anything else." Rigidity is thus a property of a *term's behaviour across
worlds*, not a thesis about what a term's users know or believe.

The passage this repository needs is the one that separates rigidity from the
polemic it is usually enlisted in. LaPorte writes: "Rigidity's work is
compatible with descriptivism. Although some philosophers seem to have the
impression that the important work that rigidity performs is to *refute*
descriptivism, this seems to be a mistake." He gives the reason in the form of
a one-way entailment: "even if rigidity does follow from anti-descriptivism,
anti-descriptivism does not follow from rigidity: so rigidity's work is not
*limited* to that of direct reference." And he names the counterexample class:
"We have seen that descriptive designators may be rigid: e.g., world-indexed
designators."

He adds a second separation, between rigidity and the direct-reference theory
that is often taken to carry it: "rigidity's importance does not hang on the
outcome of controversies surrounding the theory of direct reference," itself
"a controversial theory, as proponents concede."

So on this entry's account there are at least three positions where a reader
expects two: descriptions may fix reference; a term may refer directly and
non-descriptively; and a term may be rigid, which is compatible with either.
Rigidity does not sort the first two.
