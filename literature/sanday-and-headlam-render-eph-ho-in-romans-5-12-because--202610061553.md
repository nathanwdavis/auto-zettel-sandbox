Own-words summary of [[a-critical-and-exegetical-commentary-on-the-epistle-to-the--202610061546]].

Sanday and Headlam (10th ed., 1905) on Romans 5:12-14, read from the scan's OCR
text, with its errors left as they are.

**The phrase *eph' hō*.** "Though this expression has been much fought over",
they hold that the right rendering is 'because' (the AV's and RV's 'for that').
They list the alternatives and reject each. Origen, "followed by the Latin
commentators Aug. and Ambrstr.", took the relative as masculine with Adam as its
antecedent, 'in whom': the preposition would be the wrong one for that sense, and
the relative stands too far from its noun. Some Greeks reported by Photius took
it as 'in which', meaning death, which they call even less possible. Some
moderns read it as 'in like manner as' or 'in so far as', which they say the
Greek will not bear. 'Because' is the reading of Theodoret, Photius and
Euthymius Zigabenus and of most modern commentators: "This is in agreement with
Greek usage and is alone satisfactory." In classical Greek the phrase more often
means 'on condition that'; in the New Testament its other two uses (2 Cor 5:4,
Phil 3:12) they read as 'because'.

**The crux is not the phrase but the verb.** Having settled the conjunction, they
put the real question: "In what sense did 'all sin'?" They give three answers.
(1) All sinned implicitly in Adam's sin, Bengel's "omnes peccarunt, Adamo
peccante"; against it, the words supplied are too important to be left
unstated, and a phrase naming Adam "would have removed all ambiguity". (2) "The
Greek commentators for the most part supply nothing" and take the verb to mean
that each sinned in his own person; against it, "it destroys the parallelism
between Adam and Christ", and Paul goes on to say they could not sin as Adam
did. (3) Each sinned in his own person, but "their sin was due in part to
tendencies inherited from Adam", the reading they favour, supported by 4 Ezra's
evil heart in Adam and his descendants.

On the passage as a whole they say that the effects of Adam's fall were passed
on, but "St. Paul nowhere says how they were transmitted", nor exactly what was;
they take him to mean the liability to sin and the liability to die.

**The excursus on Jewish theology.** Paul's three propositions (death from
Adam, sin and the tendency to sin from Adam, and individual responsibility all
the same) each find partial parallels in Jewish sources, but the Talmud does
"not seem to have had any consistent doctrine on the subject". At second hand
they report Edersheim, that original sin and the sinfulness of the whole nature
"were not held by the ancient Rabbis", and that Edersheim reports two opinions
on death, one ascribing it to personal guilt and one to Adam's; they add, in
their own voice, that the latter was by far the commoner view (the OCR
breaks their word for this across a line). The closest approaches are in 4
Ezra and the Apocalypse of Baruch, which they quote in Latin: "Cor enim malignum baiulans
primus Adam transgressus et victus est". Also at second hand, Weber's summary of
the Talmud: "By the Fall man came under a curse, is guilty of death", while sin,
"to which the bent and leaning had already been planted in man by creation",
became a fact and the evil impulse gained the mastery; and Weber's formula that
"there is such a thing as transmission of guilt" but not of sin (the German,
which the OCR garbles, contrasts *Erbschuld* with *Erbsünde*). They put the
rabbis' denial of inherited sin down to their insistence on free will and
individual responsibility.

Their verdict is that Paul is not opposed to his contemporaries: "Man inherits
his nature; and yet he must not be allowed to shift responsibility from
himself", and Paul contents himself with "leaving the two complementary truths
side by side".
