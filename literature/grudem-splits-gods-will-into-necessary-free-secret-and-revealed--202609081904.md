**Where this sits.** The section opens by recalling what chapter 12 covered — God's being (spirituality, invisibility), his "mental attributes (knowledge, wisdom, and truthfulness)," and his moral attributes — and announces chapter 13 as the *attributes of purpose*: will, freedom, omnipotence. That opening sentence incidentally confirms, from the book's own words, something this repository had inferred: the wisdom material captured at pp. 232-233 belongs to chapter 12, alongside knowledge.

**Will (14).** Defined: God's will is "that attribute of God whereby he approves and determines to bring about every action necessary for the existence and activity of himself and all creation." The general case is argued from Eph. 1:11, where Grudem presses two words — *ta panta* as Paul's cosmic "all things," and *energeō* as a present participle implying continual activity, so that the verse "might more explicitly be translated, 'who continually brings about everything in the universe according to the counsel of his will.'" The scope is then filled in: creation (Rev. 4:11), government (Dan. 4:32; Rom. 13:1), the crucifixion in its details (Acts 4:27-28, where the plural *hosa* is made to carry "not simply the fact of Jesus' death but all the detailed events connected with it"), Christian suffering (1 Peter 3:17; 4:19), and the ordinary plans of James 4:13-15.

**Two distinctions (14.b).** First, *necessary* versus *free* will. God necessarily wills himself — "I am who I am" (Ex. 3:14) — and freely wills everything else, creation and redemption included. Grudem guards this hard: to look for a necessary cause of creation or redemption in God's own being "would rob God of his total independence. It would be to say that without us God could not truly be God."

Second, *secret* versus *revealed* will, grounded in Deut. 29:29. The revealed will is "sometimes also called God's will of precept or will of command"; the secret will "includes most of his hidden decrees by which he governs the universe and determines everything that will happen," and is "sometimes also called God's will of decree." Texts are then sorted between the two. To the revealed will: "Your will be done" (Matt. 6:10), Matt. 7:21, Eph. 5:17, 1 John 5:14 — and, importantly, 1 Tim. 2:4 and 2 Peter 3:9, the two verses saying God wills all to be saved. To the secret will: James 4:15, Gen. 50:20 (Joseph's brothers), 1 Cor. 4:19, Matt. 11:25-26, Rom. 9:18, Acts 4:28.

Two of those sortings are argued rather than asserted, and by different means. The Lord's Prayer cannot be about the secret will because a prayer for what is already decreed "would simply be to pray, 'May what is going to happen happen.' That would be a hollow prayer indeed, for it would not be asking for anything at all." And 1 Tim. 2:4 cannot be the secret will "because the New Testament is clear that there will be a final judgment and not all will be saved."

**The danger clause.** Grudem names the risk in his own doctrine — that one might think God takes pleasure in evil, or that responsibility is dissolved — and answers with Acts 2:23, where the "definite plan and foreknowledge of God" and killing "by the hands of lawless men" stand in one sentence. The refusal is flat and the concession explicit: "we must never understand it to imply that we are freed from responsibility for evil or that God is ever to be blamed for sin. Scripture never speaks that way, and we may not either, even though how this can be so may remain a mystery for us in this age."

**Freedom (15) and omnipotence (16).** God's freedom is "that attribute of God whereby he does whatever he pleases" — no external constraint (Ps. 115:3; Prov. 21:1; Dan. 4:35). Omnipotence is deliberately not stated as the power to do anything: "God's omnipotence means that God is able to do all his holy will," because God cannot lie, sin, deny himself, or be tempted. A footnote answers the immovable-stone puzzle with an unembarrassed "No."

**The human side.** The section closes on the creature. God "has made us in such a way that we show in our lives some faint reflection of each of them," including will: "We exercise choice and make real decisions regarding the events of our lives. Although our will is not absolutely free in the way God's is, God has nonetheless given us relative freedom within our spheres of activity in the universe he has created." The passage ends on political liberty, quoting Patrick Henry.
