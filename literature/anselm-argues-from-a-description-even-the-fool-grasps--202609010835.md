Own-words summary of [[anselm-proslogium--202609010830]].

The Preface announces the project: to replace the Monologion's chain of
arguments with "a single argument" that by itself proves that God truly
exists and is the supreme good. The whole treatise is cast as a prayer -- the
argument is addressed *to* God, in the posture of faith seeking understanding
("I believe in order to understand," ch. 1).

Chapter 2 supplies the engine. God is introduced not by name, lineage, or
scriptural deed but by a bare description: "a being than which nothing greater
can be conceived." Anselm's first move is to establish that the atheist fool of
Psalm 14 understands the phrase when he hears it ("For, when he hears of this,
he understands it"), and that what is understood exists at least in the
understanding. The second move is the
famous comparison: existing in reality is greater than existing in the
understanding alone, so a greatest-conceivable confined to the understanding
would be one than which a greater *can* be conceived -- a contradiction. Hence
the thing described exists in reality.

Chapter 3 sharpens this to necessity -- what cannot be conceived not to exist
is greater, so this being "cannot even be conceived not to exist" -- and adds a
uniqueness clause: "whatever else there is, except you alone, can be conceived
not to exist"; to this being alone does it belong to exist most truly. Chapter
4 distinguishes conceiving a word from understanding the thing, to explain how
the fool managed to say what cannot coherently be thought. Chapter 5 then
derives the attributes from the same formula: the being must be "whatever it
is better to be than not to be" -- just, truthful, blessed -- and, as "the
highest of all beings," alone self-existent and creator of everything else
from nothing.

Two features matter for reuse. The description is doing all the identifying
work, and it is graspable by an unbeliever -- the fool is a premise, not an
aside. And only after the argument concludes does Anselm identify its object
with the addressee of his prayer: "and this being you are, O Lord, our God"
(ch. 3). That identification is a separate act from the argument itself.
