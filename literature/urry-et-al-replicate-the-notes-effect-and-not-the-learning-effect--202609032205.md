Own-words summary of [[urry-et-al-dont-ditch-the-laptop-just-yet--202609032200]].
Locators are manuscript pages of the accepted-manuscript capture, not journal
pages; the reference note says why.

The design is a preregistered direct replication of Study 1 of Mueller and
Oppenheimer 2014 (preregistered 7 March 2017; materials, data and code posted
at https://osf.io/tr868/). 142 undergraduates watched a TED talk taking notes
either on a laptop (74) or longhand (68), were distracted briefly, and took a
quiz with no chance to study their notes. Three things were measured: quiz
performance, the number of words in the notes, and the proportion of words
overlapping verbatim with the lecturer's (ms. pp. 1, 8).

**What replicated.** Everything about the notes themselves, and strongly.
Laptop takers wrote far more (M = 230.69 words, SD = 133.87) than longhand
takers (M = 136.16, SD = 66.26), t(94.63) = −5.22, p < .001, and their notes
overlapped the lecture verbatim far more (12.97% vs 8.13%), t(119.46) = −4.98,
p < .001. Both effects are large and both match the original's direction and
rough size (ms. p. 10).

**What did not.** The finding the paper is famous for. The original reported
that longhand takers did better on conceptual-application items; here that
difference "was not significant" and the replication effect was "negligible in
the opposite direction," Hedges' *g* = −0.13, 95% CI [−0.45, 0.20], which is
significantly different from the original effect (*g* = 0.34), t(139.03) =
−2.78, p = .003, and statistically equivalent to zero within ±0.49 (ms. p. 9).
Factual recall showed nothing in either study — *g* = −0.08, 95% CI [−0.41,
0.25] — which is also what the original found. The authors report that the
null held across mixed-effects ANOVAs, equivalence tests, Bayesian analyses
and linear mixed-effects regressions (ms. p. 15).

**The meta-analytic layer, which is what makes this more than one failed
replication.** They pooled *k* = 8 closely matched studies — the original's
Studies 1 and 2, two experiments from Morehead, Dunlosky and Rawson 2019, this
replication, and single-study replications by Kirkland 2016, Luo et al. 2018,
and Mitchell and Zheng 2017 — selected on five stated criteria: laptop-versus-
longhand manipulated experimentally, quiz on the same day, video lecture, all
three measures reported, undergraduates (ms. p. 11). Effect sizes were
computed longhand minus laptop. Pooled total quiz performance: *g* = 0.04, 95%
CI [−0.13, 0.20], not significant (Z = 0.46, p = .645) and statistically
equivalent to zero within ±0.38. That bound is chosen to mean something
specific: it is "the effect size the original study could detect with 33%
power," so equivalence at that bound says the pooled effect "was too small to
have been detected in the original study" (ms. p. 11). Factual *g* = 0.03,
conceptual *g* = 0.14, neither significant. Meanwhile the notes-content effects
pool large and unambiguous: word count *g* = −0.91, verbatim overlap *g* =
−0.78, both p < .001 (ms. p. 14).

**Their correlational result, and their own warning about it.** In both the
original and the replication, more words in the notes went with better quiz
performance, and more verbatim overlap with worse — though the second was "not
robust" here. They explicitly refuse the causal reading: they manipulated
neither variable, and word count or verbatim overlap "may be third-variable
proxies for motivation, conscientiousness, and/or interest" (ms. p. 15).

**Limits they state themselves.** ~80 students ran the sessions around campus,
so "situation noise was likely a considerable source of random error" that
could have hidden a real effect. Eight studies is too few for a definitive
conclusion, unpublished replications were likely missed, and publication bias
was not modelled, so the pooled estimates are "preliminary" (ms. p. 16). Only
immediate testing with no study opportunity was examined, and some evidence
suggests the medium effect appears only when notes are studied later (Luo et
al. 2018; the original's own Study 3).

**The limit with the most bearing on this base**, and it is their third future
direction rather than an aside: every study in this literature compares one
note-taking medium against another, and none includes a no-notes control.
Focusing on laptop versus longhand, they write, "encourages dichotomous
thinking when the story likely is more complicated," citing Jansen, Lakens and
IJsselsteijn 2017 that any note-taking benefit "depends on the way lectures are
presented, how notes are taken, and individual differences in cognitive
abilities" (ms. p. 16). So this literature is not evidence that note taking
works. It is evidence about which pen you hold while doing it.
