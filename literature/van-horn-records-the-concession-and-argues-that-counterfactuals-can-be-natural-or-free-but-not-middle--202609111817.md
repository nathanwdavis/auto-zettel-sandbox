**The two theses.** Van Horn abbreviates theological determinism as TD and middle knowledge as MK, and opens by noting that "among theologians some of the more enthusiastic proponents of this doctrine are theological determinists", and that "some former theological determinist proponents of MK are now renouncing the doctrine." He argues "that both sides to this controversy are correct": "(1) if one assumes traditional Calvinism and the standard account of the truth conditions for counterfactuals, TD is inconsistent with MK; (2) nevertheless, there are at least prima facie ways of coherently combining Calvinism with MK." In the uncaptured section I he defines compatibilism and incompatibilism as theses about free will and causal determinism, and complains that theologians use the words for theses about free will and sovereignty instead, citing Ware's "Robots, Royalty and Relationships?" (*Criswell Theological Review*, 2004) as an example.

**The Calvinist proponents.** Tiessen (*Providence and Prayer*, 319): "Without middle knowledge I cannot conceptualize God’s decision as either wise or cognizant of the freedom of his creatures", and God "is able to know this because his moral creatures are voluntarily but not indeterministically free." Ware, Van Horn reports, "claims that God cannot have" libertarian middle knowledge, "for there could be no true CCFs if libertarianism were true" (a CCF is a counterfactual of creaturely freedom; note 26 gives Ware's reason as the grounding objection). "Nevertheless, God does have “compatibilist middle knowledge.”" Van Horn then quotes *God's Greater Glory* 114-15: "since freedom means that we always do what we most want, and since what we “most want” is shaped by the set of factors and circumstances that eventually give rise to one desire that stands above all others, therefore God can know the circumstances giving rise to our highest desires, and by knowing these, he can know the choice that we would make, given those particular circumstances. What the Molinist version of middle knowledge lacked—viz., a necessary connection between knowledge of the circumstances within which an agent makes his choice and knowledge of just what choice the agent would make—is here remedied by the replacement of libertarian freedom with compatibilist freedom. So, as a result, middle knowledge is explicable and it “works” when compatibilist freedom is employed, in a way that it does not with libertarian freedom." Feinberg concurs (*No One Like Him*, 752). The picture: God's "middle knowledge consists of his knowledge of how creatures would be causally determined to act freely if placed in various possible circumstances", and "he chose a world in which he knew his creatures would freely do what he wanted done."

**How odd this is.** "Historically, middle knowledge has been a fundamental philosophical assumption of Calvinism’s theological arch-nemesis, Arminianism." Turretin, Bavinck and Muller are quoted against it in uncaptured text (Muller: middle knowledge is not a middle ground but the heart of the original Arminian and Jesuit position); the captured text adds that the rejection "even has confessional status" and quotes the Westminster Confession III.2: "Although God knows whatsoever may or can come to pass, upon all supposed conditions; yet hath he not decreed any thing because he foresaw it as future, as that which would come to pass, upon such conditions." So "Tiessen, Ware, and others, are breaking with their tradition by affirming that God has middle knowledge, a doctrine which has been consistently rejected by Calvinists from the sixteenth century to today", to which Van Horn adds: "Or are they?" They deny middle knowledge of libertarian CCFs and affirm "compatibilist middle knowledge", so the question "is “compatibilist middle knowledge” or “Middle Knowledge Calvinism” a coherent position, or does it reduce to Calvinism simpliciter?"

**The confusion.** "Middle Knowledge Calvinism appears to be confused." Its adherents hold that God has middle knowledge, that human actions are causally determined, and apparently that determinism is a necessary truth; conjoined, "these claims entail that God’s knowledge of how creatures would freely act in various circumstances is part of either his natural or free knowledge." Knowledge of CCFs is not enough: "Historically, very few have denied that God has such knowledge"; Molina's Thomist opponents, Bavinck and Baxter all granted it. "What distinguishes middle knowledge from natural and free knowledge is that its contents are known independently of God’s decree, like natural knowledge, but are contingently true, like free knowledge." Ware's own account of the basis (GGG 115 n. 10) is quoted: "middle knowledge is grounded on four things that God knows prevolitionally", namely God's knowledge of the individual's character, of every factor in the possible setting, of how those factors with that character "would work" to "produce one strongest inclination within the individual", and "of just what choice the individual would in fact make", "giving rise in turn to this one (and only one) choice." Van Horn: "if God really can derive which CCFs are true in this way, then once again CCFs are included in his natural knowledge, since they would be necessary truths."

**The concession.** "Surprisingly, in the face of criticism (primarily by Paul Helm),41 both Tiessen and Ware have come to agree with the above reasoning that CCFs are part of God’s natural knowledge." Tiessen, in the conversation with Helm (WTJ 71 [2009] 448): "Reflection on Professor Helm’s recent comments has finally brought me to the conclusion that he is correct on this point. God’s knowledge of counterfactuals is not different from his knowledge of possibilities; it is therefore part of his necessary knowledge." For Ware, note 42 cites his "Response" in *Perspectives on the Doctrine of God* (the four-views volume of 2008, dated in an uncaptured note), p. 74, and adds: "Ware still wants to hang onto the term “middle knowledge” as a label of a useful subset of natural knowledge, but this is clearly a misuse of the term that will inevitably lead to confusion." Van Horn's own judgement: "They are definitely right, given their other beliefs, to have abandoned the idea that God has MK of CCFs. However, it is not obvious that CCFs are contained in his natural rather than free knowledge." The uncaptured argument that follows turns on whether God's concurrence (understood as premotion) and his endowment of causal powers are counted inside the circumstance C: if they are, the conditional is necessary and known naturally; if not, it is contingent and true by decree, so known freely.

**The conclusion of the argument, and Tiessen's lesson.** "Calvinist proponents of MK are confused. They affirm that God knows what any creature would freely do in any possible circumstance in which it can be placed", and this, he adds, has always been affirmed by the majority of Christian theologians and philosophers. "knowledge of CCFs is not sufficient for MK", and "while CCFs may be either natural or free, they cannot be middle, given classical Calvinism." Tiessen drew the further lesson (Van Horn's note 53, outside the capture, gives the page as WTJ 71:450) that "the sole rationale for positing middle knowledge is to give room for libertarian creaturely freedom as a fact of the world God chooses to actualize. I now believe that rejection of the Molinist construction because of its faulty understanding of freedom also entails rejection of the concept of divine middle knowledge." Van Horn disagrees: "Rejection of libertarianism does not require rejection of MK"; his two routes (uncaptured) are to weaken concurrence and the argument that freedom requires determinism, or to give counterfactuals with impossible antecedents non-trivial truth values.

**Ware on evil.** Van Horn reports that in *God's Greater Glory* Ware "explicitly rejects the traditional Calvinist claim that God causes human sinful actions. While he is eager to affirm that God causes good actions and is sovereign over evil, the idea that he causes evil actions is difficult to square with divine goodness." He quotes GGG 123-24: "Perhaps we should think of God’s regulating the factors of a situation, then, as “occasioning” a particular choice to be made, rather than as “causing” a particular choice to be made. Because God knows the natures of each person perfectly, he knows how those natures will respond to particular sets of factors presented" to them. "Thus, without causing a person to do evil, he nonetheless controls the evil they do. He controls whether evil is done, what evil is done, and in any and every case he could prevent the evil from being done. But in no case does he cause the evil to be done." Van Horn reads this narrowly: Ware "endorses the Mind argument and so thinks that all free actions, both good and evil, are caused by prior factors, and these may result in a causal chain which can be traced back to God. Rather, the context makes clear that he is rejecting the idea that God pre-moves his creatures to sin (although he does not use that term)." Note 59 adds that Tiessen quotes the passage approvingly and that Helm "sketches a similar view" in "God Does Not Take Risks" (2004).

**The cost of the reconciliations.** Van Horn's conclusion: "while those Calvinists who claim that God has middle knowledge are confused, their claim may be correct", by "modifying Calvinism’s claims concerning concurrence and freedom, or by altering/rejecting the standard account of the truth conditions for counterfactual propositions." But a Calvinist who takes either route "would need to abandon the objection, presented above by Bavinck, that Molinists are sacrificing the aseity or pure actuality of God.74 Moreover, the grounding objection would also have to be abandoned."

**Gaps.** Everything Ware and Tiessen say here is at one remove; *God's Greater Glory*, *Providence and Prayer*, the 2009 conversation and the 2008 four-views volume are unread. Van Horn's own positive proposals (Lewis-style compatibilism, Fischer's semicompatibilism, counterpossibles) are summarised from uncaptured text and not assessed. The definitions of section I, on which his charge of "confusion" partly rests, are not captured.
