Own-words summary of [[meyer-of-molecules-and-straw-men--202609011311]],
answering
[[venema-finds-no-thorough-search-behind-signatures-design-inference--202609011315]].

Meyer's defense is a boundary-drawing operation. His headline charge is
that "The balance of his review is spent refuting an argument that
Signature in the Cell does not make ... in short, Venema 'refutes' a
straw man" (p. 171): where the book "attempts to establish intelligent
design as the best explanation for the information necessary to produce
the first life, Venema critiques the claim that natural selection and
random mutation cannot produce the information necessary to produce new
forms of life from preexisting forms of life" (p. 172). The book, he
insists, "does not address the issue of biological evolution, nor does
it challenge whether mutation and selection can add new information to
DNA" (p. 174) -- so the antibody, Lenski, and nylonase counterexamples,
"in each case starting from a preexisting organism" (p. 179), leave its
thesis untouched. On the same ground he dismisses the common-ancestry
complaint: "common ancestry does not become an issue until life has
arisen" (p. 175).

Why the boundary should hold: "The process of natural selection
classically understood presupposes the differential reproduction of
living organisms and thus a preexisting mechanism of self-replication",
which itself depends on information-rich molecules -- "precisely what
origin-of-life research needs to explain" (p. 174). He seals the point
with authorities: Dobzhansky's "Pre-biological natural selection is a
contradiction in terms" and de Duve's remark that such theories "need
information which implies they have to presuppose what is to be
explained in the first place" (pp. 174-5).

Inside the boundary, the argument is stated as a positive inference,
not only a gap: from "our uniform and repeated experience", intelligence
is "a type of cause ... that has demonstrated the power to produce
functionally specified information from physical or chemical
constituents", and "we know of no other cause" that does so "starting
... from a purely physical or chemical state" (pp. 173-4). Meyer then
turns Venema's own concession into support: by acknowledging that no
complete abiogenesis mechanism exists, Venema "effectively concedes the
main argument of the book" (p. 175), and the response closes: "clearly,
Venema did not refute the argument of Signature in the Cell" (p. 179).

Scope and caveats: the RNA-world and Yarus/direct-templating sections
and the endnotes (in one of which Meyer concedes a factual correction
of Venema's -- that calling the ribosome protein-dominated was a poor
choice of words -- while rejecting the inference Venema drew from it)
are outside the captured excerpts; they are cited here only as
attributed summary and never quoted. The closing
appeals to Ewert on the immune system and to Axe contra Hunt are
reported here as Meyer's claims, not as adjudicated findings (Axe's
affiliation with the Discovery Institute's Biologic Institute is stated
in the review this piece answers). Like that review, this documents one
named side of a dispute conducted inside the ASA's own journal -- Meyer
identifying himself in the captured opening as "a longtime ASA member".
