Own-words summary of [[venema-seeking-a-signature--202609011310]].

Venema reads *Signature in the Cell* as resting everything on an
exclusivity claim. On his summary, Meyer's chapter 7 requires evidence
that a cause was "present at the required time", "known to be causally
adequate", and -- the load-bearing criterion -- backed by the "absence
of evidence (despite a thorough search) ... of ... other possible
causes" (p. 276). Since intelligence's adequacy "is taken as a given",
"the power of this argument lies squarely in the quality of his
'thorough search' for alternate causes" (p. 277). The review then
argues that search fails twice over.

First, against the claim that "there is no known natural mechanism that
can add information to DNA" (p. 278), Venema stacks counterexamples,
flagship among them antibody affinity maturation: successive rounds of
mutation and selection raise an antibody from weak to strong binding,
which "By any reasonable definition ... is an increase in biological
information", achieved with "no planned target in mind" -- so that "By
Meyer's definition, the pathogen is the antibody designer" (p. 279).
With Lenski's E. coli lines, nylonase, and de novo proteins alongside,
he concludes "Meyer's argument for the exclusivity of intelligence in
producing biological information fails", and mutation-plus-selection
"now becomes a candidate for the origin of biological information from
nonliving precursors" -- granting that this still requires "a plausible
pathway leading from nonliving precursors to a replicating entity
capable of variation on which natural selection can act" (pp. 279-80).
(He also disputes the use of Axe's protein-rarity work: rarity in
sequence space is not the same property as evolutionary isolation,
p. 278.)

Second, on origin-of-life research itself, Venema concedes "no complete
mechanism for abiogenesis has yet been put forward" but finds the
book's survey "not extensive, but selective and misleading at several
key points" (p. 280), dwelling on discarded hypotheses while giving the
RNA world one chapter and metabolism-first models none. Combined with
elementary errors catalogued in "Odds and Ends" (summarized here, not
quoted), he judges that Meyer "has made neither a thorough search ...
nor a fair assessment of current origin-of-life research" (p. 282), and
delivers the review's verdict on the book: "This is not good science,
nor science in any meaningful sense" (p. 282). The closing register
matters for locating this critique inside the design argument's own
constituency rather than outside it: "I, for one, would be fascinated
by a scientifically plausible design argument", but "for those of us
waiting for the science behind ID, it looks as if the wait goes on"
(p. 282).

Scope and caveats: this is one side of a published exchange -- Meyer's
response disputes precisely the "add information to DNA" framing
summarized above (see
[[meyer-confines-signatures-argument-to-the-prebiotic-origin-of-information--202609011316]]).
The RNA-world detail, common-ancestry section, and most of "Odds and
Ends" are outside the captured excerpts and are never quoted here.
Venema writes in the journal of the American Scientific Affiliation as
its commissioned reviewer; the article's own biography box (cited as
attributed summary, not captured) places him at Trinity Western
University and among BioLogos's contributors. The review documents one
named assessment from inside the ASA's constituency, not a survey of
evangelical opinion.
