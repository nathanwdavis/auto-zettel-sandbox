**Grounding: raw/202609081910-maimonides-teshuvah-5-and-6-3.txt, the Touger translation fetched from Sefaria. The Hebrew of these halachot was not captured, so nothing below turns on Maimonides' precise wording in Hebrew.**

**The thesis (5:1-3).** "Free will is granted to all men. If one desires to turn himself to the path of good and be righteous, the choice is his. Should he desire to turn to the path of evil and be wicked, the choice is his." The proof text is from the Torah — Gen. 3:22, "Behold, man has become unique as ourselves, knowing good and evil" — read as marking out the human species as the one that can "on his own initiative, with his knowledge and thought, know good and evil, and do what he desires."

The contrary view is not merely rejected but scorned: a person should not entertain "the thesis held by the fools among the gentiles and the majority of the undeveloped among Israel that, at the time of a man's creation, The Holy One, blessed be He, decrees whether he will be righteous or wicked. This is untrue." Each person "is fit to be righteous like Moses, our teacher, or wicked, like Jeroboam," and "There is no one who compels him, sentences him, or leads him towards either of these two paths."

Then the status claim, which is the important one: "This principle is a fundamental concept and a pillar [on which rests the totality] of the Torah and mitzvot," supported by Deut. 30:15 and 11:26 — "Behold, I have set before you today life [and good, death and evil]" — "implying that the choice is in your hands." The conclusion is stated without qualification: "the Creator does not compel or decree that people should do either good or bad. Rather, everything is left to their [own choice]."

**The argument from the commandments (5:4).** If God decreed a person righteous or wicked, or gave him a nature drawing him to a path, "how could He command us through [the words of] the prophets: 'Do this,' 'Do not do this,' 'Improve your behavior,' or 'Do not follow after your wickedness?'" A paragraph later, having spelled out the alternative, he presses it: "What place would there be for the entire Torah? According to which judgement or sense of justice would retribution be administered to the wicked or reward to the righteous?" The commandments are treated as evidence about what human beings can do; a command presupposes the capacity to obey or refuse it.

Maimonides then raises the sovereignty objection against himself, and in the strongest form: "Is it possible for anything to happen in this world without the permission and desire of its Creator as [Psalms 135:6] states: 'Whatever God wishes, He has done in the heavens and in the earth?'" His answer concedes the premise and locates freedom inside it: "everything is done in accord with His will and, nevertheless, we are responsible for our deeds." The resolution is by analogy with natures — as God willed fire to rise and water to descend, "so too, He desired that man have free choice and be responsible for his deeds, without being pulled or forced." Human freedom is itself a divine decree about what kind of creature a human is.

**The foreknowledge objection (5:5).** Stated exactly as the Christian debates state it: if God knows a man will be righteous, "[it appears] impossible for him not to be righteous"; and if he could still be wicked, "then His knowledge would be incomplete." Maimonides calls the resolution's measure "longer than the earth and broader than the sea," and answers from the doctrine of God rather than from the doctrine of man: God "does not know with a knowledge that is external from Him as do men, whose knowledge and selves are two [different entities]. Rather, He, may His name be praised, and His knowledge are one." Since we cannot comprehend God's essence (Ex. 33:20), we cannot comprehend his knowing either. What is *not* left in doubt is which half survives: "this is known without any doubt: That man's actions are in his [own] hands and The Holy One, blessed be He, does not lead him [in a particular direction] or decree that he do anything."

**Pharaoh (6:3).** The hardening is retributive and second, not constitutive and first. A person may sin so gravely that "his Teshuvah will be held back" as the penalty. "For these reasons, it is written in the Torah [Exodus 4:21], 'I will harden Pharaoh's heart.' Since, he began to sin on his own initiative and caused hardships to the Israelites who dwelled in his land … judgment obligated that he be prevented from repenting so that he would suffer retribution." Ex. 9:16 explains why Moses was still sent: to make publicly known "that when the Holy One, blessed be He, withholds repentance from a sinner, he cannot repent." Sichon (Deut. 2:30), the Canaanites (Josh. 11:20) and Israel under Elijah (1 Kings 18:37) are handled the same way, and the section ends flatly: "the Almighty did not decree that Pharaoh should harm the Israelites … They all sinned on their own initiative and they were obligated to have Teshuvah held back from them."

**Gaps.** 5:5 refers its own resolution back to Hilchot Yesodei HaTorah ch. 2, which is not on file (this base has 1:7). The *Guide for the Perplexed* III on providence and divine knowledge is unread here, and is where Maimonides' fuller and sometimes differently pitched treatment lies. No commentary on these halachot has been captured, so the internal Jewish argument about whether 5:5 succeeds — a live question in the tradition, with Ra'avad's gloss the classic objection — is not represented at all.
