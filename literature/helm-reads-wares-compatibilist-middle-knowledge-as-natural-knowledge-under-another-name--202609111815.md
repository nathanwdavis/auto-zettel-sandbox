Ware's words appear only as Helm quotes them, with Helm's page references to *God's Greater Glory* ("GGG"), which is not on file.**

**What the post is.** Helm's own analysis of Bruce Ware's "compatibilist middle knowledge" as presented in *God's Greater Glory* (Crossway, 2004). In the uncaptured opening paragraph Helm names Tiessen and Ware as the two Calvinists who have openly avowed a Calvinist middle knowledge, mentions rumours about Frame and Feinberg that he has not checked, and says his conversation with Tiessen will appear in the *Westminster Theological Journal*; the post takes Ware separately because his presentation differs from Tiessen's.

**What Ware wants middle knowledge for.** Ware "offers his thoughts about middle knowledge as a contribution to establishing and further understanding God’s asymmetrical attitude to good and evil": God works all things after the counsel of his will, but "the way in which he works his will regarding evil is different from the way in which he works good, since being immaculately holy, he cannot be the author of sin". Helm places this inside a long Reformed tradition of answering the charge that the Reformed make God the author of sin, and lists the three standard replies: that evil is a privation, so that God, "who has fullness of being, cannot author evil"; that God "is the primary cause, not to be confused with secondary, creaturely causes"; and "that God brings about evil by willingly permitting it". The three "are not exclusive of each other, but can be combined to make a cumulative case". Then the sentence the rest of the post turns on: "There remains, of course, an element of mystery, of how it is that the divine ordination meshes with human sin and evil. Augustine and Calvin, for example, routinely refer to the matter as ‘ineffable’." Ware and others "offer 'Compatibilist Middle Knowledge' as one way in which such ineffability can be mitigated."

**What is left of middle knowledge once libertarian freedom goes.** In the uncaptured definition paragraph Helm attributes the doctrine to Fonseca and Molina, says Arminius borrowed it, and argues that it collapses once its rationale, the freedom of indifference, is removed; in the uncaptured first paragraph of "Enter Bruce Ware" he records Ware's explicit commitment to compatibilist freedom as revealed truth and Ware's Edwards-like argument that incompatibilism is logically defective (GGG 78). In the captured text, Ware "notes that libertarian freedom is problematic. (GGG 112)" and holds that, libertarian freedom abandoned, there is "a necessary connection (that is, a causally necessary connection)" between "knowledge of a given state of affairs and knowledge of what the agent would choose in that particular setting", so that "God could know what the agent would choose by knowing fully the circumstances in which the agent would make his choice". Helm quotes Ware's own sentence: "The set of factors in which the agent makes his choice constitutes a set of individually necessary and jointly necessary sufficient conditions for forming within the agent a strongest inclination or highest desire by which he then makes the one choice that is in accordance with that highest desire" (GGG 114).

**Helm's objection.** "But if God knows what Jones, if placed in circumstances C, would do, then this is surely part of God’s natural knowledge, his knowledge of all necessities and possibilities." Middle knowledge "‘works’ for the compatibilist, Ware says (GGG 115), but it only works because it is not genuine middle knowledge, a distinct type of divine knowledge, but part of God’s natural knowledge. It works precisely because it is not middle knowledge." The scriptural examples Ware cites (GGG 115-9) "are of God’s knowledge of unactualised possibilities, possibilities therefore that are counterfactual, but they are not counterfactuals of human (libertarian) freedom, and so are not middle knowledge at all." Helm's diagnosis is "an imperfect grasp of what the medieval theologians, and following them the Reformed and the Reformed Orthodox, have meant by God’s necessary knowledge", and his verdict a pun: "I fear that what results is not so much middle knowledge as muddle knowledge." Two further charges are made in uncaptured paragraphs and are cited here only as summary: that Ware's picture of God passing from natural to free knowledge through middle knowledge is, despite Ware's disclaimer in a footnote (GGG 120 fn. 12), infected with temporalism; and that God does not change possible people by regulating their circumstances (GGG 121, 123) but simply selects one possibility rather than another.

**The verdict and where the mystery stays.** "The only kind of middle knowledge is that devised by Molina and his frères. There are no other kinds", and Ware's attempt to show that a compatibilist kind exists and helps deflect the charge of authoring evil "is consequently futile. There is divine counterfactual knowledge, knowledge of what might be and of what might have been, but that is part of God’s natural knowledge." What remains are "the standard ways of parrying the charge of God and evil: evil as privation, primary and secondary causation, and the Augustinian idea of ‘willing permission’. But the mystery remains as well. Which is how we might expect things to be."
