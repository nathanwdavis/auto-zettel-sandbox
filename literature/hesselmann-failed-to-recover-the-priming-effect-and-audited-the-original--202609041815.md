Own-words summary of
[[hesselmann-no-conclusive-evidence-of-a-google-stroop-effect--202609041805]],
read in full from the publisher-deposited text. Quotations are the author's
words; everything else is this note's characterisation.

**What the paper is.** A single-author replication, out of an undergraduate
research practicum in Berlin, of Experiment 1 of
[[sparrow-liu-and-wegner-google-effects-on-memory--202609041800]] — the
priming experiment, not the offloading ones. It is deliberately the *third*
attempt: two replications had already been run inside the Social Sciences
Replication Project and had failed, and this study was built to answer the
original author's published objection to those two.

**The replication record it reports, which is this repository's reason for
reading it.** Camerer and colleagues' 2018 project included a replication of
the Google Stroop effect, led by Holzmeister and Camerer, which "did not show a
significant effect despite adequate statistical power". The paper is careful
about why that was contestable: the original authors "did not provide Camerer
et al. (2018) with any materials or feedback on their inquiries", so the design
had to be reconstructed, and after publication Sparrow named design differences
that could have produced the null. This study therefore rebuilt the experiment
from the original report, the project's materials, and Sparrow's published
objection — "we carefully updated and validated the computer-related terms,
strictly avoided word repetitions, and manipulated cognitive load as in the
original study."

**The result.** 117 tested, 89 analysed. The manipulation worked — easy
questions were answered at 97% and hard ones at 60%, a gap the paper reports as
`t88 = 48.31, p < .001`, matching the original's 98% against 47%. The predicted
pattern did not appear: in hard blocks, computer terms were *faster* than
general terms (821 ms against 825 ms). A sequential Bayes factor of `BF01 =
5.07` favours the null; against the specific pattern the original reported, the
data are "16 times more likely under the null model". Restricting to the 57
participants who said they would in fact google a hard question changed nothing
(`BF01 = 5.01`); using all 117 made it stronger (`BF01 = 9.31`). The author's
conclusion is stated in the negative and stays there: "no evidence in favor of
the notion that the concept of the Internet or internet access ... becomes
automatically activated whenever participants are faced with hard trivia
questions", so the effect "might be much smaller than previously thought, and
less robust to variations in the experimental design".

**The audit, which is a separate contribution and worth more to this
repository than the null.** A section headed "Statistical issues in the
original study" reads the original against its own supplement and finds three
things. (i) The sample size is indeterminate: the supplement says "Forty-six
undergraduate students (28 female, 18 male) at Harvard University were tested",
the article reports `t(68)`, and — the point that does the damage — "the
reported dfs are incorrect in both scenarios". The original authors are
reported as having first confirmed 46 and later said 69. (ii) The headline
interaction rests on a post-hoc subset of four words out of twenty-four, and
because the original avoided word repetition each of those words was seen once
per participant, so "the RTs for Google/Yahoo and Target/Nike reported in the
original paper are based on single trials per participant"; the paper's verdict
is that "the authors might have capitalized on chance by selecting a subset of
words for their main analysis", and that without further information "it
remains possible that the choice was made after seeing the data". (iii) The
original's supplement reports that removing those four words leaves a *larger*
interaction (`F(1, 66) = 9.49`) than the subset itself yielded, which the audit
calls "difficult to reconcile with the reported data" — and here it stops,
because "access to the original data would be necessary to clarify this point."

**What the paper is careful not to claim, and this note follows it.** It does
not touch Experiments 2, 3 or 4. It does not say the offloading findings are
false; it does not mention them at all except to summarise them in its opening
paragraph. And it does not claim finality for its own null: "We are aware that
our study does not provide a definite answer, and the potential effects of
'cognitive offloading' on human cognition ... are definitely worth further
attention and investigation."

**One route this repository did not take.** The Camerer et al. 2018 project is
reported here at second hand — through this paper — and was not itself read.
Its own OSF materials are named in the text (`https://osf.io/wmgj9/`) and its
accepted version is in a university repository, so it is reachable; it was left
for a later cycle rather than claimed. No note in this repository cites Camerer
et al. directly, and where the two failed replications inside that project are
mentioned, they are mentioned as *this paper's report of them*.
