Justin raises the problem himself, as an objection his own argument invites.
He has just spent chapters 40-42 arguing that Christ's life was foretold, and
he stops to forestall the inference: "lest some suppose, from what has been
said by us, that we say that whatever happens, happens by a fatal necessity,
because it is foretold as known beforehand, this too we explain" (43). The
objection is therefore not imported from outside — it arises from his own
appeal to prophecy, which is what makes this the earliest preserved statement
in a Christian writer of the problem that foreknowledge appears to fix what
is foreknown.

His answer runs through desert. If "all things happen by fate", then "neither
is anything at all in our own power", and neither the good man nor the bad
one has earned anything: "unless the human race have the power of avoiding
evil and choosing good by free choice, they are not accountable for their
actions, of whatever kind they be." The empirical prop is moral change — "We
see the same man making a transition to opposite things" — which a fixed
nature could not produce. And the theological prop is creation: "For not like
other things, as trees and quadrupeds, which cannot act by choice, did God
make man", because a creature made good would deserve no praise for being so.
Justin keeps the word "fate" while emptying it of necessity: what is fated is
the moral law itself, that "they who choose the good have worthy rewards, and
they who choose the opposite have their merited awards."

Chapter 44 states the distinction the whole later tradition works with. God
foreknows and therefore foretells, but "what we say about future events being
foretold, we do not say it as if they came about by a fatal necessity" —
foreknowledge is a mode of knowing, not a mode of causing. Justin then claims
Plato's "The blame is his who chooses, and God is blameless" for Moses,
which places his own position on the anti-fatalist side of a Greek quarrel he
takes himself to be inheriting rather than starting.

What Justin is arguing against matters for dating the debate. His opponent
here is the Stoic and astrological doctrine of heimarmene, not any Christian
party, and God's sovereignty is not the thing free will is being defended
against — divine foreknowledge is invoked as a premise he shares with his
readers and then defused. The sovereignty side has no advocate in this text.
