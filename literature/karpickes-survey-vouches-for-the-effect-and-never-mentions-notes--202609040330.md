Own-words summary of [[karpicke-retrieval-based-learning--202609040325]], read
in the four passages named in the locator and captured as excerpts. **This note
records what the chapter says about the state of its own field and what its
coverage does not extend to. It is not a summary of the chapter's substance**,
most of which — theories of the mechanism, retrieval success against retrieval
effort, test formats, classroom implementations — was not read this cycle and
is not represented anywhere in this base.

**What it claims about durability.** Twenty years after the 2006 paper, the
chapter's verdict on p. 434 is that the benefits of practising retrieval "have
been replicated hundreds of times across a range of initial retrieval tasks,
and the effects of retrieval are long-lasting and promote transfer, inferencing,
and knowledge application", generalising across ages, abilities and real
classrooms. Its summary box on p. 411 says the same in one line: retrieval
practice "produces robust and long-lasting effects on learning and retention of
knowledge". The chapter also reports that more papers on the topic appeared in
the eight years to 2023 than in the twenty-five years to 2015, and names three
meta-analyses that have audited the literature — Rowland (2014), Adesope et al.
(2017) and Agarwal et al. (2021) (p. 412).

**Who is speaking.** Karpicke is the second author of the 2006 experiment and a
co-author of the 2008 open-book study, so these are a researcher's statements
about his own effect. The three meta-analyses would settle the question
independently and **none of them was read**, so what this base holds is an
attributed claim of robustness plus three named places to check it. That is
enough to say the effect is *presented by its field as robust*, which is a real
and citable fact about the state of the literature, and not enough to say the
effect *is* robust. The distinction is worth this much care because the base's
only other measured note-taking source is a direct replication by an outside
team that failed
([[morehead-dunlosky-and-rawson-report-a-direct-replication-that-did-not-hold--202609040205]]).

**What it never covers, which is why this note exists.** A search of the whole
31-page chapter — body and its seven-page reference list — for "note-taking",
"note taking", "notetaking", "notes", "external", "offload", "zettel",
"slip-box" and "diary" returns **zero occurrences of every one of them**. The
field's current survey of how retrieval builds durable knowledge does not
discuss taking notes, keeping notes, or storing knowledge outside the head, at
any point, even to dismiss it. That is a fact about the literature and not a
criticism of the chapter, which is about testing.

The nearest it comes is a single paragraph on p. 419 summarising Agarwal et al.
(2008) on open-book versus closed-book quizzing — and the interesting thing is
what happened when this cycle followed the citation instead of trusting the
summary. The chapter reports that "the open-book quiz conditions led to more
forgetting over 1 week relative to the closed-book condition", which is true on
the forgetting measure; the primary paper's own summary reports that the two
"produced equivalent retention on a delayed test", which is true on the
retention measure; the underlying numbers are the same numbers. Neither
sentence is wrong and either alone would mislead. Both are set side by side in
the capture, and the study itself is summarised at
[[agarwal-et-al-put-the-notes-on-the-desk-and-measured-what-happened-a-week-later--202609040320]].
That single citation is the whole of the contact between this literature and
the subject of this repository.
