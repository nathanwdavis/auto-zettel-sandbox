Own-words summary of
[[craig-the-teleological-argument-and-the-anthropic-principle--202609011005]].

Craig's opening concedes the standard story in order to reverse it: "Widely
thought to have been demolished by Hume and Darwin, the teleological
argument for God's existence has nonetheless continued during this century
to find able defenders" (Introduction) -- Tennant, Bertocci, Hackett --
who appealed to a "wider teleology" of the necessary conditions for
intelligent life rather than to particular organisms. What those defenders
lacked, Craig argues, recent physics supplies: his Summary claims that
"The discovery during our generation of the so-called anthropic
coincidences in the initial conditions of the universe has breathed new
life into the teleological argument", and the captured physics section
opens a catalogue of quantitative examples of conditions fine-tuned for
life.

The essay's argumentative core (partly outside the captured excerpts, so
summarized here on the essay's own structure) is a rebuttal of the
Anthropic Principle as an explaining-away device: per the Summary, "Use of
the Anthropic Principle to nullify our wonder at these coincidences is
logically fallacious unless conjoined with the metaphysical hypothesis of
a World Ensemble", and "There are no reasons to believe that such an
Ensemble exists nor that, if it does, it has the properties necessary for
the Anthropic Principle to function." In "The Hypothesis of Divine
Design" he answers the standing objections to theistic design (Earman's
sympathetic but non-committal position included) and concludes they "are
not probative" (Summary).

The Concluding Remarks state his own verdict with a scope worth
preserving: the fine-tuned "delicate balance of cosmological and physical
conditions necessary for intelligent life does cry out for some sort of
interpretation", the WAP-only reply is "simply logically fallacious", and
the World Ensemble alternative has "not only no evidence", but
"substantive objections against each of the proposed means of generating
such an" ensemble. Yet the argument is no shortcut to faith: "The
teleological argument, then, if successful, hardly makes belief in God
pat. Rather it helps to bring us more quickly to the true crisis of
faith" (Concluding Remarks; endnote markers omitted from the quotation).

Scope and caveats: Craig writes as perhaps the most prominent evangelical
philosopher of religion, but this is one essay from 1990 -- it grounds his
endorsement of the fine-tuning form, not a survey of evangelical opinion.
The middle sections (Classical Cosmology, Biochemistry, the Anthropic
Principle exposition and critique, WAP and a World Ensemble) are not
captured and are never quoted here. One captured sentence misprints
"World Ensemble" as "Word Ensemble"; nothing here quotes that sentence.
