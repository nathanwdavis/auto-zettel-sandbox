Own-words summary of [[hodge-systematic-theology-volume-1--202609011033]], from
its **second** capture: the Trinity chapter, which the first capture does not
reach. The repository's other literature note on this source
([[hodge-keeps-the-design-argument-while-denying-it-originates-belief--202609011037]])
summarises Part I chs. I-IV and has nothing to do with this material; the two
notes are separate because they are about separate chapters, not because either
is incomplete.

**The two rejected readings, § 3.** Hodge lays the fourth-century options out
as a pair of failures, and the pair is what matters here.

*Sabellianism* made the three a matter of aspect: "They admitted a modal
trinity. They acknowledged the true divinity of Christ, but denied any personal
distinctions in the Godhead. The same person is at once Father, Son, and Holy
Spirit; these terms expressing the different relations in which God reveals
Himself in the world and in the Church." He records its fate in one sentence:
"Sabellianism was, therefore, soon almost universally rejected." His stated
reason is not philosophical but scriptural and devotional — believers stood in
personal relation to each of the three, and in Scripture the Father addresses
the Son as "Thou".

*Arianism* went the other way, and Hodge traces it through Origen: the Son
"was of a different essence from the Father, *heteros kat' ousian*, and owed
his existence to the will of the Father", which in Origen's disciples became
the assertion that the Son is a creature.

**What Nicaea was convened to do, § 4 A.** Three things: fix the usage of
disputed terms, condemn errors, and frame a statement that would hold all the
scriptural elements together. Hodge dwells on the terminological chaos —
*hupostasis* was being used both for substance (as the Council itself uses it,
synonymously with *ousia*) and for person. The creed he prints has the Son
"begotten and not made, consubstantial with the Father, by whom all things were
made", and closes with the anathema against those who say the Son was made "out
of nothing, or of another or different essence or substance, that He was a
creature, or mutable, or susceptible of change".

**What the councils decided, § 5, and this is the part the comparison needs.**
Against Sabellianism: "These Councils decided that the terms Father, Son, and
Spirit, were not expressive merely of relations *ad extra*, analogous to the
terms, Creator, Preserver, and Benefactor." The three are internal, necessary
and eternal relations in the Godhead, and they are personal — "They differ not
as *allo kai allo*, but as *allos kai allos*; each says I, and each says Thou,
to either of the others."

Against the Arians: "The Councils held that the Father, Son, and Spirit are the
same in substance, and equal in power and glory," so that whatever divine
perfection can be predicated of one can be predicated of the others in the same
sense and measure. And a formulation that bears directly on where the divine
essence is located relative to the three: "It is not the Father as such, nor the
Son as such, who is self-existent, infinite, and eternal, but the Godhead, or
divine essence, which subsists in the three persons."

**Two limits.** First, in this chapter Hodge is doing two things at once —
stating the doctrine he holds, which is what makes this source `primary-text`
here, and narrating conciliar history, which he is a nineteenth-century
secondary reporter of. Where a note built on this one says what a council
decided, it has Hodge's report and not the acts. Second, Hodge is a partisan of
the Nicene outcome and describes the losing positions in the language of the
side that won; the summaries of Sabellius and of Arius above are his, and
nothing here needs them to be fair, only to be an accurate record of the
distinctions the Church doctrine draws.
