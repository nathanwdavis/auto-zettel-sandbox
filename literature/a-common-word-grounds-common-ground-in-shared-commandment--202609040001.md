Own-words summary of [[a-common-word-between-us-and-you--202609032358]].

The letter's stated purpose is peace between the two communities, and its
argument is that the basis for it "already exists" inside both faiths rather
than needing to be negotiated: "love of the One God, and love of the
neighbour." Those two commandments, it says, are "the common ground between
Islam and Christianity," and the invitation it issues is to "come together with
us on the basis of what is common to us, which is also what is most essential
to our faith and practice" (Summary and Abridgement).

The move worth noticing is what the letter does NOT do. It never asks whether
Muslims and Christians worship the same God, and never answers that question in
those terms — the phrase does not occur in the captured text, and neither does
the question in any wording I could find. This is not an omission of
convenience: the document is long, deliberate, addressed precisely to
Christians, and repeatedly quotes the Qur'anic passages that a same-God
argument would turn on. It declines the question and substitutes another one:
what are we each commanded to do, and do those commands coincide?

Its own statement of the relation between agreement and difference is direct
and unsmoothed (part III): "Whilst Islam and Christianity are obviously
different religions—and whilst there is no minimising some of their formal
differences—it is clear that the Two Greatest Commandments are an area of
common ground and a link between the Qur'an, the Torah and the New Testament."
Difference is conceded in the same sentence that asserts commonality, and the
commonality claimed is between *texts and commands*, not between objects of
worship.

Where it does approach divine identity, it approaches it through the
commandments' preface rather than head-on. It observes that what the Two
Commandments "arise out of" in the Torah and the New Testament is "the Unity of
God—that there is only one God," lays the Shema (Deut. 6:4), Jesus' citation of
it (Mark 12:29) and Surah al-Ikhlas (112:1-2) alongside one another, and
concludes that "the Unity of God, love of Him, and love of the neighbour form a
common ground upon which Islam and Christianity (and Judaism) are founded."
That is an argument that the same *doctrine* — divine oneness — stands at the
head of both, which is not the same claim as that the same being is worshipped,
and the letter does not take the further step.

Its central text is Qur'an 3:64, quoted in the letter's own rendering ("Come to
a common word between us and you: that we shall worship none but God, and that
we shall ascribe no partner unto Him, and that none of us shall take others for
lords beside God"). Two features of how it is used are worth recording. First,
the gloss is distributive: "we shall ascribe no partner unto Him" is assigned to
the Unity of God, "worship none but God" to the First Commandment, and — on the
authority of al-Tabari's *Jami' Al-Bayan* (d. 310 A.H. / 923 C.E.) — "none of us
shall take others for lords beside God" is read as "that none of us should obey
in disobedience to what God has commanded," and thence as a claim about justice
and freedom of religion, i.e. the Second Commandment. So a verse that also
carries the anti-association clause is put to work as an invitation. Second,
the letter is reading a classical tafsir, not innovating: the interpretive
authority it invokes is internal to its own tradition.

On Christ it is explicit rather than diplomatic: "Muslims recognize Jesus Christ
as the Messiah, not in the same way Christians do," followed by the Qur'anic
formula from 4:171 — messenger, word cast unto Mary, a spirit from Him. The
parenthesis it adds is pointed ("but Christians themselves anyway have never all
agreed with each other on Jesus Christ's nature"), and it is doing argumentative
work: it treats christological disagreement as a thing that already exists
inside Christianity, and so as a poor criterion of who is outside.

The closing frame is Qur'an 5:48 in the letter's rendering — "Had God willed He
could have made you one community... So vie one with another in good works" —
which sets plurality of law and way as intended rather than as a defect to be
resolved, and relocates the contest onto conduct.

LIMITS. This is an excerpt of the letter, not the whole of it, and the two long
sections working through the commandments inside each scripture are only partly
represented; a claim about the letter's overall balance would need the full
text. The letter is a collective ecumenical address with a stated peacemaking
purpose, which is a genre that has reasons of its own for declining a divisive
question — so "it does not ask the same-God question" is solid, while "Muslim
theology does not ask it" is not something this source can establish, and the
letter is one document rather than a survey of Muslim opinion. Finally, the
letter does not quote Qur'an 29:46 ("our Allah and your Allah is One"), the
verse that speaks most directly to shared divine identity, even though it is the
affirming verse and would have served an argument the letter chose not to make.
