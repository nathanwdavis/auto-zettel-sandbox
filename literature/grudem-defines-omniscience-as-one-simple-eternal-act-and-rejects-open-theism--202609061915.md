The section opens with a one-sentence definition: God fully knows himself and all things actual and possible, in one simple and eternal act. Six sub-sections then take the definition apart clause by clause.

**Self-knowledge (a).** God knows himself completely, which Grudem treats as remarkable because God's being is infinite, so that only an infinite knower could know it exhaustively. The scriptural warrant is the Spirit's searching "the depths of God" (1 Cor. 2:10–11) and the "no darkness at all" of 1 John 1:5, read as full self-awareness as well as moral purity.

**The actual (b).** Everything that exists and everything that happens, including the future (Isa. 46:9–10) and the smallest details of individual lives (Matt. 6:8; 10:30; Ps. 139).

**The possible (c).** Grudem gives two grounds. The first is scriptural: cases where God states what would happen and it does not, because the people involved act on the information — Saul's coming to Keilah and the Keilahites' surrender of David (1 Sam. 23:11–13), Tyre and Sidon repenting had they seen Jesus' miracles, Sodom standing to this day (Matt. 11:21, 23), and Elisha's arrows (2 Kings 13:19). The second is a deduction: knowing himself fully, God knows everything he is able to do, and that is the whole of what is possible. Grudem illustrates with the kinds of animals God could have created and did not, of each of which God knows what it would have been like and what would have happened in it.

**Simple and eternal (d).** "Simple" is glossed, as Grudem says it was earlier, as "not divided into parts", and is then explained entirely in terms of awareness: God never has to count, recall, reason to a conclusion or ponder before answering, never learns, never forgets; all his knowledge is always fully present in his consciousness and never fades into nonconscious memory. "Eternal" means the knowledge never changes or grows, since learning anything would mean not having been omniscient before. Two objections are answered: God's "forgetting" of sins (Isa. 43:25) is read relationally, and Jeremiah's "nor did it come into my mind" about child sacrifice (Jer. 7:31) is read with the KJV as "nor did it enter into my heart", that is, not desired.

**Open theism (e).** Grudem states the difficulty (if God knows every future event, how are choices free?) and describes open theism as the conclusion that God does not know the future free acts of creatures, so that the future is "open" even to God. He quotes Richard Rice, noting that Rice keeps the word "omniscience" by redefining it as knowledge of "everything logically knowable" and then excluding future free decisions from that class; and Clark Pinnock, who says he accepted the Calvinist inference from exhaustive foreknowledge to the fixity of all things, "could not shake off its logical force", and therefore gave up exhaustive foreknowledge. Grudem's reply has three steps. First, the position makes God ignorant of every future human choice, and so of every event of human history, since each turns on choices. Second, this contradicts the Bible's unfailing predictive prophecy, whose fulfilment "demonstrates that God is the true God". Third, following Bruce Ware's *God's Lesser Glory*, he sets out five passages from Isaiah 40–48 in which God distinguishes himself from idols precisely by declaring beforehand what is to come (Isa. 41:23, 26, 29; 42:8–9; 44:6–7; 44:24–26, 28; 46:9–10), and quotes Ware's conclusion that to deny foreknowledge is to deny the very basis on which God says his claim to deity will be vindicated. He adds Steven C. Roy's count of 4,017 biblical prophecies, 2,323 of them concerning free human decisions, which Scripture presents as firm declarations and not as estimates of what is likely.

**Freedom (f).** Deferring the full treatment to the chapter on providence, Grudem adopts Augustine's phrase "reasonable self-determination", valued because it avoids the word "free": our choices are reasoned and they really determine what happens. He then concedes the entailment Pinnock felt: if God knows all our thoughts, words and actions long before they occur, "there must be some sense in which our choices are not absolutely free". Freedom, if the word is wanted, is Ware's "freedom of inclination", choosing according to one's strongest inclination or deepest desire.
