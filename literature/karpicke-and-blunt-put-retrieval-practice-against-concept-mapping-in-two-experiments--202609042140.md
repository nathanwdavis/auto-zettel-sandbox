Summary of
[[karpicke-and-blunt-retrieval-practice-produces-more-learning-than-concept-mapping--202609042130]]
in my own words. The article was read in full from the author's lab copy; the
verbatim numbers live in the excerpt capture, and this note reports the design
and the reported outcomes, not internal argumentation beyond what the paper
states of itself.

**The question the paper set.** Retrieval practice was already known to beat
plain rereading (this base's Roediger & Karpicke 2006). Karpicke and Blunt asked
a harder question: does retrieval beat an *elaborative* study activity that is
itself active and effortful — building a concept map of the text? If "active
learning" were the operative variable, the two should tie; if there are
retrieval-specific mechanisms, retrieval should win.

**Experiment 1 (80 undergraduates, between-subjects, four conditions).**
Students read a science text once, then were assigned to study-once, repeated
study (four study periods), elaborative concept mapping (study then draw a
concept map while viewing the text), or retrieval practice (study, free-recall
the text from memory with no feedback, restudy, recall again). Study time was
matched between the mapping and retrieval conditions, and the two produced
near-identical amounts during initial learning (0.78 mapped vs 0.81 recalled,
not significant), so the final-test difference cannot be an artifact of unequal
initial learning. One week later, on a short-answer test with verbatim and
inference questions, retrieval practice produced the best learning: collapsed
across question type, M = 0.67 for retrieval vs M = 0.45 for concept mapping —
about a 50% improvement, d = 1.50. Concept mapping was not reliably better than
simply spending more time reading.

**Experiment 2 (120 students, within-subject).** Each student mapped one text
and retrieved another, across two text structures (enumeration and sequence),
with half taking a final short-answer test and half a final test that asked them
to *build a concept map from memory*. Here the initial-learning imbalance ran
the other way — students produced more ideas on the initial maps than on the
initial recall (0.74 vs 0.65) — so the deck was, if anything, stacked toward
mapping. Retrieval still won decisively: d = 1.07 collapsed across text formats,
and d = 1.01 even on the final concept-map test, the format most similar to the
mapping condition's own activity. Per subject, 101 of 120 (84%) did better after
retrieval practice.

**The metacognitive reversal, in both experiments.** Students' predictions ran
opposite to their results. In Experiment 1 they predicted repeated study would
be best and retrieval worst; in Experiment 2, 90 of 120 (75%) believed concept
mapping would be as good as or better than retrieval. It was not.

**Scope, for this base.** What was measured is one-week retention of short
science prose passages by undergraduates, retrieval done *without feedback* but
followed by restudy. The authors read the result as support for
retrieval-specific mechanisms (improved cue diagnosticity) over elaboration, and
frame concept mapping as elaborative only because it was done in the presence of
the text. Nothing here concerns note-taking, external memory, or horizons beyond
a week — the same scope limits the base already records for the rest of the
retrieval-practice cluster.
