Own-words summary of [[new-life-in-christ-salvation-in-orthodox-theology--202610081426]].

Meyendorff states the Orthodox account in three steps.

What is inherited (p. 493). "It has been often recognized that Eastern
patristic thought ignores the notion of a transmission" of guilt from Adam
to his descendants. "However, it does not ignore the very fact of cosmic
fallenness." That fallenness is not divine punishment passed down to all
humans ("the Augustinian massa damnata") but the tyranny of Satan and the
reign of death over creation (Jn 8:44; Heb 2:15). "What is being
transmitted from parents to children is not sin but mortality and slavery",
and in that condition sin becomes inevitable. He supports this from
Theodoret ("mortal beings are necessarily subject to passions and fears")
and Theodore of Mopsuestia ("By becoming mortal, we acquired greater urge
to sin"), and says the Greek Fathers read Romans 5:12 in Greek and "were
not conditioned by the Latin mistranslation, which implied that all sinned
"in Adam."" His footnote 45 sets out the grammar: the Latin in quo makes
"all have sinned in Adam", but "The Greek does not allow for such a
meaning". If eph' ho is neuter and means "because", the verse "defines
death as the punishment for individual sins of any human (not "original"
sin)"; if masculine, it refers to "death" (thanatos), "so that death—as a
cosmic, personalized reality—becomes the cause of individual human sins."
He cites his Byzantine Theology pp. 143-46 for the problem.

What Christ assumed (pp. 492-94). "Christ was not a human hypostasis." The
humanity he took is "our common humanity which he assumed in its fallen
state and glorified through his death and resurrection." "It is the mortal,
corruptible, and fallen humanity which was assumed by the Logos", as
Athanasius and Cyril understood: the Logos "voluntarily assumed mortal
humanity and therefore had to die in the flesh." The church's rejection of
Julian of Halicarnassus, who argued that "since death and corruption
(phthora) are consequences of human sin, they could not have been present
in Jesus, who did not sin", shows that the high Christology does not shrink
Christ's humanity. God "willed precisely that, as man, Jesus, since his
conception in the womb of Mary, would be fully conditioned by what our
human, fallen existence is: he lived in time, "grew in wisdom," did not
know, suffered, and died." Footnote 47: "mortality and corruptibility were
assumed by the Son of God from the beginning of his human life."

Why he did not sin (p. 495). The humanity assumed "was our "corruptible"
humanity which needed salvation", and Jesus was tempted in every respect
"yet without sin" (Heb 4:15). Sin is "a conscious, personal act of
rebellion against divine will", and "It is because he was God, not as "mere
man," that Jesus was able to overcome the temptations inherent in fallen
humanity".

For the fuller argument Meyendorff refers the reader (n. 49) to his
"Christ's Humanity: The Paschal Mystery", St. Vladimir's Theological
Quarterly 31 (1987) 5-40, which is not on file.
