Own-words summary of [[michaelson-reference-sep--202609041921]].

**The causal theory, stated in two parts.** The entry's formulation is that "a
name refers to whatever is linked to it in the appropriate way -- a way that
does not require speakers to associate any identifying descriptive content
whatsoever with the name." It then splits the mechanism: "one dealing with
reference fixing, the other dealing with reference borrowing." Fixing is a
dubbing -- "a language user gives a name to an object by saying something like
'You are to be called N'" -- and after that "the name gets passed on from
speaker to speaker through communicative exchanges." Later users refer because
"underlying their uses of the name are links in a causal chain stretching back
to the initial dubbing of the object with that name." The competence bar is
deliberately low: "borrowers needn't be able to identify any of the lenders
they are in fact relying on."

**Four models, not two.** The entry closes its survey by naming four distinct
answers to what it calls the metasemantics of referential terms: the
*descriptivist* model (words refer "in virtue of being associated with a
specific descriptive contents that serve to identify particular objects"); the
*causal* model (in virtue of "chains of use leading back to an initiating use
or 'baptism'"); the *character* model (in virtue of "being associated with
regular rules of reference"); and the *intentionalist* model (in virtue of
"being used, intentionally, to refer to particular objects").

**And the four are not rivals in the way a dispute needs them to be.** The
entry says so directly: "these four models aren't strictly exclusive of each
other." It gives two reductions as examples -- classical descriptivism may be
read as "a particular kind of intentionalism," and "one might view the causal
theory as a special case of the character theory."
