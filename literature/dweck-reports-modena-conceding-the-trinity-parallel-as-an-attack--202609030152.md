Own-words summary of [[dweck-the-scandal-of-kabbalah--202609030150]].

Read for one question — what Jews said back to the Christian kabbalists — the
introduction supplies both the setting and one Jewish answer, and the answer is
not the expected one.

The setting (p. 9). Pico "drew on Kabbalah as a component of his ancient
theology", on the view that "a single truth united all periods and cultures";
he was "only the most prominent in a long line of non-Jewish intellectuals who
studied Kabbalah, including Johannes Reuchlin, Pietro Galatino, and many
others". Dweck refuses to read the exchange as scholarly traffic: Christians
"drew on Kabbalah when preaching to potential Jewish converts", and the whole
appropriation "was hardly a disinterested intellectual exchange", being
"fraught with conversionary pressures and uneven power relations".

The respondent (p. 10). Leon Modena's *Ari Nohem* (Venice, 1639) is the first
sustained Jewish critique of Kabbalah. Dweck is careful about its temper:
Modena "criticized Kabbalah to diminish its status, not to destroy it", "paid
kabbalists the devastating compliment of taking their arguments seriously and
refuting them one by one", and "repeatedly extolled the virtues of the Zohar as
a reservoir of homiletic insight" — while dating Kabbalah's core documents to
"only the late Middle Ages" and identifying the Zohar as "the pseudepigraphic
creation of Moses de Leon and his circle".

The answer, and its shape (p. 11). Against kabbalists who "branded as heretics
anyone who denied" the centrality of the sefirot, "Modena repudiated this claim
and leveled a severe countercharge of his own: after an examination of the
sefirot as a concept, he concluded that it pointed to a plurality within God
similar to the Christian doctrine of the Trinity."

What to carry forward, and what not to. The move is a concession turned into a
weapon: Modena does not deny the resemblance the Christian kabbalists asserted,
he grants it and treats it as disqualifying — which is available to him
precisely because he is attacking Kabbalah rather than defending it. Dweck's
own frame for the book is Funkenstein's "counterhistory", a method that
"systematically exploited the sources of his opponents".

The limit is strict and belongs in every note built on this. **This is Dweck
reporting Modena's conclusion; *Ari Nohem* is not in this repository.** Nothing
here establishes how Modena argued from the sefirot to the parallel, which
sefirotic doctrine he examined, or whether he was answering Pico specifically
rather than Christian kabbalism at large — Dweck's introduction names Pico as
the setting and does not say Modena replied to him by name.
