**Grounding: raw/202609151853-calvin-institutes-iii-21-of-the-eternal-election.html, the whole chapter as transcribed by CCEL; sections 1-4 read entire, sections 5-7 (the doctrine itself) only skimmed and not used.**

**Against curiosity (secs. 1-2).** The chapter opens by naming the danger before the doctrine: predestination is made "perilous by human curiosity, which cannot be restrained from wandering into forbidden paths and climbing to the clouds determined if it can that none of the secret things of God shall remain unexplored." The inquirer who presses on will "enter in inextricable labyrinth", because "it is not right that man should with impunity pry into things which the Lord has been pleased to conceal within himself, and scan that sublime eternal wisdom which it is his pleasure that we should not apprehend but adore". Then the criterion of what has been disclosed: "Those secrets of his will, which he has seen it meet to manifest, are revealed in his word—revealed in so far as he knew to be conducive to our interest and welfare." Section 2 quotes Augustine quoting John 16:12 ("I have yet many things to say unto you, but ye cannot bear them now") for the same point, and draws the rule that "the moment we go beyond the bounds of the word we are out of the course, in darkness", ending with the sentence that names the posture: "Let us not be ashamed to be ignorant in a matter in which ignorance is learning."

**Against false modesty (secs. 3-4).** The second class of readers would suppress the doctrine altogether, and Calvin will not allow them either. Scripture "is the school of the Holy Spirit, in which as nothing useful and necessary to be known has been omitted, so nothing is taught but what it is of importance to know", so the Christian may open his mind "to all the words of God which are addressed to him, provided he do it with this moderation—viz. that whenever the Lord shuts his sacred mouth, he also desists from inquiry. The best rule of sobriety is, not only in learning to follow wherever God leads, but also when he makes an end of teaching, to cease also from wishing to be wise." Prov. 25:2 ("It is the glory of God to conceal a thing") is cited and immediately bounded: it "is not to be understood of every thing", or modesty becomes "a brutish ignorance". The distinction that settles it "is clearly expressed by Moses in a few words", and Deut. 29:29 is quoted in full, with the gloss that Moses "exhorts the people to study the doctrine of the law in accordance with a heavenly decree, because God has been pleased to promulgate it, while he at the same time confines them within these boundaries, for the simple reason that it is not lawful for men to pry into the secret things of God." Section 4 states the outcome as a "general rule, that the secret things of God are not to be scrutinized, and that those which he has revealed are not to be overlooked, lest we may, on the one hand, be chargeable with curiosity, and, on the other, with ingratitude", and adds Augustine's image of Scripture, which "walks softly, as with a mother's step, in accommodation to our weakness".

**What the verse does for Calvin.** It is used symmetrically, once against each class of reader, and it is read of knowledge throughout: the "secret things" are the unrevealed counsel of God about election, the "revealed" things are what the word says of it. Calvin adds two things the verse does not contain. A criterion of selection, welfare: the revealed is what God "knew to be conducive to our interest and welfare". And a duty on the hidden side, adoration in place of apprehension. He also supplies a name for the right relation to the hidden tier, an ignorance that is learning, which is the Latin *docta ignorantia* in Beveridge's English.

**Limits.** Beveridge's translation; the base's other Calvin capture is Allen's, and wording differs between them. Calvin's subject is predestination specifically, and the fence he builds is for that doctrine; whether he would apply the same rule to every hidden thing is not said here.
