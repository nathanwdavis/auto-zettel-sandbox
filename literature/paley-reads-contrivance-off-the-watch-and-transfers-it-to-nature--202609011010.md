Own-words summary of [[paley-natural-theology--202609011004]].

Chapter 1 opens with the famous asymmetry. A stone found on a heath needs no
special account -- "for any thing I knew to the contrary, it had lain there
for ever" -- but a watch does, because inspection shows "that its several
parts are framed and put together for a purpose" (p. 2): wheels, spring, and
glass adjusted so that motion measures time. From that observed adjustment of means to an end,
"the inference, we think, is inevitable, that the watch must have had a
maker: that there must have existed, at some time, and at some place or
other, an artificer or artificers who formed it for the purpose which we
find it actually to answer; who comprehended its construction, and designed
its use" (pp. 1-4). The rest of the chapter clears objections in advance:
the inference stands even if we never saw a watch made, even if the watch
sometimes goes wrong, even if some parts' functions are unknown.

Chapter 2 raises the stakes with a thought experiment: suppose the watch
turned out to possess "the unexpected property of producing, in the course
of its movement, another watch like itself" (p. 8). Self-reproduction,
Paley argues, would increase our admiration for the contriver, not
substitute for one -- a chain of reproducing watches, however long, never
explains the contrivance it transmits. To stop at the series and refuse a
designer: "Yet this is atheism" (p. 17, the chapter's closing verdict).

Chapter 3 makes the transfer that carries the whole book: "every indication
of contrivance, every manifestation of design, which existed in the watch,
exists in the works of nature; with the difference, on the side of nature,
of being greater and more, and that in a degree which exceeds all
computation" (p. 17-18). The worked example is the eye compared with the
telescope -- "there is precisely the same proof that the eye was made for
vision, as there is that the telescope was made for assisting it" -- and
the chapter dwells on anatomical detail (lens curvature differences between
fish and land animals, the eye's adjusting mechanisms) as observed
contrivance.

Scope and caveats: only chs. 1-3 are captured; Paley's later chapters
(succession of plants and animals, the attributes of the Deity) are not
summarized here. Quotations anchor on the Darwin Online transcription of
the 1809 12th edition and are cross-checked against the 1879 Sheldon OCR
witness, which confirms the quoted wording modulo OCR noise and the
editions' punctuation accidentals (e.g. "inevitable," vs "inevitable ;").
