**The question both schools answer.** Pohle defines Molinism as "one of the systems which purpose to reconcile grace and free will," opposed by the Thomism of Banez, and frames a single question for both: "Whence does efficacious grace (gratia efficax), which includes in its very concept the actual free consent of the will, derive its infallible effect; and how is it that, in spite of the infallible efficacy of grace, the freedom of the will is not impaired?" Every Catholic answer "must safeguard two principles: first, the supremacy and causality of grace (against Pelagianism and Semipelagianism), and second, the unimpaired freedom of consent in the will (against early Protestantism and Jansenism)." The two schools start from opposite ends, Thomism from efficacious grace and Molinism from "the rather clear concept of freedom"; he expected them to meet "As in the tunnelling of a mountain," found that "they passed each other along parallel lines," and attributes the failure to the subject: "The problem seems to lie so far beyond the horizon of the human mind, that man will never be able fully to penetrate its mystery."

**Freedom, and the Thomist charge.** Freedom is "the power of the will to act or not to act, to act this or that way," as against necessary causes. Pohle reports the Thomists' accusation that the Molinists, "by their undue exaltation of man's freedom of will, seriously circumscribe and diminish the supremacy of the Creator over His creatures," since if the free will decides whether a grace is efficacious "the result of the salutary act must be attributed to man and not to God," against 1 Corinthians 4:7 and Philippians 2:13.

**The medium of foreknowledge.** The reply "leads us to the very heart of Molina's system": even Molinism admits that God's decree to give an efficacious grace "involves with metaphysical certainty the free co-operation of the will," so God "must possess some infallible source of knowledge" of whether the will is going to co-operate. The controversy therefore "resolves itself into a discussion on the foreknowledge which God has of the free future acts," and both systems face one question: "What is the medium of knowledge (medium in quo) in which God foresees the (absolute or conditioned) free operations of His rational creatures?" The Thomists answer that "God foresees the (absolute and conditioned) free acts of man in the eternal decrees of His own will," which produce those acts by physical premotion as predeterminations to one; the Molinists that predetermining decrees "must logically and necessarily destroy freedom and lead to Determinism," so that "these decrees must presuppose a special knowledge (scientia media), in the light of which God infallibly foresees from all eternity what attitude man's will would in any conceivable combination of circumstances assume if this or that particular grace were offered it." The worked example is Peter and Judas: foreseeing "that St. Peter, after his denial of Christ, shall freely co-operate with a certain grace, He decrees to give him this particular grace and none other"; for Judas, "foreseeing the future resistance of this Apostle to a certain grace of conversion," God "decreed to allow it" and gave a grace "really sufficient, but remained inefficacious solely on account of the refractory disposition of the Apostle's will." The principle Molinism is pledged to: "The knowledge of God precedes as a guiding light the decree of His will, and His will is in no way the source of His knowledge." It was because their scientia media was "a knowledge independent of any decrees, that they were most sharply assailed by the Thomists."

**The three knowledges, and Tyre and Sidon.** Molina took the idea from his teacher Fonseca, "who called it scientia mixta," and justified it thus: besides "the Divine knowledge of the purely possible (scientia simplicis intelligentiœ) and the knowledge of the actually existing (scientia visionis), there must be a third kind of 'intermediate knowledge', which embraces all objects that are found neither in the region of pure possibility nor strictly in that of actuality, but partake equally of both extremes and in some sort belong to both kinds of knowledge." Its objects are conditional future free acts, "futuribilis." By it "Christ, for example, was able to declare with certainty to His obstinate hearers that the inhabitants of Tyre and Sidon would have done penance in sackcloth and ashes, if they had witnessed the signs and miracles which were wrought in Corozain and Bethsaida (cf. Matthew 11:21 sq.)."

**Congruism.** The Society did not bind itself to every assertion of the *Concordia*; the modification called Congruism, "which owes its final form to the joint labours of Bellarmine, Francisco Suárez, Vasquez, and Lessius," names the grace that produces its effect "in the light of the scientia media with infallible certainty" a *gratia congrua*, "a grace accommodated to circumstances," after Augustine's "quomodo scit ei congruere."

**Gaps.** The Thomist position is present here only as Pohle reports it; no text of Banez is on file. The article is a century old and knows nothing of the analytic revival of the doctrine, so it has no vocabulary of counterfactuals or grounding. Its later sections, on the history of Molinism after the Congregatio and on sufficient grace, and its long bibliography are captured but not summarized here. The Catholic dogmatic frame, in which both freedom and the priority of grace are Tridentine definitions, is Pohle's and is not shared by the evangelical sources this cluster otherwise rests on.
