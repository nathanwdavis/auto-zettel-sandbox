Own-words summary of [[behe-molecular-machines--202609011400]], Behe's
self-described overview of *Darwin's Black Box*.

The essay's thesis is announced up front: modern biochemistry shows "that
life at its most fundamental level is irreducibly complex and that such
complexity is incompatible with undirected evolution" ("Darwinism's
Prosperity"). Behe stakes the argument on Darwin's own falsification
criterion from the *Origin* -- "If it could be demonstrated that any complex
organ existed which could not possibly have been formed by numerous,
successive, slight modifications, my theory would absolutely break down" --
and defines a system meeting that criterion as irreducibly complex: "a
single system which is composed of several interacting parts that contribute
to the basic function, and where the removal of any one of the parts causes
the system to effectively cease functioning." The load-bearing move follows
immediately: "any precursor to an irreducibly complex system is by
definition nonfunctional", and "Since natural selection requires a function
to select, an irreducibly complex biological system, if there is such a
thing, would have to arise as an integrated unit" ("Irreducible
Complexity"). The household mousetrap (base, hammer, spring, catch, holding
bar) shows such systems exist at all; the cilium is his flagship biological
case: at least a half dozen required proteins, one function, and -- because "The components of
cilia are single molecules" -- "no more black boxes to invoke; the
complexity of the cilium is final, fundamental." The inference is stated as
a chain: no functional precursors, so no path for a mechanism that
"requires a continuum of function to work"; "Natural selection is powerless
when there is no function to select. We can go further and say that, if the
cilium can not be produced by natural selection, then the cilium was
designed" ("Molecular Machines").

The design conclusion itself is presented as ordinary inference, not
revelation: "Design is simply the purposeful arrangement of parts", and the
conclusion "flows naturally from the data itself-not from sacred books or
sectarian beliefs" ("Detection of Design"). Behe closes by insisting the
argument runs on knowledge rather than ignorance: "we are not inferring
design from what we do not know, but from what we do know. We are not
inferring design to account for a black box, but to account for an open
box" ("Conclusion").

Two scope-limits Behe himself draws, worth recording because critics often
elide them: intelligent design "does not try to explain everything", and
"The fact that some biochemical systems were designed by an intelligent
agent does not mean that any of the other factors are not operative,
common, or important" -- common descent, selection, drift, and gene flow
included ("Detection of Design").

Scope and caveats: this is one side's own statement of its argument, tiered
primary-text and hosted by Behe's own institute; it reports no opposing
case, and its empirical claims (e.g. that the cilium has no functional
precursors, and the essay's uncaptured survey of the *Journal of Molecular
Evolution*) are cited here as Behe's claims, not adjudicated findings. The
sections on the history of vision research, proteins, the
Haeckel/spontaneous-generation analogy, the protein-transport example, and
the JME survey are outside the captured excerpts and are cited only as
attributed summary, never quoted. The essay's closing verdict -- "The
theory of undirected evolution is already dead" -- long predates, and is
contested by, the intra-evangelical exchange documented in
[[venema-finds-no-thorough-search-behind-signatures-design-inference--202609011315]].
