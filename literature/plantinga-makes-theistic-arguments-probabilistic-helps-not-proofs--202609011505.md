Own-words summary of [[plantinga-two-dozen-theistic-arguments--202609011500]],
Plantinga's 1986 lecture notes as published in 2007.

The whole exercise sits inside a stated epistemology: Plantinga opens the
1986 notes by repeating that "belief in God is properly basic", then
immediately adds, "But it doesn't follow, of course, that there aren't any
good arguments. Are there some? At least a couple of dozen or so" (p. 210).
The 2006 preface holds the same two thoughts at once -- belief needs no
argument "for justification or rationality or (if true) warrant", and yet
"there are good theistic arguments -- at least two dozen or so", though as
stated they are "merely argument sketches, or maybe only pointers to good
arguments" (p. 203). What the arguments are good *for* is the headnote's
real contribution. They "are not coercive in the sense that every person is
obliged to accept their premises on pain of irrationality"; they are
"probabilistic, either with respect to the premises or with respect to the
connection between the premises and conclusion, or both"; and their office
is that "They can serve to bolster and confirm ('helps' à la John Calvin),
perhaps to convince" (p. 210). He even warns that argument can run
backwards: confronted with a valid argument against something you know, you
may give up a premise, so an argument can "reduce someone from knowledge to
ignorance" (p. 210). The headnote closes with a compressed reading of the
teleological tradition's oldest text: "Add Aquinas's fifth way: this is
really an argument from proper function, I think" (p. 210) -- on which see
[[the-fifth-way-infers-a-director-from-regularity-not-contrivance--202609011211]].

Of the lettered sketches, the two developed design entries are (E) and (F)
-- a third teleological heading, (G) "Tony Kenny's Style of Teleological
Argument", stands with no body text at all, a measure of how nascent the
notes are.
Section (E), the argument from physical constants, assembles the fine-tuning
data by quotation (Carr and Rees's "apparent 'coincidences' among the
physical constants", Hawking on the expansion rate, Davies's "very special
place") and then spends its argumentative effort rebutting two deflationary
replies: against the reply that any outcome would have been equally
improbable, he sets the poker dealer who keeps dealing himself aces -- "Would that explanation play in
Dodge City (or Tombstone)?" -- and against the anthropic reply he answers,
"Again, this seems right, but how is it relevant? What does it explain?"
The section's whole positive case is its last sentence: "From a theistic
point of view, however, no mystery at all and an easy explanation" (pp.
215-16). Section (F), the "naive" teleological argument, is not an
inference at all on his telling: quoting Swinburne's regularity data,
Newton, and Hume's Cleanthes, its result is that "Contemplating these
things can result in a strong impulse to believe that the universe was
indeed designed -- by God", and he suggests Hume's version may really be a
different kind of "argument" -- one that puts the arguee where the Sensus
Divinitatis operates (pp. 216-17; the echo of Calvin is explicit in both
places, see
[[calvin-pairs-an-inner-sense-with-an-outer-display-of-god--202609011036]]).

Scope and caveats: the capture is bounded -- preface opening, headnote,
and sections (E) and (F) only -- so the other twenty-four sketches
(including the epistemological arguments where Plantinga's own
proper-function design argument lives, section (J)) are cited only as
attributed summary, never quoted. The notes are, by the author's own 2006
description, unrevised sketches that "await that loving development to
become genuinely good" (p. 203): this is a considered private assessment
that Plantinga chose to publish, not a worked-out position paper, and the
preface's account of what makes an argument good (pp. 203-209, uncaptured)
qualifies everything the headnote says compactly.
