Own-words summary of [[blair-note-taking-as-an-art-of-transmission--202609032100]].

Blair treats note taking as a historical practice rather than a technique to
be recommended, and her interest is in what the practice does inside the
circulation of knowledge. Her opening move (p. 85) is to call note taking a
central but usually invisible stage in the transmission of knowledge: notes
taken from reading feed a person's own later writing and conversation, others
draw on that in turn, and the cycle carries knowledge forward while
transforming it. She is careful that the ordinary case is transmission *within*
one person — from the moment of reading to a later moment of use — with
sharing between people the wider and rarer case.

Underneath the many historical forms, she proposes that note taking reduces to
four basic operations, the "four Ss": storing, sorting, summarizing, and
selecting (p. 85). The four are offered as an analytic frame for comparing
practices across media and centuries, not as a method anyone followed. Worth
noting for anyone citing them: her closing restatement (p. 107) gives the same
four in a different order, so the p. 85 ordering is the one to quote.

On organisation, she distinguishes two European methods (p. 87): notes kept in
the order of the text they came from (*adversaria*), and notes selected and
then "sorted under a thematic or topical heading to facilitate retrieval" — the
commonplace tradition, which Bacon preferred. The purpose she attaches to
topical filing is explicitly retrieval.

The material with the most direct bearing on slip-based systems is her account
of notes changing hands (pp. 104-5). Early modern notes were bequeathed in
wills, fought over in disputed legacies, and sometimes bought; professional
readers were hired to take them for someone else; and Blair's judgement is that
in all these cases notes taken by one person were presumed useful to another,
consultable "in a predictable way" and applicable to purposes of their own —
even though the new owner had no memory of the reading that produced the note
and had no intention of memorising it. Systematic method, she argues, is what
made this possible: it let note taking be delegated and "almost mechanized."
Her example of the form pushed furthest is Vincent Placcius's *scrinium
literatum* of 1689, a closet of slips hung on hooks behind swivelling slats
labelled by topical heading, which Placcius advertised precisely because it let
a group pool their notes and work on different parts of a shared task.

Her closing argument (pp. 106-7) is the one she flags as most significant, and
it is a claim about what the note is *for*. Alongside Leibniz on mechanising
calculation, she traces a shift in what note takers thought memory was owed:
Drexel and his contemporaries saw no harm in memorising one's notes, whereas by
1920 Chavigny was recommending notes on index cards as a "personal memory on
paper" on the express grounds that "too much memorizing can be harmful to the
higher intellectual qualities." The memory function is handed to paper on
purpose. Blair says plainly that she cannot yet trace this transformation in
detail — a hedge worth preserving in anything built on it.

She then supplies her own limit (p. 107). If every text one wanted were
always available to be searched afresh, "the note itself, the selection made
for later reuse, might play a less prominent role" — she observes that websites
are already treated this way. That sentence also gives the compact definition
of a note she works with: a selection made for later reuse. Her counterweight
is that judgement still applies only to what is present to mind, so notes must
be brought back into active memory to be used in an argument.

Two limits on what this note can carry. Blair is a historian of early modern
information management, and the twentieth-century material (Chavigny, the index
card) is the thin end of her evidence and is presented as a sketch, by her own
statement. And she nowhere discusses one-idea-per-note atomicity, note titles,
or anything resembling a compounding effect from accumulated notes; the
capture was read for those and they are not there.
