Own-words summary of
[[sparrow-liu-and-wegner-google-effects-on-memory--202609041800]], the paper
that gave the phrase "Google effect on memory" to the popular literature and,
through it, most of the confidence with which people say that writing something
down means not remembering it. The whole four-page article was read; the
`raw/` capture is excerpts, for the licence reason its reference note gives.
Quotations are the authors' words; everything else is this note's
characterisation.

**Why this repository wanted it.** It is the first source here that measures
*external memory* rather than learning. Four retrieval-practice sources were
read on 2026-09-04 and, as
[[karpickes-survey-vouches-for-the-effect-and-never-mentions-notes--202609040330]]
records, none of them mentions notes or offloading at all. This one is about
nothing else.

**The single most important structural fact, and it is not in the abstract.**
The paper contains two different research programmes wearing one title. The
abstract runs them together — "when faced with difficult questions, people are
primed to think about computers and ... when people expect to have future
access to information, they have lower rates of recall of the information
itself and enhanced recall instead for where to access it" — but the first
clause is Experiment 1 alone and the second is Experiments 2 through 4. The
authors themselves keep them apart in the closing discussion, assigning each
experiment its own job: people "rapidly think of computers when they find they
need knowledge (experiment 1)"; they "forget items they think will be available
externally and remember items they think will not be available (experiments 2
and 3)"; and they are "better able to remember where an item has been stored
than the identity of the item itself (experiment 4)". Anyone citing "the Google
effect" is citing one of three findings and usually does not say which.

**Experiment 1 — priming.** Participants answered a block of easy and a block
of hard yes/no trivia questions, each followed by a colour-naming Stroop task
over computer words and general words. The prediction is a slowing on computer
words after the hard block. Two paired *t*-tests over the complete word set came
out significant, reported as `t(68) = 3.26` after hard questions and
`t(68) = 2.98` after easy ones. The headline *interaction* — the result the
paper actually rests on, that the computer-word cost is bigger after hard
questions — is reported not over the whole word set but over four words:
Google and Yahoo against Target and Nike, `F(1,66) = 5.02, P < 0.03`.

**Experiment 2 — offloading, and the one number in this paper that bears
directly on a slip box.** Sixty participants read forty trivia statements and
typed each one in; half were told the computer would save what they typed, half
that it would be erased, and half of each group were additionally told to try
to remember. Recall was better in the erased conditions (0.31 and 0.29) than in
the saved ones (0.22 and 0.19), omnibus `F(3, 56) = 2.80, P < 0.05`. The
instruction to remember did nothing. The authors' reading: "Participants
apparently did not make the effort to remember when they thought they could
later look up the trivia statements they had read."

**Experiment 3 — the same effect in recognition, and its mirror image.**
Twenty-eight participants, each statement marked saved, saved-to-a-named-folder,
or erased. Recognition of the statement's exact wording was best for erased
items (0.93 against 0.88 and 0.85). Memory for *whether* an item had been saved
ran the other way: saved items were correctly classified more often (0.61 and
0.66) than erased ones (0.51). The authors' summary is the paper's cleanest
sentence: "believing that one won't have access to the information in the
future enhances memory for the information itself, whereas believing the
information was saved externally enhances memory for the fact that the
information could be accessed, at least in general."

**Experiment 4 — the famous one, and the one the authors trust least.**
Thirty-two participants, everything saved to a named folder, then free recall
of the statements and cued recall of the folders. Folder recall (0.49) beat
statement recall (0.23), `t(31) = 6.70, P < 0.001`. This is the result that
became "we remember where, not what". Its authors immediately qualify it in
their own text: "Participants did have a cue to memory (a word from the trivia
statement) with the folder recall that the statements themselves did not have.
We were not able to counterbalance the trivia and the folders trials such that
the folders were as numerous as the statements, which would be necessary to
counterbalance the uncued and cued recall tasks." They then run an if/then
analysis showing participants were as likely to recall the folder without the
statement (0.30) as to recall nothing (0.38), and call the whole thing
"preliminary evidence".

**What is not in this paper.** No delay: every measurement is taken in the same
session as the exposure. No accumulation and no linking: the material is forty
unconnected trivia statements, and nothing in the design lets an item be
related to another item. No writing in the participant's own words — typing a
sentence verbatim is the *opposite* of the reformulation that
[[mueller-and-oppenheimer-attribute-the-longhand-advantage-to-not-transcribing--202609040200]]
records as the proposed mechanism of note-taking. So the gap that
[[the-note-taking-experiments-do-not-reach-the-slip-box-claims--202609040215]]
names is not closed by this source either; what changes is that the gap is now
narrower on one side, because at least the *subject* is external storage.

where the sample sizes and the full method live — was not fetched. Everything
above comes from the article body, except where this repository reports what

[[hesselmann-no-conclusive-evidence-of-a-google-stroop-effect--202609041805]]
