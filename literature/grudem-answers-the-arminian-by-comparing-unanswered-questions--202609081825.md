Grudem's opponents are known here only as he quotes them: Cottrell, Pinnock, Marshall, Basinger, Craig and Frame are all cited from his footnotes and none has been read directly by this repository.**

**The position stated (F).** Grudem gives the Arminian view an unusually long and courteous hearing — he names Pinnock's two edited volumes "excellent defenses," calls Cottrell's essay "the most comprehensive and persuasive" of them, and prefaces his objection to Craig by noting a forty-year friendship and calling what follows "a difference between friends." The position: to preserve real human choices, "God cannot cause or plan our voluntary choices," so God's control "must not include every specific detail of every event," and he instead achieves general purposes by responding to free choices. Cottrell: "God does not have a specific, unconditional purpose for each discrete particle, object, person, and event within the creation." Three arguments are reported — that the Calvinist view makes God the author of sin, that a choice caused by God cannot be a real choice (Cottrell's lever analogy), and that Calvinism breeds fatalism.

**The reply (G).** On real choices the answer is that the Arminian premise is an extrapolation from human experience rather than a text: "Arminians have simply not answered the question, Where does Scripture say that a choice ordained by God is not a real choice?" Grudem's own position is stated as a pair to be held together — God "ordains all that we do" and "we exercise our personal will and make real, voluntary choices" — with Calvin's distinction between necessity and compulsion doing the work, and the incomprehensibility conceded rather than resolved.

**Three Arminian answers on foreknowledge, and the entailment.** Asked how God can predict the future if choices are uncaused, Arminians answer in three ways, and Grudem's treatment of the first is the one that matters most for this repository. Against *foreknowledge without foreordination* — Cottrell: "I affirm that God has a true foreknowledge of future free-will choices without himself being the agent that causes them or renders them certain" — Grudem replies that foreknowledge alone already costs the Arminian what he was protecting: "even if God did not plan or cause things to happen, the fact that they are foreknown means that they will certainly come about," and so "If our future choices are known, then they are fixed. And if they are fixed, then they are not 'free' in the Arminian sense (undetermined or uncaused)." Of the second answer he says that open theism "seems to me to be the most consistent Arminian position" — a concession about consistency, immediately withdrawn on the ground that its predictions could be wrong, which is not how Scripture treats prophecy.

**Middle knowledge (Molinism).** Classified as a third Arminian view, because on it "the ultimate factor that determines whether someone is saved" is the person's own libertarian choice. Grudem first clears ground: the dispute is *not* about whether God knows how people would respond in various circumstances — "I affirmed this in chapter 12, pages 225–31" — and he adds Frame's point that such knowledge "is already part of God's necessary knowledge, or what Craig calls natural knowledge." Four objections follow; the third is the argument rather than the assertion. Since "every situation in which people find themselves is itself the result of thousands, in fact millions, of individual human choices," God cannot bring about a situation without determining those prior choices too, and "such a process denies the kind of libertarian free will that middle knowledge advocates are claiming to protect." The illustration is autobiographical — great-grandparents emigrating from Norway, the founding of Westminster in 1929, a seminary founded in Arizona in 1987.

**The closing move.** The chapter does not end by claiming the Calvinist has fewer difficulties. It concedes two questions the Calvinist cannot answer — "Exactly how can God not be blamed for evil when he ordains that we do evil willingly?" and "Exactly how can God cause us to choose something willingly?" — and then compares the two sets of residues by their effect: "the effect of these unanswered questions is to increase our appreciation of the greatness of God," whereas the Arminian's "tends to diminish the greatness of God—his omniscience, his omnipotence, and the absolute reliability of his promises." The last sentence of the chapter's argument returns to the attribute the base already holds from pp. 232-233: the Arminian position "diminishes the wisdom and skill of God the Creator."

**Rom. 8:28 again.** Arguing that the practical differences are "immense," Grudem grounds the Calvinist's "incredible freedom from worry about the future" (Calvin's phrase) in the same verse the wisdom section used, and glosses adversity as something that "has not come about because God was unable to prevent it, but because it too is part of his wise plan."

**Gaps.** Every opponent is read through Grudem's quotation, so whether Cottrell, Pinnock, Marshall or Craig would accept these as fair statements cannot be checked here; Grudem's own footnote conceding that Cottrell, unlike Pinnock and Rice, does not deny divine omniscience is the base's only evidence that the Arminian camp is not uniform.
