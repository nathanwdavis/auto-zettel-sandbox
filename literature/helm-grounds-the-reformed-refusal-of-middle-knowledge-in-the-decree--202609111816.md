**The confessional cut.** Helm reads the Westminster Confession's chapter on the decree as closing the door on middle knowledge before any philosophical argument begins. The paragraph he labels "III" (it is chapter 3, paragraph 2; his 2009 post cites it correctly, and Van Horn quotes its text) says that although God knows whatever may or can come to pass on all supposed conditions, he has not decreed anything because he foresaw it as future or as what would come to pass on such conditions. Helm: "Paragraph III cuts off middle knowledge", the doctrine on which God is presented with an array of counterfactuals of libertarian freedom and elects the one most in accord with his purposes. "Not so, says the Confession. God’s decrees and his foreknowledge cover the same ground, and are grounded by the divine intuition, not by God's inspection of un-decreed possibilities. His decrees are the ground of whatever happens. They do not include decreeing based on what he foreknew as future, nor of what he foreknew would occur as a result of conditions other than his decree."

**Twisse and Rutherford.** The seventeenth-century Reformed "were not so much interested in whether Molinism was internally satisfactory", that is whether God could know the outcome of a counterfactual of libertarian freedom, "as in cutting it off at the root, because they could not conceive of any counterfactuals of creaturely freedom being true that were not first decreed by God, and true because of this, and so part of his free knowledge." A counterfactual "is not a ‘factual’, what is decreed"; a conditional about what a decreed thing would have done is not a fact, "because not decreed", and "does not follow from what has already been decreed, but it is simply a ‘free floating’ form of words. So they argued ad hominem against Molinism by denying the very idea of middle knowledge." In an uncaptured paragraph Helm quotes van Mastricht (in Rester's 2019 translation) to the same effect: every knowable thing falls under natural or free knowledge, and what would occur given certain second causes belongs to the knowledge that depends on the decree constituting the order of things, as dry straw would burn if laid near a flame though God never decreed that it be laid there.

**The Reformed answer to the grounding objection.** "Their answer to the ‘grounding’ objection would be that what grounds the truth is not a state of affairs that exists apart from the decree of God, but only what is decreed, one of the countless events, or states of affairs, brought to exist by God’s decree. For what comes to pass is only what is decreed." Hence a knowledge "between the divine natural knowledge (his knowledge of all possibilities), and free knowledge" is "inadmissible. How could it be known to God that in circumstances C, A will freely do P other than by being unconditionally decreed by him, and so being an aspect of the divine free knowledge."

**Ware as the exception.** For these theologians "God’s knowledge of what takes place in his creation, whatever else it is, is necessarily knowledge of what he will decree", and the idea of states of affairs, "including the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, which are distinct from the divine mind and which are made true or false only by acts of creaturely freedom which God abets by supporting and enabling, is quite unacceptable. Theologians such as Bruce Ware, who find a place for ‘Reformed Molinism’ (God’s Greater Glory, pp.110-112) are an odd and an inexplicable exception. The problem with introducing such a theological view into the current work on middle knowledge is that it has the effect of changing the subject." The uncaptured closing paragraph adds that Calvinists do not figure in current debates about Molinism, which Helm calls a debate within the libertarian guild, conducted without reference to the necessity and scope of the decrees.

**An observation, this base's own.** In the 2009 post Helm says a compatibilist's knowledge of what Jones would do in C is natural knowledge; here the tradition he reports puts a counterfactual of creaturely freedom in free knowledge, true because decreed. The two are not contradictory (the first concerns a possibility given a nature and a setting, the second a truth about a creature God has decreed), but the post does not say which side of the decree the Reformed put the conditional on, and the difference is the one Van Horn presses.
