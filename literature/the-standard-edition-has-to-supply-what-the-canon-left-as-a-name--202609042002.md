Own-words summary of [[excursus-heresies-canon-1--202609041957]].

Percival appends to Canon I an excursus that walks the list of parties and says,
for each, what it held — because the canon does not. That editorial necessity is
the point of this note.

On the entry at issue he quotes William Bright: "'The Sabellians,' whose theory
is traceable to Noetus and Praxeas in the latter part of the second century:
they regarded the Son and the Holy Spirit as aspects and modes of, or as
emanations from, the One Person of the Father." He adds the assessment that such
a view "tended directly to dissolve Christian belief in the Trinity and in the
Incarnation," and cites Dionysius of Alexandria calling it "blasphemy, unbelief,
and irreverence."

Two features of the gloss matter downstream.

**Its date and genre.** This is 1900, quoting a Victorian patristic scholar. It
is the same evidential class as a nineteenth-century systematician's report of
the councils — good scholarship, but not a conciliar text, and not contemporary
with one. A note that cites it is citing what the standard edition took the
canon to mean.

**Its content includes emanation.** Bright's gloss puts *emanation* language on
the Sabellian side, together with "aspects and modes". That is worth recording
precisely because it does not line up with how emanation is usually sorted:
Bright ranges under one condemned party both the "modes" reading and the
"emanation from" reading, which a reader coming from elsewhere might expect to
fall on opposite sides.

Bright also notes the polemical traffic in the label: the imputation of
"Sabellianizing" could be "utilised by the Arians against maintainers of the
Consubstantiality". The name was an accusation before it was a taxonomy.
