Own-words summary of
[[agarwal-nunes-and-blunt-retrieval-practice-consistently-benefits-student-learning--202609040400]].
Quotations are the authors' words. **Page references are to the authors' 2020
preprint**, which is what was read in full; the published 2021 article is held
here only as its abstract, and the two disagree on three headline figures. The
reference note sets out that disagreement, and this note flags each place where
it bites.

**Why this base went looking for it.** Permanent note
[[retrieval-not-re-exposure-is-what-makes-learning-last--202609040340]] rested
its robustness claim on a survey chapter written by one of the authors of the
very study it was assessing, and said so rather than rounding it up. That
chapter named three audits of the literature. This is the first of the three
this base has read, and it was chosen because it was the only one an index
called open.

**What it is.** A screening exercise, not an experiment. The authors wrote a
deliberately narrow definition of "classroom research", ran it over nearly
2,000 abstracts, and came out with 50 experiments drawn from 37 studies, total
*n* = 5,374. They then coded each one and, where the reporting allowed it,
computed an effect size. Their answer to their own question — does retrieval
practice improve learning in real classrooms — is "an unequivocal 'yes.'"
(p. 37; the inner single quotes are this note's rendering of the authors' own
double quotes around the word).

**What it is not, and this is the finding.** It is not a meta-analysis, and the
authors do not call it one; they call it a literature review throughout. The
distinction is not pedantry. A meta-analysis pools effect sizes into a single
estimate with a confidence interval, weights studies by precision, and tests
for heterogeneity and for publication bias. This review does none of those. It
**counts**: "Across the 50 experiments coded, we derived 66 effect sizes (60
Cohen's *d* and 6 partial eta squared). For 16 comparisons, data were
insufficiently reported to calculate an effect size" (p. 19). The headline is a
tally of that count — "the majority of effect sizes (62%) indicated medium or
large benefits from retrieval practice. In other words, 37 out of 60 Cohen's
*d*s were greater than 0.50" (p. 19) — and the breakdown behind it is 21 large,
16 medium, 22 small or very small, and one negative (*d* = -0.31), which sums
to the 60. This is vote-counting. It is a legitimate and useful thing to do; it
is not a measurement of the size of the effect, and it cannot be, because
nothing is pooled.

**The bias the authors name and do not correct.** "Our literature review is
subject to publication bias, when studies with positive outcomes tend to be
published to a greater extent than studies with negative outcomes" (p. 37).
They say this and then move on: there is no funnel plot, no trim-and-fill, no
*p*-curve, nothing. A vote count run over a literature its own authors describe
as biased toward positive outcomes will come out positive, and the 59-of-60
figure they close on cannot distinguish a robust effect from a well-filtered
one. Two further limits sit in the same paragraph: "our search of the
literature did not return any studies published before 1999", and "our review
was conducted in January 2018" — three years before publication.

**The comparison conditions are where the number gets its size.** What the 50
experiments mostly measured retrieval practice *against* was students re-reading
material (*k* = 19), lessons taught without quizzes (*k* = 14), and non-quizzed
items on the same final test (*k* = 12). Only five experiments compared a
larger amount of retrieval practice with a smaller amount, and the authors
report the result plainly: "Only five experiments included a comparison
condition of fewer opportunities for retrieval practice (e.g., comparing two
exams vs. weekly quizzes); experiments using this type of comparison yielded
small and very small effect sizes" (p. 23). Their own third recommendation
concedes the point — "More stringent and realistic comparisons to retrieval
practice in classroom settings include traditional lectures, flipped classroom
activities, think-pair-share discussions, and student presentations" (p. 38).

**Where the consistency is thin.** The effect sizes are largest in middle
school, and "all of the studies at the middle school level were conducted
within the same school district near St. Louis, Missouri, United States"
(p. 23); three of the medical-school studies come from one school in
Copenhagen and three from one in St. Louis. Undergraduate classrooms give the
smallest effects — in the preprint's Table 2, one of twenty undergraduate
experiments falls in the medium-or-large column against ten in the small
column — and the authors' own first explanation is not a property of students:
"the role of the teacher-researcher may have contributed to smaller effect
sizes in undergraduate classrooms" (p. 39), because at that level the
instructor was usually also the researcher and at K-12 level was not. So the
strongest results come from the sites where the experimenter was furthest from
the teaching, and the weakest from where they were the same person.

**The null result they report as a positive.** "We had anticipated an emergence
of optimal conditions for retrieval practice: content areas, formats,
frequency, and so on. However, we did not find any singular or specifically
optimal conditions; instead, we found that nearly all conditions in schools and
classrooms yielded a benefit from retrieval practice" (p. 41). Read as a
practical recommendation this is liberating: do it, and stop worrying about the
format. Read as evidence it is a failure to detect moderators in a
vote-counted, publication-biased, unpooled set of 60 numbers — which is what
you would also expect to see if the moderators were there.

**And it excludes notes by construction.** This is the part that matters most
to this repository, and it is not an omission but a stated criterion. Among the
three guidelines defining "classroom research" for the review: "Closed-book,
not open-book: All retrieval practice took place without the use of notes,
external learning aids, or the internet" (p. 10), restated in the Methods as
"the retrieval practice intervention must take place without the use of notes,
external learning aids, or the internet (e.g., quizzes in class must be
closed-book, not open-book)" (p. 17). One coded study kept note-taking as a
*comparison* condition — Dobson and Linderholm 2015(b), whose "Read-read while
taking notes" arm (abbreviated R-R+N) the appendix records as yielding a
"Numerical benefit from retrieval practice" over it (p. 56), meaning a
difference in direction only, with no effect size computed. It is one row of
fifty.

**The version problem, in the one place it changes a sentence.** The preprint's
abstract says "only 10 out of 50 experiments were conducted in non-WEIRD
countries" (p. 2); the published abstract says 6%. The preprint's own General
Discussion says "Only 10 out of 50 experiments that met our screening criteria
were conducted outside the United States" and, a sentence later, "80% of
experiments reviewed were conducted in WEIRD countries" (p. 40) — 20% non-WEIRD,
not 6%. The three non-WEIRD countries it actually names among its coded
experiments are Pakistan, Taiwan and Turkey (p. 19), and 3 of 50 is 6%. **This
repository's reading**, not the authors': the preprint's abstract conflated
"outside the United States" with "non-WEIRD", most of the non-US sites being
Denmark and other WEIRD countries, and peer review corrected it. The published
6% is the figure to cite, and it makes the geographic narrowness worse than the
preprint's abstract admitted, not better. The other two changes — 60 effect
sizes down to 49, and 62% down to 57% — are not explained in either document,
and every per-experiment count above is therefore a preprint count.
