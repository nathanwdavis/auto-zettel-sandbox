Own-words summary of
[[roediger-and-karpicke-test-enhanced-learning--202609040305]]. Unlike this
base's two 2026-09-04 note-taking summaries, this one rests on the whole
article, so design and numbers are reportable here. Quotations are the authors'
words; everything else is this note's characterisation.

**The design, and the one choice that makes the paper worth anything.** Two
experiments, 120 and 180 Washington University undergraduates, reading short
prose passages — TOEFL comprehension pieces on the sun and on sea otters, 256
and 275 words, each scored against 30 "idea units". Everyone read a passage.
Then the manipulation: some students *read it again*, others *tried to write
down what they could remember of it*, with no feedback and no chance to check.
Then a final recall test after 5 minutes, 2 days or 1 week.

The choice that matters is the control condition. A weaker study would compare
being tested against doing nothing, and would then be open to the obvious
objection that testing simply gave people a second look. Roediger and Karpicke
gave their control group the *whole passage* to reread — deliberately stacking
the deck against their own hypothesis, since a student recalling from memory
without feedback "can only reexperience whatever material they can recall"
(p. 249). Experiment 2 pushes it further and counts the rereads: students in
the four-study condition (SSSS) got through the passage 14.2 times on average,
those in the study-once-then-test-three-times condition (STTT) 3.4 times.

**The result is a crossover, and the crossover is the finding.** At 5 minutes
the rereaders win — 81% against 75% in Experiment 1, and in Experiment 2 the
ordering runs strictly with exposure: 83% for SSSS, 78% for SSST, 71% for STTT.
At a week the ordering inverts completely: 56% against 42% in Experiment 1, and
61% for STTT against 40% for SSSS in Experiment 2. Four times the reading, two
thirds of the retention. The authors put the point about re-exposure plainly:
"the testing effect is not simply a result of students gaining reexposure to
the material during testing, because restudying allowed students to reexperience
100% of the material but produced poor long-term retention" (p. 253).

Two further observations from the same data. Measured as proportional
forgetting rather than absolute recall, the four-study group lost 52% of what
it had, the single-test group 28%, the three-test group 14%. And in
Experiment 1 the tested group's recall after a *week* matched the reread group's
after *two days* — testing, the authors say, "prevented forgetting of
information for an additional 5 days relative to repeated study" (p. 251).

**The metacognitive result, which they present almost as an aside and which
this base thinks is the more useful half.** After the learning session,
students in Experiment 2 rated how well they thought they would remember the
passage in a week, on a 7-point scale. The rereaders were the most confident
(4.8) and the repeated-testers the least (4.0), and the rereaders went on to
perform the worst. The authors: "Relative to testing, repeated studying inflated
students' confidence in their ability to remember the passages in the future,
even though repeated-study subjects actually showed much poorer retention on
delayed tests" (p. 254). Their explanation is that students judge future memory
by what is producing rapid gains now, and rereading produces exactly that.

**What this paper does not do**, which matters because inquiry 202609032123
sent this cycle looking for a measured cost of externalising memory. It does
not measure one. Every condition here happens inside the head: read again, or
recall from memory. Nobody in either experiment was given anything to keep, and
the words "note" and "external" occur nowhere in the article in that sense — the
only hits are the "Note." headings under two tables and the instruction to
"write down as much of the material from the passage as they could remember"
(p. 250), which describes the recall test itself. The authors are also candid
that they do not know how far the effect travels: "The boundary conditions for
the testing effect are not yet known" (p. 254).

So the honest reading is that this paper supplies a *mechanism* by which
delegating memory to paper could cost something — if a note removes the occasion
for retrieval, it removes what produced the durability here — and supplies no
measurement of that cost at all. The measurement, such as it is, comes from a
different study in the same research line:
[[agarwal-et-al-put-the-notes-on-the-desk-and-measured-what-happened-a-week-later--202609040320]].
