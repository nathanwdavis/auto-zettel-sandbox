Own-words summary of
[[rowland-the-effect-of-testing-versus-restudy-on-retention--202609040600]],
read in full. Quotations are the author's words; everything else is this note's
characterisation. All page locators are **advance online** pages, not journal
pages — see the reference note.

**What it is.** A single-author random-effects meta-analysis of the testing
effect: 159 effect sizes drawn from 61 studies "published or otherwise reported
from 1975 through 2013" (p. 8), searched to March 2013. The estimate is
`g = 0.50, CI [0.42, 0.58]`, `p < .001`, and the author's summary of it is one
sentence: "Using meta-analysis, the present study demonstrates the reliability of
the testing effect" (p. 16). That number is the thing this base came for. It is
the first independent measurement of the effect in the base — Rowland is not a
co-author of any other source here — and it says the effect is real and about
half a standard deviation.

**The narrowing that defines the paper, and it is the mirror image of the other
audit this base holds.** Studies were kept only if they had an initial study
phase, a test-versus-restudy manipulation, and a final assessment; and they were
excluded, by explicit criterion, if they "assessed participant learning of
information for a real or simulated class in which they were enrolled (k = 13
studies eliminated; but see Bangert-Drowns et al., 1991, for a relevant
meta-analysis)" (p. 8). Thirteen classroom studies were identified and thrown
out, with readers pointed to a different review for them. This paper is
laboratory evidence by construction. Its counterpart in this base,
[[agarwal-nunes-and-blunt-audited-the-classroom-half-and-declined-to-meta-analyse--202609040510]],
is classroom evidence by construction, having screened out the laboratory for
the symmetrical reason. Neither review is hiding anything; they simply never
looked at the same studies.

**The spread, which the headline hides.** The paper prints a stem-and-leaf plot
of all 159 effect sizes (Figure 1, p. 12). Counting its leaves — they sum to
exactly 159, which is how the transcription in the capture was checked — **28 of
the 159 effect sizes are negative**, that is, restudy beat testing, with the most
extreme at about −1.5. So roughly one comparison in six in the laboratory
literature runs against the effect. This is not a criticism the author conceals;
it is his own figure, and it sits beside a pooled estimate that is nonetheless
comfortably positive. The comparable figure in the classroom review is 3 of 49
(202609040510), which is a smaller fraction, from a much smaller and differently
selected sample.

**The delay result, stated precisely because a note in this base turns on it.**
Testing effects were larger at longer delays. Categorically, studies with
retention intervals of at least one day gave `g = 0.69, CI [0.56, 0.81]` (k = 56)
against `g = 0.41, CI [0.31, 0.51]` (k = 103) for intervals under a day, and the
between-levels test was significant at p < .01 (Table 1, p. 13; text at p. 15).
The high-exposure subset repeats it more weakly: 0.78 against 0.58, p < .05
(Table 2, p. 14). Continuously, log retention interval was the *only* significant
continuous moderator in either data set, slope 0.083, CI [0.031, 0.135] (Table 3,
p. 16). The author's own gloss: "the magnitude of the testing effect appears to
grow with the duration of the retention interval" (p. 16).

**But read what the categorical split actually is.** It is binary, at one day.
There is no third level. The range of intervals the paper is discussing is named
in its own framing of the question — "testing effects emerge following long
retention intervals (of days or weeks)" at one end, "short retention intervals on
the order of minutes" at the other (p. 6). Nothing here is measured at a
semester's distance, let alone at the horizons a slip-box claim concerns. This is
the fact that
[[the-laboratory-and-classroom-evidence-were-separated-on-purpose--202609040610]]
is built on.

**Two things testing does not need, and one it does.** It does not need a
particular population: college and non-college samples were homogeneous, 0.49
against 0.59 (Table 1, p. 13). It does not need the final test to match the
initial one — the initial–final match moderator was not significant, which the
author reads as evidence against transfer-appropriate-processing accounts. What
it does need is that the retrieval attempt either succeed or be corrected.
Without feedback, studies whose initial test performance was at or below 50%
produced `g = 0.03, CI [-0.21, 0.27], p = .79` — no effect at all — rising to
0.29 at 51–75% success and 0.56 above 75%; with feedback, `g = 0.73` regardless
of success (p. 15). That is the sharpest boundary condition in the paper and it
has its own note,
[[a-failed-retrieval-that-is-never-corrected-buys-nothing--202609040620]].

**The file-drawer check, and how it comes out.** Published effect sizes averaged
`g = 0.58` (k = 122) against `g = 0.25` (k = 37) for unpublished ones, a
significant difference (Table 1, p. 13) — on its face the signature of
publication bias, and the author names it as such: "An indication of possible
publication bias was detected from the analysis of publication status in the full
data set" (p. 20). He then argues it away rather than correcting for it, and the
argument is a moderator one. The unpublished reports cluster on the two variables
that most affect the size of the effect: feedback "was absent in 89% of effect
sizes derived from unpublished studies", and their mean initial test performance
was 59% (p. 20). In the high-exposure subset, which holds those two variables
roughly constant, the gap disappears — 0.67 against 0.60, not significant (Table
2, p. 14) — and his conclusion is that "the unreliable testing effect found in
the unpublished studies in the full data set was very likely attributable to the
designs and procedures employed, thereby leading to a negatively biased effect
estimate" (p. 21). He also says explicitly why he did not run the usual tests:
they "provide skewed to potentially misleading information, especially in the
presence of substantial heterogeneity, as was the case in the present study"
(p. 20). No funnel plot, trim-and-fill or Egger test appears in the paper. This
base should record both halves — the gap was found, and it was explained rather
than adjusted — which is what
[[the-independent-audit-puts-the-effect-at-half-a-standard-deviation--202609040615]]
does.

**What the theory sections conclude**, recorded briefly because this base does
not use them: recall tests produce larger benefits than recognition tests, which
the author reads as support for effortful-processing accounts; "Limited support
was found for existing theoretical accounts attributing the testing effect to
enhanced semantic elaboration" (p. 1), and none at all for
transfer-appropriate processing, the initial–final test match moderator having
come out non-significant; and he offers the bifurcation model as the best
available framework.
Nothing in this base currently rests on any of that.
