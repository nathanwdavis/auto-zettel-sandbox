**What the paper does.** Ware takes up Pinnock's question "Why draw the line at foreknowledge?" and answers that the line belongs there. Open theism "affirms God’s exhaustive knowledge of the past and present, but it denies exhaustive divine foreknowledge, in that it denies that God knows—or can know—the future free decisions and actions of his moral creatures, even while it affirms that God knows all future possibilities and all divinely determined and logically-necessary future actualities." The conclusion: "open theism, by its denial of exhaustive divine foreknowledge, has shown itself to be unacceptable as a viable, legitimate model within evangelicalism." By its own uncaptured footnote 7 the paper restricts itself to that denial and does not argue the libertarian question, which Ware refers to *God's Lesser Glory*. The body (uncaptured except as noted) lists implications under four headings: God's character and work; revelation and Scripture; the gospel; the Christian life.

**The inference Ware puts in the open theist's mouth.** Why the denial is essential to open theism: "Were God to know some future choice, say, of what you will have for dinner this evening, since God’s knowledge is infallible, it must be the case that you will have for dinner what God knows you will, in which case you are not free to choose otherwise. As central and essential as libertarian freedom is to open theism, so equally central and essential is its denial of exhaustive divine foreknowledge." Ware states the entailment from infallible foreknowledge to fixity as the open theist's reasoning and does not dispute it in the captured text; what he disputes is the conclusion drawn from it.

**Foreknowledge as God's self-chosen proof of deity.** Sections II.1.f and g: "What is true both of the God of open theism and of these idols is that neither can declare what specific future events will unfold, events that involve innumerable future free choices and actions of human beings." Isaiah 46:10-11 declares the end from the beginning and names, in Cyrus, "a man, Cyrus no doubt, whom he knows will accomplish his purposes"; Isaiah 41:23 challenges the idols to "Declare the things that are going to come afterward, that we may know that you are gods." The denial "dishonors and belittles both the true and living God and the divine Son of the Father by denying to both one of their self-chosen bases for asserting the uniqueness of their deity, namely, that God alone, as God, knows and declares what the future will be." Jesus in John 13:19 and the prediction of Peter's denial are the Son's version. "How dare we deny to God what God himself has chosen as a basis for asserting his own unique deity!"

**The predestined crucifixion, freely chosen.** Section II.3.f presses a dilemma on open theism. If Christ's going to the cross was a free act in the openness sense, "it follows that Christ could choose to be given over or not, and then it follows that God cannot have known, prior to his choice, just what Christ would do", against Psalm 22, Isaiah 52:13-53:12, Acts 2:23, 4:27-28 and 1 Peter 1:20; Sanders is quoted conceding that "Until this moment in history other routes were, perhaps, open." If instead, with Boyd, the crucifixion was predestined, then "must God not know, at bare minimum, that his Son will choose to go to the cross?", and by openness standards of freedom the predetermined act would be coerced: "if his life of obedience and crucifixion was constrained, is it not, then, morally vacuous, and is not the cross, then, worthless?" Ware's own position is not stated here but is visible in the shape of the argument: a predestined event, foreknown in every particular, that is nonetheless the Son's own free and meritorious obedience, which is the compatibilist reading of Acts 2:23 and 4:27-28.

**Risk against assurance.** Section II.4.b: the Christian's hope that hardships "are permitted by God for what he knows will turn out to be ultimately good purposes" (Romans 8:28) is lost, since "God simply cannot give assurances that things will work out for good because he does not know how the future will unfold." Section II.4.d: "Openness advocates want it both ways. They want high risk, and they also want high assurance of God’s success. They cannot have it both ways. Clearly, what wins in the open view is risk; what loses is assurance of God’s success."

**Why no council spoke.** No council spoke "because no serious proposal was ever set before the church that would deny what all Christians believed without question, namely, that God, as God, knew the future", as well as the past and present, exhaustively. Councils and confessions are occasional; the absence of a creed is silence, not permission, and Ware asks Christians "to unite to declare now what we believe on this matter".
