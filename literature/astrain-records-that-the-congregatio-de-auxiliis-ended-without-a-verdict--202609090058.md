**The question and the two solutions.** The Congregatio was "A commission established by Pope Clement VIII to settle the theological controversy regarding grace which arose between the Dominicans and the Jesuits towards the close of the sixteenth century," and its crucial point "was the reconciliation of the efficacy of grace with human freedom." Astrain states the problem as a pair of certainties: efficacious grace "obtains, infallibly, man's consent," and "in so acting, man is free"; "Hence the question: How can these two things--the infallible result and liberty--be harmonized?" The Dominicans answered with "physical premotion and predetermination," a physical impulsion "by means of which God determines and applies our faculties to the action"; the Jesuits "found the explanation in that mediate knowledge (scientia media) whereby God knows, in the objective reality of things what a man, in any circumstances in which he might be placed, would do," so that God, foreseeing that a man would freely correspond with grace A and not with grace B, "desirous of man's conversion, gives him grace A." Each side charged the other with the opposite heresy: "The Dominicans declared that the Jesuits conceded too much to free will, and so tended toward Pelagianism. In turn, the Jesuits complained that the Dominicans did not sufficiently safeguard human liberty, and seemed in consequence to lean towards Calvinism."

**The course of the dispute.** It "is usually supposed to have begun in the year 1581," when theses defended by the Jesuit Prudencio de Montemayor "were vigorously attacked by the Dominican Domingo Báñez"; it ran through Louvain (Baius against Lessius, 1587), Spain, and Rome. "In 1588 the Spanish Jesuit Luis de Molina published at Lisbon his 'Concordia liberi arbitrii cum gratiæ donis', in which he explained efficacious grace on the basis of scientia media"; Banez reported it to the Viceroy of Portugal as containing propositions the Inquisition had censured, the sale was stopped and then allowed with Molina's replies appended in 1589. After public disturbances "Clement VIII took the matter into his own hands and ordered both parties to refrain from further discussion and await the decision of the Apostolic See." A commission that began "2 Jan., 1598" twice reported for condemning Molina's book; the pope, "Displeased at their haste in treating a question of such importance," ordered the work re-examined and then had the matter debated in his presence: "Sixty-eight sessions were thus held (1602-1605)," and under Paul V "seventeen debates took place."

**The decree of 1607.** "Finally, after twenty years of discussion public and private, and eighty-five conferences in the presence of the popes, the question was not solved but an end was put to the disputes. The pope's decree communicated (5 September, 1607) to both Dominicans and Jesuits, allowed each party to defend its own doctrine, enjoined each from censoring or condemning the opposite opinion, and commanded them to await, as loyal sons of the Church, the final decision of the Apostolic See. That decision, however, has not been reached, and both orders, consequently, maintain their respective theories, just as any other theological opinion is held." A decree of the Inquisition of 1 December 1611 then forbade "the publication of any book concerning efficacious grace until further action by the Holy See," a prohibition in force "during the greater part of the seventeenth century."

**Gaps.** The article is a Catholic reference-work account and no Dominican account of the same events is on file, so its judgements of the parties are not relied on here; only its dates and the wording of the decree are. The text of the 1607 decree itself is not on file, nor is any Dominican account of the same events. The New Advent transcription garbles some Spanish names, and they are quoted as they stand.
