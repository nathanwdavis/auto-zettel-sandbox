Own-words summary of section 1 of [[vallicella-divine-simplicity-sep--202609042072]],
the section the 2026-09-04 capture stopped short of and the 2026-09-12 repair
capture supplies in full (raw/202609120015). Section 1 asks "what could motivate
such a strange and seemingly incoherent doctrine?" and gives, in order, three
positive grounds before turning to immateriality, eternity, immutability, and the
Trinity.

**From aseity.** A maximally perfect God must be *a se*, dependent on nothing
distinct from itself. But a thing that *has* its properties — whether as
constituents or by instantiating them — depends on them, so "the divine aseity
would seem to require that God be rather than have his attributes." Simplicity is
what removes the dependence.

**From necessity.** A maximally perfect being must be metaphysically necessary,
and the entry argues that possible-worlds talk only represents necessity without
grounding it; the ground is "the identity in God of essence and existence,
possibility and actuality," and concludes: "The divine simplicity thus grounds
the divine necessity. God is necessary because he is simple." The same identity
grounds God's having his attributes essentially.

**From the Euthyphro dilemma.** The third ground is that DDS "provides a solution
to the ancient Euthyphro dilemma." Either God commands the good because it is
good (subjecting his will to an external standard, "to the detriment of the
divine sovereignty") or the good is good because he commands it (making morality
arbitrary). The entry answers: "On DDS, however, the dilemma is a false
alternative. If God is goodness itself in its prime instance, then he is not
subject to a standard; he is the standard. And if God, being simple, just is his
willing, then he cannot will anything other than the good and the appearance of
arbitrarity vanishes." The entry credits Feser (2009, 129), drawing on Stump
(2003).

The three motivations share a form: each names something classical theism wants
to say of God — aseity, necessity, non-arbitrary goodness — and argues that
possession-of-properties would forfeit it while identity-with-them secures it.
That is the same "argument from dependence" the base already holds from
Maimonides and Aquinas, run toward three different payoffs rather than toward the
denial of composition alone.
