Own-words summary of
[[ratzsch-koperski-teleological-arguments-for-gods-existence--202609011003]].

The survey's starting point is an intuition, not an argument: "random,
unplanned, unexplained accident just couldn’t produce the order, beauty,
elegance, and seeming purpose that we experience in the natural world
around us" (sec. 1). Teleological arguments are the attempts to shape that
intuition into rigorous inference, and the entry sorts them by inference
pattern: analogical arguments (schema 1), deductive arguments from marks of
design (schema 2), and inferences to the best explanation (schema 3, not
captured; summarized here on the entry's authority only).

On Paley the entry is revisionist in a useful way. Against the standard
argument-from-analogy reading, it holds that "The watch does play an
obvious and crucial role—but as a paradigmatic instance of design
inferences rather than as the analogical foundation for an inferential
comparison" (sec. 2.2): Paley reads marks of contrivance directly off
natural objects, as in his claim that "there is precisely the same proof
that the eye was made for vision as there is that the telescope was made
for assisting it" (sec. 2.2, quoting Paley).

The contemporary forms get the entry's longest treatment. Cosmic
fine-tuning (sec. 4.1): "Even an extraordinarily small change in one of
many key parameters in the laws of physics would have made life impossible
anywhere in the universe" -- the cosmological constant is the lead example
-- and the main replies canvassed are multiverse hypotheses and objections
about probability assignments. Biological Intelligent Design (sec. 4.2): ID
advocates propose two specialized design marks -- "irreducible complexity
(Behe 1996) and specified complex information (Dembski 1998, 2002)" --
both of which the entry classifies as tending toward gap arguments, and it records
that "The movement has elicited vociferous criticism and opposition
(Pennock 2000)" from the scientific community, including the charge that
ID gets the relevant science wrong.

The conclusion declines a verdict but predicts persistence: perception of
intricacy and beauty in nature has "inclined many toward thoughts of
purpose and design", and "design convictions and arguments are unlikely to
disappear quietly" (sec. 5).
