Own-words summary of
[[agarwal-karpicke-kang-roediger-and-mcdermott-open-and-closed-book-tests--202609040315]],
read in full. Quotations are the authors' words.

**Why this study answers a question this base has been asking about for two
cycles.** Its manipulation is whether the learner can look at the written
material while answering questions about it. Open book means, in the authors'
own definition on p. 861, that students "view notes and textbooks while taking
the test"; closed book means they cannot. That is as near as this base has come
to an experiment on delegating memory to paper: the paper is on the desk, or it
is not, and recall is measured a week later.

**Design.** Two experiments, 36 and 48 Washington University undergraduates,
eight ~1000-word passages from a school textbook, seven short-answer questions
each, conditions counterbalanced within subjects by Latin square so that every
student met every condition. The conditions worth holding onto: study once;
study twice; study three times; closed-book test; closed-book test then
self-graded against the passage (i.e. with feedback); open-book test; and
"simultaneous answering", where students answered the questions with the
passage in front of them having never read it first. The final test came a week
later and was **closed-book for everyone** — the paper had to be given back.

**What having the material to hand bought, and for how long.** It bought a
great deal immediately and nothing at all durably. On the initial test the
open-book conditions averaged .82 against .70 (Experiment 1) and .82 against
.66 (Experiment 2) for the closed-book conditions. A week later that advantage
was gone: in Experiment 2 the open-book condition finished at .66 and the
closed-book-with-feedback condition also at .66; in Experiment 1, .65 against
.68. The authors state it twice, once per experiment, and once more in the
General Discussion: "while open-book tests resulted in substantially greater
initial performance than closed-book tests, this boost in initial performance
did not carry forward to the final test" (p. 866).

Read on the other measure the numbers say something sharper. The open-book
condition fell from .81 to .66 across the week; the closed-book-with-feedback
condition went from .65 to .66 and lost essentially nothing. Equivalent
destinations, very different journeys — a point worth flagging because a
current survey of this literature reports the same study on the forgetting
measure alone and the paper's own summary reports it on the retention measure
alone, and either sentence quoted by itself would misdescribe the study. Both
are recorded together in
`raw/202609040325-karpicke-2025-retrieval-based-learning-excerpts.txt`.

**The explanation the authors offer**, following Bjork and Bjork's distinction
between how accessible something is right now and how well it is stored: "open-
book test conditions increased the momentary accessibility of some of the
information, as evidenced by high initial performance in these conditions. The
retrieval of accessible information on the initial open-book tests, however,
produced small increments in storage strength" (p. 872). Looking something up
you could have recalled is a transaction that costs nothing and builds nothing.

**Two things the study does not license, both flagged by the authors.** First,
the open-book conditions still beat studying — every test condition beat every
matched study condition on the delayed test, so nothing here says consulting
notes is worse than not testing yourself at all. The comparison that fails is
open book against closed book, not test against no test. Second, the authors
warn that their design deliberately kept students from knowing which kind of
test was coming, and that in real classrooms the anticipation itself changes
behaviour: students "sometimes perform worse on open-book tests than on
closed-book tests because they prepare less effectively when they will be
allowed to use their notes or textbooks during the test", so open-book testing
"may have a negative effect of reducing the effectiveness of students'
studying" (p. 872). That is offered as a citation to older classroom work,
not as a finding of this study, and this base has not read those classroom
studies.

**The metamemory result, replicating the 2006 one on a different measure.**
Students predicted, on a 0–100% scale, how much they would remember in a week.
Predictions rose with rereading — .57, .65, .71 for one, two and three readings
— while actual recall for those same conditions was .40, .50, .54, and the
matched testing conditions that were predicted *lower* recalled *more*. The
authors call this "a striking dissociation" (p. 870) and close the paper by
naming it "This metacognitive illusion", which "could have dire consequences for
students when they must monitor and regulate their own learning" (p. 873). The
substantive convergence with
[[roediger-and-karpicke-measured-retrieval-against-re-exposure-and-let-time-decide--202609040310]]
is real; the independence is not — Karpicke and Roediger are authors of both,
and the two papers come out of the same laboratory.
