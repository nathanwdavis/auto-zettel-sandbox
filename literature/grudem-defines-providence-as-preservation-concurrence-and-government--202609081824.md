**The definition.** Providence is "God is continually involved with all created things in such a way that he (1) keeps them existing and maintaining the properties with which he created them; (2) cooperates with created things in every action, directing their distinctive properties to cause them to act as they do; and (3) directs them to fulfill his purposes." The three clauses give the three subtopics: preservation, concurrence, government. The chapter is positioned against four errors by name — deism, pantheism, chance, and impersonal fate.

**Preservation (A).** God "keeps all created things existing and maintaining the properties with which he created them." Argued from Heb. 1:3, where Grudem presses the Greek *pherō* ("carry, bear") past "sustain" to active carrying, and from Col. 1:17, Acts 17:28, Neh. 9:6, 2 Peter 3:7, Job 34:14–15. Two guards: this is not continuous re-creation, and created properties are real ("I do not just imagine that it hurts—it does hurt!"). He then makes preservation the ground of science and technology: experiments repeat because God sustains a universe whose things keep their properties.

**Concurrence (B).** God "cooperates with created things in every action, directing their distinctive properties to cause them to act as they do," on Eph. 1:11 ("works all things according to the counsel of his will"). The scriptural survey runs from inanimate creation through animals and the casting of lots to the affairs of nations and then to every aspect of a human life — steps, words, hearts, talents, the hearts of kings.

The load-bearing move is sub-section 4. A natural explanation and a divine one are not rivals dividing the work: "it is not as though the event was partly caused by God and partly by factors in the created world." Instead, "it is possible to affirm that in one sense events are fully (100 percent) caused by God and fully (100 percent) caused by the creature as well. However, divine and creaturely causes work in different ways." The divine cause is the "primary cause" that "plans and initiates everything that happens"; creaturely properties are the "secondary causes," and they are the ones open to observation. The Macbeth analogy makes the two levels concrete — on the level of the characters Macbeth "fully (100%)" killed Duncan, on the level of the play's creator Shakespeare did — with a caution attached that the analogy must not make God the "author" of sin in the older sense of doer.

Grudem states the limit himself: "It seems better to affirm that God causes all things that happen but that he does so in such a way that he somehow upholds our ability to make willing, responsible choices … Exactly how God combines his providential control with our willing and significant choices, Scripture does not explain to us." On evil the same confession is sharper, and he takes Berkhof's words for it: "the problem of God's relation to sin remains a mystery."

**Freedom (B.9).** The question is answered by distinguishing senses. Scripture "nowhere says that we are 'free' in the sense of being outside of God's control or of being able to make decisions that are not caused by anything" — indeed "to be outside of that providential control would simply be not to exist!" What is affirmed: "we are nonetheless free in the greatest sense that any creature of God could be free—we make willing choices, choices that have real effects." He then adopts Bruce Ware's pair of labels: *libertarian free will*, "the ability, all things being just what they are, to choose differently," against *freedom of inclination*, "we are free when we choose according to our strongest inclination or deepest desire." The second "seems to me more consistent with the teachings of Scripture," and — the decisive sentence — "it does not rule out the idea that our choices may ultimately be determined by God in ways invisible to us." A footnote adds that this "freedom of inclination" is also called "compatibilist freedom."

**Decrees (D).** "The decrees of God are the eternal plans of God whereby, before the creation of the world, he determined to bring about everything that happens" — providence in time is the outworking of them.

**Human action (E).** The section exists to block the inference from providence to passivity, and it does so by making means part of what is ordained: "God has ordained that our actions have effects. God has ordained that events will come about by our causing them." Worked through responsibility, prayer, Calvin on using remedies, and Paul at Corinth and in the shipwreck, where a revealed outcome still required that the sailors stay aboard. The summary is flat: "A hearty belief in God's providence is not a discouragement but a spur to action."
