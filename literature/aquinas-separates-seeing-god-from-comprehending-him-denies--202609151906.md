**Grounding: raw/202609151852-aquinas-summa-theologiae-i-q-12-how-god-is-known-by-us.html, the whole question as published by New Advent. Articles 1, 7, 8, 11, 12 and 13 read entire; the others only for their titles.**

**Seeing the essence (a.1).** Against Chrysostom and Dionysius, read as denying any vision of God, Aquinas holds that "it must be absolutely granted that the blessed see the essence of God", on two grounds: beatitude would otherwise lie in something other than God, and the natural desire to know the first cause "would remain void". The authorities are reconciled by a distinction that governs the rest of the question: they "speak of the vision of comprehension", and God "exceeds every kind of knowledge; which means that He is not comprehended".

**Seeing without comprehending (a.7).** "It is impossible for any created intellect to comprehend God." The definition: "what is comprehended is perfectly known; and that is perfectly known which is known so far as it can be known." A demonstrable truth held only on probable opinion is not comprehended, though nothing of it is missing. God is "infinitely knowable", the created light of glory is finite, so "it is clearly impossible for any created intellect to know God in an infinite degree". The reply to the second objection states the result in a form this base can use: "God is called incomprehensible not because anything of Him is not seen; but because He is not seen as perfectly as He is capable of being seen." Incomprehensibility is a limit on the *mode* of knowing, not a missing *part* of the object. A second, looser sense of "comprehension", as opposed to non-attainment, is conceded to the blessed.

**Not seeing all that God knows (a.8).** "The created intellect, in seeing the divine essence, does not see in it all that God does or can do." Things are seen in God as effects in their cause, and "the more perfectly a cause is seen, the more of its effects can be seen in it", so only an intellect that comprehended God wholly would see everything; none does. The *sed contra* gives the concrete residue: the angels see God's essence and yet "are ignorant of future contingent things, and of secret thoughts; for this knowledge belongs to God alone." The reply to the fourth objection narrows the natural desire so that its non-satisfaction is no defect: the intellect desires species and genera, not "other singulars, their thoughts and their deeds", nor "things that exist not as yet, but which God can call into being".

**This life (aa. 11-13).** No one sees the essence in this life: "God cannot be seen in His essence by a mere human being, except he be separated from this mortal life", Moses and Paul being treated as raptures above the common order (a.11). Natural reason reaches "whether He exists" and what must belong to a first cause, because sensible effects "do not equal the power of God as their cause" (a.12); reason "can know 'whether it is'" but not "'what it is'" (a.12 ad 1). Grace gives "a more perfect knowledge of God ... than by natural reason", adding effects and truths reason cannot reach, "as, for instance, that God is Three and One"; yet "by the revelation of grace in this life we cannot know of God 'what He is,' and thus are united to Him as to one unknown" (a.13 and ad 1). The *sed contra* of a.13 is 1 Cor. 2:10, the Spirit's revealing of what, in the gloss Aquinas cites, "none of the princes of this world knew", the philosophers.

**The ladder the question builds.** Read together the six articles give four rungs: natural reason (that God is, and what a first cause must be); grace and revelation in this life (more effects, some truths beyond reason, still not what God is); the beatific vision (the essence seen whole, not comprehended, and not all its effects seen in it); and God's own knowledge, which alone includes future contingents and secret thoughts. Every rung but the last is bounded, and at every rung Aquinas describes the bound in the same way, as a deficit of mode in the knower rather than a portion of the object withheld.

**Limits.** Articles 2-6, 9 and 10 are not read. Aquinas's own vocabulary is *comprehendere* and *videre*; the translation's "comprehend" is used here in his strict sense and the English word's modern sense should not be read back into it.
