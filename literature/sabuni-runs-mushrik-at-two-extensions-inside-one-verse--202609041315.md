Every English rendering below is this repository's own working translation of the
captured Arabic, and the Arabic is printed beside each rendering that carries
weight. The standing translation debt is unpaid here too.

## The lexical section states the disputed question in two sentences

Glossing `تَنْكِحُواْ ٱلْمُشْرِكَاتِ`, al-Sabuni writes: "that is, do not marry
idolatresses; the *mushrika* is she who worships idols and has no heavenly
religion, and likewise the *mushrik*. And it is said that it comprehends the
women of the Book too, **because the People of the Book are associators**, on the
strength of God's word 'the Jews said Ezra is the son of God and the Christians
said the Messiah is the son of God' … up to 'glory be to Him above what they
associate' [Q 9:30-31]" —
`وقيل: إنها تعم الكتابيات أيضاً لأن أهل الكتاب مشركون`.

Two things are worth marking. The default definition he gives is not doctrinal
but institutional: an associator is one who worships idols **and has no revealed
religion** (`وليس لها دين سماوي`). And the dissenting view is not offered as a
sociological observation; it is offered with a Qur'anic proof text whose closing
verb is `يُشْرِكُونَ` — "they associate", said of the People of the Book by name.

## The majority's proof, numbered

Under `الحكم الأول: هل يحرم نكاح الكتابيات؟` he records that marriage to women
of the Book is permitted per Q 5:5, "and this is the position of the majority of
the scholars, and the four imams held it" — `وهذا قول جمهور العلماء، وبه قال
الأئمة الأربعة` — while **Ibn 'Umar** held it forbidden, quoting the same
*Muwatta'* line this base has from al-Qurtubi. Al-Sabuni then adds what
al-Qurtubi does not: "and to this the **Imamiyya and some of the Zaydiyya** went,
and they made the Ma'ida verse abrogated by this verse, the abrogation of the
specific by the general" — `وإلى هذا ذهب الإمامية، وبعض الزيدية`.

His `حجة الجمهور` then runs five lettered proofs. (a) is the grammatical one, and
it is the same argument al-Qurtubi reports, from the same two verses — Q 2:105
and Q 98:1 — reaching the same conclusion: "the conjunction requires
distinctness, so the apparent wording *mushrikat* does not comprehend the women
of the Book" (`والعطفُ يقتضي المغايرة`). (b) Qatada: what is meant is "the
associating women *of the Arabs*, who have no book that they read"; and
al-Nakha'i, asked whether Q 2:221 forbade marrying a Jewish or Christian woman,
answered `إنما تلك المجوسيات وأهل الأوثان` — "those are the Magian women and the
idolaters". (c) Chronology: al-Baqara is among the first revealed at Medina and
al-Ma'ida among the last, and the later abrogates the earlier, not the reverse.
(d) 'Umar separated Hudhayfa from his Jewish wife but said `لا أزعم أنها حرام`,
"I do not claim she is forbidden" — prudence, not prohibition. (e) The hadith on
the Magians, "treat them by the rule of the People of the Book, except that
their women are not married and their slaughter not eaten", which is pointless
unless the People of the Book's women *are* marriageable.

He closes the ruling by quoting **al-Tabari**'s own verdict: the best reading is
Qatada's, the verse is `عام ظاهرها، خاص باطنها` — "general in its outward form,
specific in its inner sense" — "nothing in it has been abrogated, and the women
of the People of the Book are not included in it".

## The second ruling, which is where the word changes size

`الحكم الثاني: من هم المشركون الذين يحرم تزويجهم؟` — who are the associators to
whom a Muslim woman may not be married? Here al-Sabuni's answer is the opposite
one: "what is meant by *mushrik* here is **every unbeliever who does not profess
the religion of Islam**, so it takes in the idolater, the Magian, **the Jew, the
Christian** and the apostate from Islam" — `والمراد بالمشرك هنا كل كافر لا يدين
بدين الإسلام، فيشمل الوثني، والمجوسي، واليهودي، والنصراني، والمرتد عن الإسلام`.

So the same word, in two halves of a single verse, is given two different
extensions by one author on one page — narrow in the first half (the People of
the Book fall outside it), broad in the second (they fall inside it). The reason
al-Sabuni gives is not semantic at all. It is `أن الإسلام يعلو ولا يُعلى عليه`,
"Islam is exalted and is not exalted over", elaborated as a claim about
household authority: the husband has `سلطة وولاية` over the wife and the children
follow the father.

He then adds a reason of a different kind, which cuts the other way and is worth
recording because it is the nearest thing in this capture to a same-God
argument: a Muslim husband "venerates Moses and Jesus, and believes in their
mission, and believes in the Torah and the Gospel that God sent down, and his
faith does not lead him to harm his Jewish or Christian wife on account of
creed, **because he meets her on faith in God and veneration of His messengers**"
— `لأنه يلتقي معها على الإيمان بالله، وتعظيم رسله`. The asymmetry is thus
justified twice over, once by hierarchy and once by an asymmetry of shared
content, and the two justifications are not the same and are not reconciled.

## Independence, stated honestly

Al-Sabuni is compiling here, not innovating: proofs (a)-(e) are classical and
(a) is the argument al-Qurtubi already carries. The two sources are therefore
**not** independent witnesses to the argument; they are independent witnesses to
its standing, six and a half centuries apart. The Imamiyya/Zaydiyya attribution
and the whole of the second ruling are his contribution to this base, and the
first-person anecdote in the second ruling is his own voice and is used as
evidence of nothing.
