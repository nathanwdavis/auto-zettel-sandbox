**Grounding: raw/202609151854-pseudo-dionysius-the-mystical-theology-rolt-translation.html, the whole treatise (five chapters) with Rolt's footnotes, as transcribed by CCEL. Read entire. Rolt's notes are the translator's and are marked as such wherever they are used.**

**The Darkness (ch. I).** The treatise opens with a prayer to be guided to where "the simple, absolute, and unchangeable mysteries of heavenly Truth lie hidden in the dazzling obscurity of the secret Silence", and then counsels Timothy to "leave the senses and the activities of the intellect" and strain "towards an union with Him whom neither being nor understanding can contain", to "the Ray of that divine Darkness which exceedeth all existence". Moses on the mountain is the type: after purification and the lights and trumpets he "meets not with God Himself", but only "the place wherein He dwells", and then is plunged "unto the Darkness of Unknowing wherein he renounces all the apprehensions of his understanding", so that "by a rejection of all knowledge he possesses a knowledge that exceeds his understanding". Rolt's note on the word (516) insists that the Greek *agnōstōs* means a transcendent unknowing distinguished from mere ignorance, and his long note 519 ends: "The sum of the whole matter is that God is incomprehensible."

**Negation as a method (chs. II-III).** The prayer of ch. II is to "attain unto vision through the loss of sight and knowledge", and the method is the sculptor's: as men "carving a statue out of marble, remove all the impediments that hinder the clear perceptive of the latent image", so the negative way strips qualities away to display what was hidden. Rolt's note 525 reads the simile as proof that the negative way "is, in the truest sense, positive". Ch. III sets the two ways in opposite motion. Affirmations descend from the most universal names and multiply as they go; negations ascend from the lowest and contract, "and when the whole ascent is passed it will be totally dumb, being at last wholly united with Him Whom words cannot describe". The order of negation is not arbitrary: "Surely it is truer to affirm that God is life and goodness than that He is air or stone", so one denies the remoter predicates first.

**The end of both ways (chs. IV-V).** God is none of the things sensibly perceived (ch. IV) and none of the things intelligibly perceived (ch. V): "not soul, or mind", "not number, or order", "not knowledge or truth", "nor is It one, nor is It unity, nor is It Godhead"; and then further, "nor does It belong to the category of non-existence or to that of existence", "nor can any affirmation or negation" apply to it, since it "transcends all affirmation by being the perfect and unique Cause of all things, and transcends all negation by the pre-eminence of Its simple and absolute nature". The treatise ends there, in a silence that has passed beyond negation as well as affirmation.

**What this is and is not.** The hidden here is not a set of withheld propositions that could in principle be stated; it is the divine nature beyond every predicate, and the route to it is subtraction rather than disclosure. The terminus is not an answer but a union without an answer. That makes it a third posture toward what God has not made known, distinct from Calvin's fence and from any inventory of mysteries: the apophatic way approaches the hidden, but by removing what one knows rather than by adding to it.

**Limits.** Rolt's 1920 translation, whose diction ("Divine Gloom", "super-essential") is his; the Greek is not on file. Rolt's footnotes carry his own theology (notes 519 and 524 especially) and are cited here only where flagged as his.
