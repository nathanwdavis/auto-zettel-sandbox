Own-words summary of [[gregory-of-nazianzus-epistle-101-to-cledonius-the-priest--202610031807]].

Gregory writes against the Apollinarians, who gave Christ a human body but put
the divine Word in place of a human mind. Whoever trusts in Christ "as a Man
without a human mind" is, he says, "quite unworthy of salvation". The reason
is the maxim later theology made famous: "For that which He has not assumed He
has not healed; but that which is united to His Godhead is also saved." He
presses it with Adam: "If only half Adam fell, then that which Christ assumes
and saves may be half also; but if the whole of his nature fell, it must be
united to the whole nature of Him that was begotten, and so be saved as a
whole."

In this passage the maxim is an argument about completeness. Its target is a
Christ missing one part of human nature, the mind. The passage does not take
up whether the nature Christ assumed carried original sin, or whether it was
in the condition of Adam before or after the fall. Later fallen-nature
Christologies cite the maxim for that further question; the letter itself
answers a different one.
