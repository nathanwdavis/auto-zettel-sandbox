**Scope:** This note is based on close reading of De Decretis sections 8-12 (a sample of the work's thirty-two sections). Within this range, simplicity functions in multiple directions.

In section 11, Athanasius addresses the Arian objection that human procreation provides a model for understanding God's procreation of the Son. He argues the comparison fails because of a fundamental difference in the nature of God and man.

The opening of section 11 uses simplicity as a **constraint on how generation must be understood**: human offspring are "portions of their fathers, since the very nature of bodies is not uncompounded, but in a state of flux and composed of parts." Because human nature is composite, human procreation necessarily involves partition. By contrast, "God, being without parts, is Father of the Son without partition or passion; for there is neither effluence of the Immaterial, nor influx from without, as among men; and being uncompounded in nature, He is Father of One Only Son."

However, immediately following: "**This is why He is Only-begotten**"—here simplicity (being uncompounded) functions as a **premise** explaining why the Son is uniquely begotten.

Athanasius also states that "**from which may be understood the impassible and impartitive nature of the Father**, in that not even a human word is begotten with passion or partition, much less the Word of God"—this phrase runs the argument in yet another direction, inferring the Father's simplicity FROM the Word's impassible nature rather than deriving the Son's status from simplicity.

Eternality of generation is grounded separately: "God, in that He ever is, is ever Father of the Son" (section 12), where eternality rests on the Father's constant being, not on simplicity per se.Within sections 8-12, simplicity appears in multiple logical roles: as a constraint on generation's manner, as a premise for the Son's unique status, and as a consequence inferred from the Word's nature.
