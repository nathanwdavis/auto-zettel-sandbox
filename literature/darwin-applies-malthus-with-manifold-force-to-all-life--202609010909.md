Own-words summary of the increase argument of
[[darwin-on-the-origin-of-species--202609010908]], Chapter III, "Struggle
for Existence" (core statements pp. 63-67, closing sentence p. 79).
Darwin's opening move is to take Malthus's two-ratio machinery and remove
its human escape valves: among animals and plants there is no growing
food by art and no restraint from prudence, so the geometric side of the
ledger runs at full force while the room it runs into stays fixed. He
makes the compounding claim universal — every organic being, without
exception, multiplies at a rate that would carry a single pair to
planetary coverage if nothing destroyed its offspring — and he
deliberately grounds it on the *slowest* case rather than the fastest:
the elephant calculation (one breeding pair, three pair of young across
sixty years) still yields fifteen million descendants in five centuries,
so the conclusion cannot be an artifact of picking a fecund species. The
struggle for existence is then not an occasional event but the standing
condition this arithmetic forces: since vastly more individuals are
produced than any country can support, destruction is continuous, and
whatever escapes it does so by out-competing the others pressing into the
same room. His summary image makes the bounded-room structure explicit —
nature as a surface already packed with wedges, where one wedge can be
driven in only by forcing others out.

Verbatim quotations, with the quoting policy recorded: wording is
accepted only where the raw capture's two textually independent blocks
(the Project Gutenberg hand-keyed transcription, block 1; the Darwin
Online scholarly transcription of F373, block 2) agree word-for-word;
dash style (block 1's em-dashes vs. the converted text's), the ligature
in "Linnæus", line-break hyphenation, and block 2's interleaved
"[page]" running heads are normalized. Page numbers are the printed ones
in block 2's page markers:

- "A struggle for existence inevitably follows from the high rate at
  which all organic beings tend to increase." (p. 63)
- "Every being, which during its natural lifetime produces several eggs
  or seeds, must suffer destruction during some period of its life, and
  during some season or occasional year, otherwise, on the principle of
  geometrical increase, its numbers would quickly become so inordinately
  great that no country could support the product." (p. 63)
- "It is the doctrine of Malthus applied with manifold force to the whole
  animal and vegetable kingdoms; for in this case there can be no
  artificial increase of food, and no prudential restraint from
  marriage." (p. 63)
- "There is no exception to the rule that every organic being naturally
  increases at so high a rate, that if not destroyed, the earth would
  soon be covered by the progeny of a single pair." (p. 64)
- "Linnæus has calculated that if an annual plant produced only two
  seeds — and there is no plant so unproductive as this — and their
  seedlings next year produced two, and so on, then in twenty years there
  would be a million plants." (p. 64)
- "The elephant is reckoned to be the slowest breeder of all known
  animals, and I have taken some pains to estimate its probable minimum
  rate of natural increase" ... "at the end of the fifth century there
  would be alive fifteen million elephants, descended from the first
  pair." (p. 64)
- "never to forget that every single organic being around us may be said
  to be striving to the utmost to increase in numbers; that each lives by
  a struggle at some period of its life" (p. 66)
- "The face of Nature may be compared to a yielding surface, with ten
  thousand sharp wedges packed close together and driven inwards by
  incessant blows, sometimes one wedge being struck, and then another
  with greater force." (p. 67)
- "the war of nature is not incessant, that no fear is felt, that death
  is generally prompt, and that the vigorous, the healthy, and the happy
  survive and multiply." (p. 79)

Scope note, recorded honestly: this note covers only Chapter III's
increase-and-checks argument — the span in the raw capture. The chapter's
survey of particular checks (climate, epidemics, predation, the complex
webs of dependence) is present in the capture but only gestured at here,
and the wider argument of the book — that this struggle, acting on
variation, yields natural selection — belongs to chapters not captured.
Darwin's Victorian natural-history examples are his 1859 evidence, not
established modern ecology; what this base takes from him is the
*structure*: universal compounding pressure meeting bounded room.
