Schmidt examines the surviving card index from Luhmann's literary estate —
about 90,000 handwritten A6 cards in two collections (roughly 23,000 from
1951–1962, 67,000 from 1963–1997) — and argues that its design made
unexpected discovery a systematic product rather than luck: the file "makes —
to use Robert Merton's term — serendipity possible in a systemically and
theoretically informed way" (sec. 1).

Four design features carry the argument. First, integration without taxonomy
(sec. 3): a new card only needed a local fit — "there must be a local solution
(i.e. connection or internal fit) only" — so sequences drift ever farther from
their opening subject, there are "no privileged positions in this web of
notes: there is no top and no bottom", and the same topic can be stored in
several places ("multiple storage"), each context generating different lines
of comparison. Second, fixed positional numbering (sec. 4): every card gets an
immutable number encoding where it sits, not what it means, which lets an
effectively infinite number of cards be inserted between any two neighbors —
Luhmann's "capacity for internal ramifications" — and makes each card
individually addressable. Third, explicit links (sec. 5): an estimated 20,000
references in the first collection and 30,000 in the second relate cards
"often thematically and spatial far away". Schmidt reads this
network-theoretically: serendipity is produced by "short cuts", references
that "lead to a completely different (both in terms of subject and location),
distant region in the network", and by hub cards of up to 25 collected
references that "provide access points to extensive parts of the file".
Placement can then be almost arbitrary — Luhmann: "The decision where to place
what in the file can involve a great deal of randomness as long as I add
references linking the other options" — at the price of permanent upkeep of
the reference web. Fourth, a deliberately incomplete keyword index (sec. 6):
typically "only one to four places" per term, on Ashby's model of the brain,
whose capacity "does not derive from a huge number of point-to-point-accesses
but on the relations between the nodes", the index being only an entry point
from which the internal references fan out.

The failure mode is the mirror image of the mechanism (sec. 5): a note "that
is not linked to the web of references becomes lost irretrievably in the bulk
of notes"; poorly linked regions behave like "black holes" that "remain
isolated later on and hence fade away" — Schmidt reports smaller parts of the
actual collection apparently untouched since their compilation. Schmidt's
summary (sec. 7) is that retrieval exceeds intention — the filing and
referencing technique "make it possible to retrieve more in a later query ...
than what was intended when the notes were initially taken" — and that the
file also worked as a thinking tool premised on Luhmann's dictum that
"without writing, there is no thinking", its never-deleted cards preserving
blind alleys as "the backstage of his theory".
