Ahrens opens by arguing that the quality of a piece of writing is set long
before the blank page: it depends on what was already written down while
reading and thinking, not on willpower or intelligence at the moment of
drafting. A blank page only panics someone who has nothing on hand to fill
it; someone with a stock of existing notes is assembling, not inventing
from nothing.

The chapter then distinguishes a *structure to work in* from a *plan
imposed on yourself*. A plan is a fixed sequence you must push through with
willpower, which is a poor fit for research and writing, where each new
insight changes what should come next. A trusted structure instead lets you
drop one task and pick up another without willpower, because you know
nothing is being lost in the process.

Ahrens credits David Allen's "Getting Things Done" for the core insight
that trusting a system frees attention for the immediate task, but argues
GTD does not transfer to insight-driven writing: GTD assumes fixed,
predefined objectives and concrete next steps, while research and writing
start from vague ideas whose direction can't be known in advance. The
chapter closes by introducing Niklas Luhmann's slip-box as the alternative
system built for that open-endedness.
