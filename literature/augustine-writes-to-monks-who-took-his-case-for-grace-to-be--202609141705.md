The opening chapter is the evidence that the question has changed hands. In
the second-century and third-century texts the opponent is outside the
church; here Augustine addresses "my brother Valentinus" and the monks
serving God with him at Hadrumetum, and the reason for writing is that "there
are dissensions among you on this subject". The dispute is internal, between
parties who are both trying to be orthodox.

He describes two errors, and he has to warn against both. The first is the
one he has already written against at length: "those persons who so preach
and defend man's free will" as "as boldly to deny, and endeavour to do away
with, the grace of God". The second is new, and it is the occasion of this
book — "there are some persons who so defend God's grace as to deny man's
free will", "or who suppose that free will is denied when grace is
defended". The second group are his own readers, who had drawn that
conclusion from his anti-Pelagian work. Augustine's problem in 426 is
therefore not a pagan fatalism but an over-reading of his own doctrine of
grace by fellow monks.

The method of the book follows from that. Chapter 2 proves free will from the
form of God's commands, on the old ground that a precept presupposes a power
to obey; chapter 6 then turns and warns that these same "testimonies of Holy
Scripture" adduced "in the maintenance of free will" must not "be understood
in such a way as to leave no room for God's assistance and grace in leading a
godly life". Both series of texts are conceded, and the work is spent
holding them together rather than choosing between them.

The chapter-6 title also records how Augustine dates his opponents: "God's
Grace to Be Maintained Against the Pelagians; The Pelagian Heresy Not an Old
One." He is claiming that the party is a recent arrival, which is itself a
witness that the intra-Christian form of this dispute was not felt to be
ancient when he wrote.
