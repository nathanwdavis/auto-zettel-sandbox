Own-words summary of [[calvin-institutes-of-the-christian-religion--202609011032]].

Chapter III asserts the inner channel: "the human mind, even by natural
instinct, possesses some sense of a Deity" -- laid down "as a position not to
be controverted." Calvin's evidence is anthropological (with Cicero as his
witness): no nation so barbarous as to lack persuasion "of the being of a
God"; even idolatry proves the point, since preferring to worship "a piece of
wood or stone, to being thought to have no god" shows how strong the
impression is. The purpose clause matters: God gave this apprehension so
that no one "might shelter himself under the pretext of ignorance" -- the
knowledge condemns as much as it informs.

Chapter V adds the outer channel and explicitly stacks it on the first: God
"hath not only sown in the minds of men the seed of religion, already
mentioned, but hath manifested himself in the formation of every part of the
world," so that men "cannot open their eyes without being constrained to
behold him." The epistemic structure is careful: "His essence indeed is
incomprehensible," but "on all his works he hath inscribed his glory in
characters so clear, unequivocal, and striking, that the most illiterate and
stupid cannot exculpate themselves by the plea of ignorance." Knowledge of
God from the works is real without being knowledge of the essence.

Two features for reuse. First, the display in the works is what a design
argument argues *from*, but Calvin does not run it as a demonstration for
doubters -- it functions as testimony that leaves everyone inexcusable, an
appeal to what is already seen rather than an inference to be won. Second,
the sequence is deliberately incomplete: the very next chapter's title hands
the task over -- "The Guidance And Teaching Of The Scripture Necessary To
Lead To The Knowledge Of God The Creator." The two natural channels open the
case; Scripture closes it.
