**Grounding: raw/202609151855-plato-apology-jowett-translation.html, the whole dialogue as served by the Internet Classics Archive; only the Delphi passage is used, and the Stephanus numbers are supplied from the standard pagination, since the Archive prints none.**

Chaerephon asked the oracle "whether there was anyone wiser than I was, and the Pythian prophetess answered that there was no man wiser." Socrates' first reaction is the premise of everything that follows: "What can the god mean? ... for I know that I have no wisdom, small or great." He tests the oracle on a politician "who had the reputation of wisdom" and finds that "he was not really wise, although he was thought wise by many, and wiser still by himself". The comparison he draws on leaving is the whole of the passage's philosophy: "although I do not suppose that either of us knows anything really beautiful and good, I am better off than he is - for he knows nothing, and thinks that he knows. I neither know nor think that I know. In this latter particular, then, I seem to have slightly the advantage of him."

**What the passage supplies.** Two things. First, a reflexive known unknown as the entire content of a wisdom: Socrates does not know, and knows that, and the second-order knowledge is what the oracle's verdict turns out to mean. Second, a fourth cell that a taxonomy of known and unknown does not contain: the politician who "knows nothing, and thinks that he knows" is neither a known unknown nor an unknown unknown but a false known, an unknown mistaken for a known. Socrates rates his own state above it, so the passage ranks the cells as well as naming them.

**Limits.** Jowett's Victorian English; the Archive does not say which of his editions it prints. The passage is about human wisdom generally, not about the knowledge of God, and is on file here as the oldest statement in the base of the posture the owner's note calls a known unknown, not as a theological source.
