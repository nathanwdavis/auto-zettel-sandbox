Origen's chapter is the first extended Christian treatise on the question,
and its longer title states the shape of the problem as he found it: "On the
Freedom of the Will, With an Explanation and Interpretation of Those
Statements of Scripture Which Appear to Nullify It." The threat to free will
is no longer Stoic fate or Gnostic natures alone. It is now Scripture — the
texts in which God is said to harden, to choose, and to dispose.

He opens from the church's preaching of judgement, which he takes to
presuppose "that it is within our own power to devote ourselves either to a
life that is worthy of praise, or to one that is worthy of censure," and only
then turns to the passages that cut against it. The bulk of the chapter is
that catalogue, worked one at a time: Pharaoh, whose heart God is said to
harden; Ezekiel's promise to take away the stony heart and give a heart of
flesh; the seed on rocky ground; and Romans 9. This is the first preserved
Christian text in which the sovereignty material is assembled as a body of
evidence requiring an answer.

On Romans 9 he states the deterministic reading in full before refusing it —
the potter "out of the same lump makes some vessels to honour" and others to
dishonour, and "so God creates some men for perdition, and others for
salvation", from which it follows that "it is not therefore in our own power
either to be saved or to perish; by which reasoning we appear not to be
possessed of free-will".
His first move against it is internal consistency rather than exegesis:
"Whether it is possible for the apostle to contradict himself?" — Paul blames
the Corinthian fornicators and praises the house of Onesiphorus, which he
could not do if the vessels were fixed at creation. He then reads the lump as
common nature, not as sorted destinies: "the nature of all rational souls is
the same, as one lump of clay is described as being under the treatment of
the potter."

The hardening is handled by making God's action occasioning rather than
producing. The same sun melts wax and dries mud, so one operation of God
meets two dispositions and yields opposite results; and delay in curing
Pharaoh is a physician's judgement, not malice, since "a rapid cure is not
for the advantage of those who are healed" when the disease would otherwise
rage inwardly. The asymmetry Origen wants — God causing the good directly and
the hardening only by occasion — is recognisably the move that Reformed and
evangelical writers still reach for, and it is made here in the third
century.
