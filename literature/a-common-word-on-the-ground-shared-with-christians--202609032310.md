Own-words summary of section III of [[a-common-word-between-us-and-you--202609032305]],
the concluding section of the 2007 open letter from 138 Muslim scholars to the
leaders of the Christian churches. Section III is where the letter states what
it takes the shared ground to be; sections I and II, which set out the two
commandments inside each scripture, were not captured and are not summarised
here.

**The shape of the argument.** The letter does not ask whether Muslims and
Christians worship the same God. It asks what the two religions hold in
*common*, and answers with two commandments rather than with an identity claim:
love of the one God, and love of the neighbour. It grants at the outset that
the two are different religions and that some of their formal differences
cannot be minimised, and it treats that concession as compatible with the
common ground rather than as an obstacle to it. The unity of God is brought in
as what the two commandments arise out of and are prefaced by -- it is the
premise of the shared command, not the conclusion the letter is arguing to.

**How the ground is established.** By putting the traditions' own texts
alongside each other: the Shema, Jesus' answer about the greatest commandment
in Mark 12, and Surah al-Ikhlas. The letter then argues from within the Qur'an
that this is what should be expected, since it presents Muhammad as bringing
nothing essentially new and as one messenger among those sent to every nation
with the same message. So the claim is not that the two religions have
converged; it is that they were never carrying different fundamentals.

**The condition attached.** The invitation is a quotation of Qur'an 3:64 and
carries that verse's terms: to worship none but God, to ascribe no partner to
Him, and for none to take others as lords beside God. The letter glosses the
second of these as bearing on divine unity and the first on total devotion. The
third it reads through al-Tabari's commentary as a claim about not obeying
others in disobedience to God -- which it turns towards religious freedom and
justice between the communities, rather than towards doctrine.

**What it says about Christ, and about Christians.** Muslims recognise Jesus as
the Messiah "not in the same way Christians do", and the letter states the
Qur'anic account it does hold (messenger, word, spirit). It adds, parenthetically,
that Christians have never all agreed among themselves about Christ's nature.
It then applies the gospel sayings about who is with and against to argue that
Christians should count Muslims as with them rather than against them, citing
Theophylact's reading to reconcile the sayings.

**Register.** The letter's stated stakes are peace between the two communities
and the survival of the world, not the settlement of a doctrinal question, and
it closes by asking that differences not produce hatred and that each vie with
the other in good works. That register matters for how much weight the letter
can bear: it is an appeal issued to a particular audience for a stated
practical purpose, and a claim about what Muslim theology holds *in general*
does not follow from it.
