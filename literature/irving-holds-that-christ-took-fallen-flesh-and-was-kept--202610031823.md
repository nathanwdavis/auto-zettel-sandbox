Own-words summary of [[edward-irving-collected-writings-vol-5-sermons-on-the--202610031814]].

In the third of his six sermons on the incarnation, whose method he calls
taking up the fallen humanity (pp. 114ff.), Irving argues "That Christ took
our fallen nature" and that this "is most manifest, because there was no other
in existence to take" (pp. 115-16). Unless God had made the Virgin as Adam was
before the fall, which he dismisses as "a figment of Romish superstition", no
human nature but a fallen one existed for Christ to share. So Christ's flesh,
"like my flesh, was in its proper nature mortal and corruptible", and he was
of "the seed of the woman after she fell, and not before she fell".

Later in the same sermon (pp. 169-70) he goes further than mortality and
openness to temptation. He will maintain "that Christ's flesh was as rebellious
as ours, as fallen as ours", and he sees the Holy Ghost at work "subduing,
restraining, conquering, the evil propensities of the fallen manhood". The
flesh, on this account, carried the fallen inclination itself; what kept
Christ holy was the Spirit's mastery of it, not its absence.

Irving denies that any of this makes Christ a sinner. He was holy throughout,
"seeing wickedness consisteth not in being tempted, but in yielding to the
temptation" (p. 127). The opposite view, that Christ's flesh was unfallen, he
calls "a virtual denial of His humanity". In the appendix to the treatise
(from p. 563; the passage follows the p. 566 running head) he states the
whole dispute in one sentence: "Whether Christ's flesh had the grace of
sinlessness and incorruption from its proper nature, or from the indwelling of
the Holy Ghost. I say the latter." The flesh, "in its proper nature it was as
the flesh of His mother", was kept sinless by the Spirit's indwelling.
