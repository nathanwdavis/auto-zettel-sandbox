Own-words summary of
[[qurtubi-al-jami-li-ahkam-al-quran-on-3-64-and-29-46--202609040800]]. Every
English rendering below is this repository's own working translation of the
captured Arabic, and the Arabic is printed beside each rendering that carries
weight.

**On Q 29:46.** al-Qurtubi announces his own structure: `فيه مسألتان` — this
verse contains two questions. The first is whether the prohibition on disputing
with the People of the Book except in the better way is still in force. He sets
out Mujahid's view that it is unabrogated and that the "better way" means
calling them to God and drawing attention to His proofs, `لا على طريق الإغلاظ
والمخاشنة` (not by way of harshness and rudeness); a second reading on which
the address is to the People of the Book who had already believed; and
Qatada's view that the verse is abrogated by the fighting verse. He then reports, on
al-Nahhas's authority, the argument the abrogationists themselves used — `من قال
هي منسوخة احتج بأن الآية مكية`, whoever held it abrogated argued from the verse
being Meccan, at a time when no fighting was prescribed and no *jizya* sought —
and comes down against them: Mujahid's view is the good one, because God's
rulings are not called abrogated `إلا بخبر يقطع العذر أو حجة من معقول` (except
by a report that removes all excuse, or a rational proof), a position he notes
Ibn al-'Arabi also chose. On
the exception "those of them who do wrong" he transmits Mujahid and Sa'id ibn
Jubayr: those who have taken up war against the believers, `فجدالهم بالسيف حتى
يؤمنوا أو يعطوا الجزية` — disputing with them is by the sword until they
believe or pay the *jizya*.

The second question is the clause "and say: we believe in what was sent down to
us and sent down to you". He answers it entirely with transmitted reports:
al-Bukhari's report from Abu Hurayra that the People of the Book read the Torah
in Hebrew and expounded it in Arabic, and the Prophet said `لا تصدقوا أهل
الكتاب ولا تكذبوهم` (do not believe the People of the Book and do not disbelieve
them); Ibn Mas'ud's warning not to question them at all; and Mu'awiya's remark
about Ka'b al-Ahbar, that he was among the most truthful of those who transmit
from the People of the Book and that they nevertheless caught falsehood in what
he said.

**What is absent, and it is the point of this note.** The verse's next clause
is `وإلهنا وإلهكم واحد` — "and our God and your God is one". al-Qurtubi does
not lemmatise it, does not gloss it, and does not report anyone else's gloss on
it. His comment on the verse ends with the Mu'awiya report. The word `معبود` —
"object of worship", the word in which al-Tabari states the identification —
does not occur anywhere in his comment on this verse. Two independent servings
of the text end at the same sentence, so this is the extent of the comment and
not a truncation.

**On Q 3:64.** Here he does comment at length, and in the same direction as
al-Tabari. He organises the verse into three questions. `سواء` is glossed
`العدل والنصفة` — justice and equity. He reports, from al-Farra', a variant
reading attributed to `عبد الله` — `إلى كلمة عدل بيننا وبينكم` ("to a word of
justice between us and you"). The text names only `عبد الله`; that this is Ibn
Mas'ud is the standard identification and not something the capture states. The
sense he then draws is: answer what you are called to, which is the equitable,
upright word `التي ليس فيها ميل عن الحق` (in which there is no deviation from
the truth). The content of that word is the verse's own `ألا نعبد إلا الله`.
He reads "let none of us take others as lords besides God" as not following
anyone in declaring a thing lawful or forbidden except as God has, turns it
polemically against unanchored juristic preference and against those who would
accept an imam's ruling without a stated legal basis, and glosses "bear witness
that we are Muslims" as: professing the religion of Islam, submitting to its
rulings, `غير متخذين أحدا ربا لا عيسى ولا عزيرا ولا الملائكة` — taking none as
lord, not Jesus, not Ezra, not the angels. He also quotes the Prophet's letter
to Heraclius, in which the verse appears inside a summons to accept Islam.
