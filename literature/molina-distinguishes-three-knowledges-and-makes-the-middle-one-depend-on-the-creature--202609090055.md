**Where this sits.** Disputation 52 belongs to Molina's commentary on *Summa* I q. 14 a. 13, whether God knows future contingents. The passage opens by saying that three knowledges must be distinguished in God, "nisi periculose in concilianda libertate arbitrii nostri et contingentia rerum cum divina praescientia hallucinari velimus" — unless we wish to go dangerously astray in reconciling the freedom of our will and the contingency of things with divine foreknowledge. The reconciliation is the stated purpose of the distinction.

**The three knowledges (pp. 317-318).** The first is "mere naturalem, quae proinde nulla ratione potuit esse aliter in Deo": purely natural, which therefore could in no way have been otherwise in God, and by which he knows everything to which the divine power extends, including contingents, though only as able indifferently to be or not to be. The second is "mere liberam, qua Deus post liberum actum suae voluntatis absque hypothesi et conditione aliqua cognovit absolute et determinate" which contingents "re ipsa essent futurae, quae non item": purely free, by which God, after the free act of his will and without any hypothesis or condition, knew absolutely and determinately which would in fact be future and which not. The third is the middle: "Tertiam denique mediam scientiam, qua ex altissima et inscrutabili comprehensione cujusque liberi arbitrii in sua essentia intuitus est, quid pro sua innata libertate, si in hoc, vel illo, vel etiam infinitis rerum ordinibus collocaretur, acturum esset, cum tamen posset, si vellet, facere re reipsa oppositum" — by which, out of the most profound and inscrutable comprehension of each free will, he saw in his own essence what it would do by its innate freedom if it were placed in this or that or even infinitely many orders of things, although it could, if it willed, in fact do the opposite. The marginal glosses label the three "Mere naturalis," "Omnino libera," and "Media inter utraque."

**Neither free nor natural, but partly each.** Asked whether this middle knowledge should be called free or natural, Molina answers that it cannot be called free, "tum quia antecedit omnem liberum actum voluntatis divinae, tum etiam quia in potestate Dei non fuit scire per eam scientiam aliud quam re ipsa sciverit" — both because it precedes every free act of the divine will, and because it was not in God's power to know by it anything other than what he in fact knew. Nor is it natural in the sense of being so innate that God could not have known the opposite: had the created will been going to do the opposite, "idipsum scivisset per eandem scientiam" — he would have known that very thing by the same knowledge. It therefore has partly the condition of natural knowledge, in preceding the will's free act, and partly that of free knowledge, in that which side it holds "habet ex eo quod liberum arbitrium, ex hypothesi quod crearetur in uno aut altero ordine rerum, esset potius facturum unum quam aliud, cum utrumvis indifferenter posset facere" — it has from the fact that the free will, on the hypothesis of being created in one or another order of things, would rather do the one than the other, although it could indifferently do either.

**The list of what coheres (p. 318).** Anticipating that the doctrine will alarm on first sight, Molina lists what "apertissime inter se convenire et cohaerere" — most plainly agree and cohere with one another. "Nihil esse in potestate creaturae, quod etiam non sit in potestate Dei": nothing is in the creature's power that is not also in God's. "Deum sua omnipotentia inflectere posse nostrum liberum arbitrium quocunque ipse voluerit, praeterquam in peccatum": God can by his omnipotence bend our free will wherever he wills, except into sin, which would imply a contradiction. God can do alone whatever he does through a secondary cause. "Deum permittere posse peccata, non vero praecipere, aut ad ea incitare vel inclinare": God can permit sins, but not command them or incite or incline to them. And a free thing's turning one way or the other, when placed in a definite order of things and circumstances, "non provenire ex praescientia Dei, quin potius ideo Deum id praescire, quia ipsa res libero arbitrio praedicta libere id ipsum agere debet" — does not come from God's foreknowledge; rather God foreknows it because the thing endowed with free will is going freely to do that very thing — "neque provenire ex eo quod Deus velit id ab ea fieri, sed ex eo quod ipsa libere id velit facere" — nor does it come from God's willing it to be done by the creature, but from the creature's freely willing to do it. From which, he says, it most plainly follows that the knowledge by which God, before deciding to create a will, foresees what it would do in a given order of things, "pendere ex eo quod ipsa pro sua libertate hoc vel illud sit factura et non e contrario" — depends on the fact that it would by its freedom do this or that, and not the reverse. Free knowledge is the opposite case: it "semper est in Deo libera, pendetque a determinatione libera suae voluntatis, qua tale liberum arbitrium in tali vel tali ordine rerum creare statuit" — is always free in God and depends on the free determination of his will by which he decided to create such a free will in such an order of things.

**Which knowledge is the cause of what (pp. 323-324).** Created things are of three kinds: from God immediately; through secondary causes acting by necessity of nature; and those that "a libero arbitrio creato emanant" — issue from created free will. Of the first two kinds God is the cause "per solam scientiam mere naturalem" together with the free determination of his will, because natural knowledge alone "rationem habet artis" — has the character of an art, by which God knows how to make things and provide for each toward its end; an art does not operate until the artificer's will commands its execution, so natural knowledge is the remote cause and the will's determination "proxima ac sufficiens." For the third kind the account changes. So that God might exercise due providence over free will toward both its natural and its supernatural end — "nunc hominem variis eventibus exercendo, nunc defectus illius tolerando et permittendo nunc illum vocando, eique opitulando, atque illum in bonum promovendo" — now exercising a man by various events, now tolerating and permitting his failures, now calling him, helping him and advancing him in good — and so that he "quosdam homines aut angelos posset praedestinare, omniaque in debitos fines ordinare" — could predestine certain men or angels and order all things to their due ends — "necessaria etiam fuit scientia illa media, qua, ex hypothesi quod hunc vel illum ordinem rerum vellet efficere, certo praevideret ea omnia quae pro libertate arbitrii, tam angelici quam humani, in unoquoque eorum ordinum essent futura" — that middle knowledge was also necessary, by which, on the hypothesis that he willed to bring about this or that order of things, he foresaw with certainty everything that would come to be by the freedom of angelic and human will in each of those orders. God is therefore the cause of free acts remotely through both knowledges and proximately "per determinationem suae voluntatis, qua statuens collocare homines et angelos in eo ordine rerum in quo eos collocavit, simul statuit hoc vel illo modo cum libero arbitrio illorum cooperari" — through the determination of his will by which, deciding to place men and angels in the order of things in which he placed them, he at the same time decided to cooperate in this or that way with their free will. Of sins he is not the cause: "Ne tamen intelligas causam ullo modo esse peccatorum : haec enim, quoad culpam et defectum, in solum arbitrium creatam tanquam in causam reducuntur" — do not, however, understand him to be in any way the cause of sins: these, as to fault and defect, are reduced to the created will alone as their cause. Free knowledge, coming after the will's determination in which "tota ratio causae" is complete, "nullo modo est causa rerum" — is in no way the cause of things.

**Knowledge follows the thing, not the reverse (p. 324).** The marginal gloss states the principle: "Futura contingentia non quia sciuntur a Deo ideo futura sunt, sed e contrario" — future contingents are not future because they are known by God, but the reverse. The text grants that God takes no knowledge from things and knows all in his essence and in the determination of his will, "attamen non quia cognoscit aliquid esse futurum, ideo illud futurum, sed e contrario, quia illud futurum est ex suis causis, ideo cognoscere illud esse futurum" — yet it is not because he knows something to be future that it is future, but the reverse: because it is future from its causes, he knows it to be future. Molina runs this through all three kinds of thing, ending with free acts, which "ita sunt futurae ut possint non esse" — are future in such a way that they can not-be — and are foreseen "per scientiam naturalem, et per illam mediam inter mere naturalem et liberam."

**Tyre and Sidon (p. 337).** In disputation 53 Molina turns on opponents who, having rejected as dangerous the opinion that God does not know conditional futures at all, still hold that he knows them only as possibles: "Renuunt namque concedere medium inter futurum simpliciter et possibile merum, idque ut mediam scientiam effugiant" — for they refuse to concede a middle between the simply future and the merely possible, and this in order to escape middle knowledge. Against them stands Matthew 11: on the hypothesis of the same miracles, Christ asserted the repentance of Tyre and Sidon "futuram fuisse," while that of Chorazin and Bethsaida "solum permanserit intra limites rei possibilis" — remained only within the limits of the possible — so the middle must be conceded, and the difference arose "ex minore duritie et culpa proprii arbitrii" — from the lesser hardness and fault of their own will. Those who refer all the certainty of future contingents to an efficacious help and to God's predefinition of giving it, he says, "enervare atque pervertere verba illa Christi Matthaei 11" — enervate and pervert those words of Christ — since on their account Tyre and Sidon would have been converted not from the hypothesis itself but because God had predefined an efficacious help for them that he withheld from Chorazin and Bethsaida.
