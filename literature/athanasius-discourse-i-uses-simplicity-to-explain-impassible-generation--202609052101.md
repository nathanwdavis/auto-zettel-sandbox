**Scope:** This note is based on close reading of Discourse I sections 19-32 (14 of the discourse's 64 sections). Within this range, simplicity functions as a constraint on how generation's manner must be understood. Premise-form arguments for the Son's status (simplicity as ground for conclusion) appear in De Decretis sections 11 and 22, beyond this passage's scope.

In sections 21-22, Athanasius addresses the Arian objection that if the Son is begotten, He must also beget others, leading to infinite regress: "as He is begotten, so He begets, and He too becomes father of a son. And again, he who is begotten from Him, begets in his turn, and so on without limit." Athanasius responds that God is not like man, and therefore such succession does not follow.

In sections 26-28, Athanasius answers a different objection—the temporal-priority charge from human analogy: "Hadst thou a son before bearing?" He addresses this by distinguishing divine nature from human nature, emphasizing that humans beget passibly and transiently. Here he introduces simplicity: "God is not as man; for men beget passibly, having a transitive nature... But with God this cannot be; **for He is not composed of parts, but being impassible and simple, He is impassibly and indivisibly Father of the Son.**" (Discourse I.28).

Within sections 19-32, simplicity appears only at section 28. The preceding argument (sections 19-27) rests on other premises: that the Son is "proper to the Father's essence" (not external), the Image and Radiance imagery; the senses of “unoriginate” are canvassed later, at 30-31. When simplicity does appear at 28, it functions as a **constraint**: it explains why, given God's nature, the generation cannot involve the passion and partition inherent in human procreation. The argument does not run "simplicity therefore eternal generation" but rather "given simplicity, we must understand generation as lacking partition and succession.
