**The definition and its placement.** Middle knowledge, first attributed to God by Molina, "is best characterized as God’s prevolitional knowledge of all true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom," and its proponents see it as "the key to understanding the compatibility of divine providence and creaturely (libertarian) freedom." It is so named because it stands between the two categories inherited from Aquinas: "middle knowledge is like natural knowledge in that it is prevolitional, or prior to God’s choice to create. This, of course, also means that the content of middle knowledge is true independent of God’s will and therefore, He has no control over it. Yet, it is not the same as natural knowledge because, like free knowledge, its content is contingent." Its content is "truths which refer to what would be the case if various states of affairs were to obtain."

**Three assumptions.** The first is libertarian: "for an action to be free, it must be determined by the agent performing the action. This means that God cannot will a free creature to act in a particular way and the act still be free"; and "free creatures have the ability to choose between competing alternatives, and really could choose one or the other of the alternatives." Influence and persuasion are allowed, determination is not. The second is that the divine deliberation has a logical order: "This is not to deny the simplicity or omniscience of God, or to say that He gains knowledge that He did not previously possess," but "a relationship of logical priority, but not necessarily temporal priority exists between some of the content of divine knowledge." The third, that things could have been otherwise, is stated in the uncaptured part of the section.

**The three knowledges.** Natural knowledge is "that part of God’s knowledge which He knows by His very nature or essence, and since His essence is necessary, so is that which is known through it"; it includes all necessary truths and all possibilities, is independent of God's will, and "is prevolitional." Free knowledge is "that part of God’s knowledge which He knows by His knowledge of His own will, both His desires and what He will, in fact, do"; its content is contingent and it "is postvolitional; it is dependent upon God’s will." Middle knowledge shares prevolitionality with the first and contingency with the second, and its content "can be thought of as including a virtually infinite number of propositions of the form, If person, P, were in situation, S, then P would freely perform action, A". The entry adds, as a basic assumption, "that God cannot will a creature to freely choose anything."

**Actualization, strong and weak.** In the possible-worlds vocabulary the entry adopts, "God can actualize a world where His will is brought about by the free decisions of creatures," but only by distinguishing two kinds of actualization: "Strong actualization refers to the efforts of a being when it causally determines the occurrence of an event (e.g., God causes something to happen), while weak actualization refers to the contribution of a being to the occurrence of an event by placement of a free creature in circumstances in which he will freely cause the event." The entry then states the cost in its own voice: "it must be noted that it implies that there may be some states of affairs that God cannot weakly actualize, which leads to the further conclusion that there may be some possible worlds that God cannot actualize."

**The objections, in three families.** The first rejects libertarian freedom. "The principle objection to middle knowledge in Molina’s day was that it afforded creatures such a high view of freedom that God’s providence was compromised"; today it takes the form either of an appeal to God's transcendence, or of the compatibilist alternative: "Whereas libertarian freedom is seen as the ability to choose between competing alternatives, compatibilist freedom is seen as the ability to choose in accordance with one’s desires." The libertarian reply recorded is that "proponents of compatibilist freedom must believe that God possesses libertarian freedom in order to avoid theological fatalism," so the coherence of libertarian freedom is conceded by everyone, and only its truth of creatures remains in dispute. Within the second family, the argument from divine risk is reported thus: since middle knowledge with free knowledge gives God comprehensive knowledge of the future, "all risk on God’s part is removed; He cannot be surprised and further, He specifically planned for everything that will occur," and the objectors say true freedom requires risk; the Molinist answer is "that it begs the question of compatibilism."

**The grounding objection and five replies.** The most popular objection, which the entry presents as the one the other objections rest on, is that "there appears to be no good answer to the question of what grounds the truth of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom. They cannot be grounded in God because determinism would follow," and "they also cannot be grounded in the individuals to which they refer for at least four reasons": the counterfactuals are true before the individuals exist; the individuals' existence depends on God's will; counterfactuals refer to what never happens; and grounding in psychological makeup would mean the acts were not free. Five replies are surveyed: that "counterfactuals of freedom do not need to be grounded," being brute facts; that the objection "begs the question of compatibilism" by assuming a causal link between antecedent and consequent; that propositions about the actual future "are either true or false now, even though there is nothing in the present that can be pointed to as grounding their truth," and counterfactuals are no worse off; that their truth may be grounded "in the occurrence or nonoccurrence of the event in the closest possible-but-not-actual world to the actual world"; and that it is grounded "in the individuals to which they refer as they exist in the precreative mind of God as ideas." The entry's own verdict is double-edged: some replies "may be seen as more of a shifting of the burden of proof than an answer to the specific objection," yet the demand for grounding "is somewhat unclear," and "some sort of idea of grounding is at least conceivable."
