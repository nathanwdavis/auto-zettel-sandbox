The chapter's own summary states both halves of Irenaeus' position at once:
"Men are possessed of free will, and endowed with the faculty of making a
choice. It is not true, therefore, that some are by nature good, and others
bad." The second sentence is the point of the first. Free will is not being
defended for its own sake but as the premise that destroys the Valentinian
doctrine of fixed natures, on which the spiritual are saved and the material
lost by constitution rather than by choice.

The argument is from creation and from the form of God's commands. Christ's
lament "How often would I have gathered your children together, and you would
not" is read as setting forth "the ancient law of human liberty, because God
made man a free [agent] from the beginning, possessing his own power," a
creature made "to obey the behests ( ad utendum sententia ) of God
voluntarily, and not by compulsion of God". The reason given is a claim about God's manner of
dealing rather than about human psychology: there is "no coercion with God".
Irenaeus extends the same freedom to angels, and then to faith itself —
"has God preserved the will of man free and under his own
control" in faith as well as in works — so that even believing is not something
worked in a man against his will.

The desert argument is Justin's, sharpened against the specific opponent:
"But if some had been made by nature bad, and others good, these latter would
not be deserving of praise for being good, for such were they created; nor
would the former be reprehensible, for thus they were made". The positive
claim is uniformity of nature — "all men are of the same nature, able both to
hold fast and to do what is good" — and the difference between men is
therefore located in use, not in kind.

As with Justin, the contrast that would later define the debate is absent.
Irenaeus is not weighing human freedom against divine sovereignty; he is
using human freedom to defend the justice of one Creator God against a
system that distributed salvation by nature. Divine sovereignty is on his
side of the argument, not across from it.
