Own-words summary of [[aquinas-st-ii-ii-q103-dulia--202609042052]].

Article 3 asks "whether dulia is a special virtue distinct from latria," and the
objections are the case for saying no — that honour paid to a man made in God's
image is the same movement as honour paid to God, since "the movement whereby
one is moved towards an image is the same as the movement whereby one is moved
towards the thing represented."

Aquinas answers with a criterion of *aspect*: "where there are different aspects
of that which is due, there must needs be different virtues to render those
dues. Now servitude is due to God and to man under different aspects." God "has
absolute and paramount lordship over the creature wholly and singly"; a man
"partakes of a certain likeness to the divine lordship, forasmuch as he
exercises a particular power." Hence "dulia, which pays due service to a human
lord, is a distinct virtue from latria, which pays due service to the lordship
of God."

The reply to the first objection sharpens what marks the difference: "the
creature does not partake of the power to create by reason of which latria is
due to God." Creation is the aspect no creature shares, and latria is the
service owed under that aspect.

Two things follow for this repository's purposes. First, on Aquinas's account
worship is not distinguished from other honour by *intensity* — dulia is not a
smaller latria — but by the description under which the act is rendered. Second,
the distinction is doing exactly the work Maimonides refuses to let it do: it
licenses honouring God's servants without that honour becoming worship of them.
Augustine, quoted here on the contrary side, supplies the authority: dulia is
"distinct from latria which denotes the homage that consists in the worship of
God."
