Own-words summary of
[[agarwal-nunes-and-blunt-retrieval-practice-in-schools-and-classrooms--202609040505]],
read in full in the authors' accepted manuscript. Quotations are the authors'
words; everything else is this note's characterisation. All page locators are
**accepted-manuscript** pages, not journal pages — see the reference note.

**What they set out to do, and the narrowing that defines the paper.** Prior
reviews of retrieval practice mix laboratory and classroom studies: the authors
point out that Adesope et al. 2017 pooled 217 studies of which 11% were from
classrooms (p. 5). Their complaint is that "what works in the laboratory does not
necessarily work in the classroom—and vice versa" (p. 5), so recommendations
drawn from the mixture may be too unspecific to act on. They therefore screened
"nearly 2,000 abstracts" (p. 42) against a deliberately narrow definition of
classroom research — retrieval practice administered individually, in person,
in a real class; no laboratory studies, no collaborative retrieval, no online
quizzing — and kept 37 studies, 50 experiments, 49 effect sizes, total
n = 5,374 (p. 42). Their operational definition of the thing being tested is "an
active attempt by a student to recall or recognize, and then reconstruct, their
memory of knowledge during initial learning" (p. 7).

**They refuse to meta-analyse, and say why.** This is the paper's most
consequential methodological choice and it is easy to miss, because the paper is
cited elsewhere as one of three meta-analyses. "[W]e felt that conditions across
the 50 experiments included in the present review were too varied for a
meta-analytic approach" (p. 7). Three reasons are given (pp. 6–7): effect sizes
within these studies are frequently non-independent (one condition compared
against several controls, or the reverse, or data collapsed across conditions);
recent work questions random-effects models on complex data and finds the
reproducibility of meta-analytic means low; and the prior reviews reported
pooled weighted effects without individual effect sizes, sample sizes or
confidence intervals. In place of a pooled estimate they publish every
individual effect size with its 95% confidence interval, in four forest plots
and an appendix.

**What they found.** Of 49 effect sizes (Cohen's *d*), 16 were large (*d* > 0.80),
12 medium (0.50 < *d* < 0.80), 18 small or very small (*d* < 0.50), and three
negative — that is, three favoured the comparison condition, lessons without
quizzes (p. 23). "[T]he majority of effect sizes (57%) indicated medium or large
benefits from retrieval practice" (p. 23); the complement, which the abstract does
not put in a headline, is that 43% were small, very small or negative. On
precision rather than magnitude the picture is more uniform: "only six confidence
intervals around positive effect sizes extend below a Cohen's *d* of 0.00"
(p. 23). Their verdict on their own question is stated without hedging: "our
response for researchers and educators is an unequivocal 'yes'" (p. 42).

**The finding this base did not expect, and the one it should carry.** The
benefit *shrank* as the delay to the final test grew. "Effect sizes were larger
following a 1-3 day delay, while smaller following an end-of-semester delay"
(p. 35) — and the authors immediately name the problem this creates: "in
laboratory research, the opposite effect has been shown—longer delays lead to a
larger benefit" (p. 35), citing Roediger and Karpicke 2006 among others. In the
discussion they repeat it as a research priority and offer a candidate
explanation: classroom delays (6–15 weeks to a final exam) are far longer than
laboratory delays, most of which are under a week, so "benefits from retrieval
practice increase in the first few days, and then taper off as delays approach
weeks or months" (p. 43). That is an explanation, not a result; nothing in this
review measures the taper directly, because delay was never manipulated within a
study — it is a coding variable across studies with everything else varying too.

**The limits the authors put on their own review.** Publication bias, stated
directly: "As is the case for all systematic reviews, ours is subject to
publication bias" (p. 42), sharpened by two facts they volunteer — the search
returned no qualifying study published before 1999, and "we did not include
unpublished studies" (p. 42). Sample: "only three out of 50 experiments that met
our screening criteria were conducted outside the United States and Western
Europe" (p. 46), Turkey, Pakistan and Taiwan, n = 266 of 5,374. Coverage: 35 of
the 50 experiments were in science or psychology courses, and none involved
foreign-language learning. Reporting: "[f]or 24 comparisons, data were
insufficiently reported to calculate an effect size" (p. 21) — roughly a third
of the comparisons found could not be scored at all. And the comparison
condition is usually re-reading (k = 19), which the authors themselves say is
too easy a rival; they ask future work to test retrieval practice against
lectures, flipped classrooms and discussion instead (p. 44).

**What it does not touch.** Nothing in these 65 pages concerns notes. A search
of the full extracted text for `note-taking`, `note taking`, `notetaking`,
`external memory`, `offloading`, `slip box` and `zettelkasten` returns zero
occurrences, and the search is recorded in the capture. That is now three
retrieval-practice sources in a row in this base with the same result.
