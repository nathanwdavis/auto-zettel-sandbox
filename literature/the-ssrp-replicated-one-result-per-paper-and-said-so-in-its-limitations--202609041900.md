Summary of
[[camerer-et-al-evaluating-the-replicability-of-social-science-experiments--202609041855]]
in my own words.

**What the project did.** Twenty-one experimental social-science papers
published in *Nature* and *Science* between 2010 and 2015 were selected by
three stated criteria — an experimental treatment effect, at least one clearly
stated significant hypothesis, and an accessible subject pool — and one result
from each was replicated once. The replications were pre-registered and their
analysis plans were sent to the original authors for review before data
collection. Sample sizes were about five times the originals' on average, run
in two stages: stage 1 powered at 90% to detect 75% of the original effect
size, and, for anything that did not clear P < 0.05 there, a stage 2 powered at
90% to detect 50% of it (p. 638).

**The headline numbers.** Thirteen of the 21 (61.9%) produced a significant
effect in the same direction as the original. Complementary indicators put the
figure between 12 (57.1%, the "small telescopes" criterion) and 14 (66.7%, the
95% prediction interval), and a Bayesian mixture model estimated the
true-positive rate at 67%. Mean standardised effect size was 0.249 against the
originals' 0.460 — a mean relative effect size of 46.2%, which divides sharply:
74.5% among the 13 that replicated, 0.3% among the 8 that did not (pp. 637-638).
The project reads that split as two distinct problems in the original
literature: some false positives, and inflated effect sizes even among the true
ones, the mixture model putting the true positives' real effects at about 71%
of what was published.

**The finding the paper is proudest of.** Before the results were known, peer
beliefs were elicited by survey and by prediction market. The market's average
forecast was a 63.4% replication rate and the survey's 60.6%, against an
observed 61.9%; the two belief measures correlate with each other and each
correlates strongly with which individual studies replicated (p. 639). The
authors draw a specific inference from that, not just a pleasing one: if
failures were chance, forecasts could not have tracked them, so "researchers
seem to have identified a priori systematic differences between the studies
that replicated and those that did not" (p. 640).

**The limitation that matters most to this repository**, stated by the project
about itself: for a paper reporting a series of studies it replicated only the
first study reporting a significant treatment effect, and within that study
only one hypothesis, so "this study does not provide definitive insight on any
of the original papers from which we designed the replication studies"
(p. 640). It adds that the direction of the resulting bias is unknown — if
first studies replicate differently from later ones, the selection rule
systematically over- or under-estimates replicability, and it names two sets of
original authors who said in published Correspondence that a later study in
their paper was the more important one (p. 641).

**Three of the 21 are sources or near-neighbours of this base's own cluster.**
The enumerated stage lists (p. 638), read against the figure legends' key,
place Karpicke and Blunt (2011) among the twelve that replicated in stage 1 and
Pyc and Rawson (2010) among the two added in stage 2; Sparrow et al. (2011)
appears in neither list. The Sparrow replication is also singled out twice: it
is the one of 21 not designed with the original authors' collaboration, because
they did not respond to the project's queries, and the one for which the
original authors supplied no materials, raised design differences only after
the replication was complete, and left the project saying "we cannot rule out
that they influenced the replication result" (pp. 640-641).

**What the paper declines to claim.** For the eight non-replications, the
average relative effect size was near zero, but the authors refuse the
inference that those eight were false positives: an unimplemented feature of
the original protocol would produce the same picture, and they say they have no
evidence either way (p. 640). They are equally careful about the rate itself,
setting 62% beside the RPP's 36%, the EERP's 61%, and Many Labs 1, 2 and 3 at
77%, 50% and 30%, and concluding it is "too early to draw a specific
conclusion" about reproducibility rates in the field (p. 641).
