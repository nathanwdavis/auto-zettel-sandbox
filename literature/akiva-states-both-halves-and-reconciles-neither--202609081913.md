The mishnah runs: "Everything is foreseen yet freedom of choice is granted, And the world is judged with goodness; And everything is in accordance with the preponderance of works" — *ha-kol tzafui, ve-ha-reshut netunah*.

Three things about it are worth stating precisely, because it is short enough to be made to say more than it does.

It asserts both halves and reconciles neither. There is no "yet" of concession in the Hebrew, only the two clauses set side by side by the conjunction; the English "yet" is the translator's reading of the juxtaposition, and it is a reasonable one, but the mishnah offers no account of how the two consist together. The pair is stated, not solved.

The word for what is granted is *reshut* — permission, authority, jurisdiction — the same word Maimonides opens Hilchot Teshuvah 5:1 with (*reshut le-khol adam netunah*). So the Mishneh Torah's formulation is the mishnah's, and Maimonides is elaborating a received formula rather than coining one.

And the saying does not move toward metaphysics. Having named the pair it turns immediately to judgement and to works, which is where the interest lies: the two halves matter because a person is judged. That is the same centre of gravity the Hilchot Teshuvah passage has, and it is a different centre from the Christian discussions in this base, which reach the pair through the doctrine of God and stay there.

**Gaps.** *Tzafui* ("foreseen," from *tzafah*, to watch or look out) is not self-evidently exhaustive foreknowledge of future contingents, and nothing here establishes that Akiva means what the later debates mean. No commentary is on file — not Maimonides' own commentary on the Mishnah, not Bartenura — so the tradition's own reading of the line is unrepresented.
