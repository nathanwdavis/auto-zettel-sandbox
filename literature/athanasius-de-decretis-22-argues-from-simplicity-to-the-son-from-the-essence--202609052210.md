**Scope:** This note covers *De Decretis* section 22 only, a single section of the work's thirty-two sections, in which Athanasius advances a premise-form argument using divine simplicity.

In section 22, Athanasius addresses those who misconceive God as compound, having parts or external properties. His argument runs from divine simplicity as a logical premise to a conclusion about the Son's origin:

> But if God be simple, as He is, it follows that in saying `God' and naming `Father,' we name nothing as if about Him, but signify his essence itself. For though to comprehend what the essence of God is be impossible, yet if we only understand that God is, and if Scripture indicates Him by means of these titles, we, with the intention of indicating Him and none else, call Him God and Father and Lord.

From this understanding—that the names "God" and "Father" signify essence itself because God is simple—Athanasius draws the conclusion:

> Therefore let no one be startled on hearing that the Son of God is from the Essence of the Father; but rather let him accept the explanation of the Fathers, who in more explicit but equivalent language have for `from God' written `of the essence.'

The logical form is: (1) God is simple; (2) therefore, when we say "God" or "Father," we name His essence itself; (3) therefore, the Son—called the Word, the Wisdom, the proper offspring of the Father—must be from that essence, not from anything external or accidental to it.

Section 22 is an instance of the kind of use the survey reports at second hand from Wedgeworth (2019) — simplicity as a premise in an argument for the Son's status — which is enough to retire the charge that the survey's report is inaccurate as stated. It is not the survey's claim itself: section 22 concludes that the Son is from the Father's essence, not that eternal generation proves absolute deity, and nothing here supports the survey's “consistently”. The word “eternal” does not occur in this section.

**Caveat:** This note rests on Robertson's 1900 English translation from Tertullian.org, a single host, and makes no claim about the Greek text. The logical form described here is the form of the English rendering, not necessarily the form of Athanasius's Greek. The claim is about section 22 and the sections actually read (8–32 of the work's thirty-two sections), not about the work's whole argument.
