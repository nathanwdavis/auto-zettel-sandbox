p. 411 (Key points, the chapter's own summary box):
  "• Testing does more than merely assess learning – it also enhances learning
  because of the retrieval processes learners engage in while taking tests.
  • Retrieval practice produces robust and long-lasting effects on learning and
  retention of knowledge.
  • Retrieval enhances learning because it requires learners to retrieve,
  reinstate, and update context representations.
  • Retrieval tends to enhance learning the most when learners can successfully
  retrieve knowledge with relatively less retrieval support.
  • Retrieval practice enhances learning of complex educational materials as
  assessed on measures of meaningful learning.
  • Retrieval-based learning has been generalized across a wide range of learner
  characteristics, materials, and classroom and school settings."

p. 419 (section 4.21.4, on the open-book/closed-book manipulation -- this is
the chapter's account of the same Agarwal et al. 2008 study captured in full at
raw/202609040315, and the two should be read together; see the note below this
excerpt):
  "Another way to examine retrieval practice is to have students complete the
  same activity either while viewing the material, as an encoding task, or
  without viewing it, as a retrieval task. In educational settings, this
  distinction is often called open-book vs. closed-book testing. Agarwal et al.
  (2008) directly compared open-book and closed-book quiz formats. Students read
  educational texts and answered short-answer questions either with or without
  access to the texts. The open-book quiz conditions led to more forgetting over
  1 week relative to the closed-book condition, in which learners attempted to
  retrieve answers before studying the answers. Thus, the same questioning or
  quizzing activity was made more effective when it required learners to engage
  in retrieval practice, rather than merely looking for answers in the text (see
  also Agarwal and Roediger, 2011; Arnold et al., 2021)."

  READER'S NOTE, this base's own observation and not the chapter's words: the
  sentence above and the Agarwal et al. (2008) summary on that paper's own
  p. 861 report the same data on two different measures and neither is wrong.
  The chapter reports FORGETTING -- proportional loss from initial performance
  -- and open-book conditions did forget more (Exp. 1: .81 -> .65 against
  .69 -> .68 for the closed-book-with-feedback condition the relative clause
  describes; Exp. 2: .81 -> .66 against .65 -> .66). The paper's own summary
  reports the LEVEL of delayed retention, which was equivalent (.66 and .66 in
  Exp. 2). A note citing either sentence alone, without the other, would
  mislead.

p. 434 (Conclusions, on replication status):
  "The benefits of practicing retrieval have been replicated hundreds of times
  across a range of initial retrieval tasks, and the effects of retrieval are
  long-lasting and promote transfer, inferencing, and knowledge application.
  Retrieval practice has been shown to enhance the learning of a wide variety of
  materials for learners of nearly all ages and ability levels, and the effects
  have been established in classroom settings with authentic course content."
