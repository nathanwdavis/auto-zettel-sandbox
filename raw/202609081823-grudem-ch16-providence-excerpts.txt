Short RECONSTRUCTED excerpts from Wayne Grudem, Systematic Theology: An
Introduction to Biblical Doctrine, 2nd ed. (Zondervan Academic, 2020), chapter
16, "God's Providence" (reference: grudem-systematic-theology--202609050158).

NOT a verbatim capture, and deliberately not called one. README defines raw/ as
"Verbatim source captures", and this file does not meet that description: the
PDF's text layer could not be read directly (see TEXT RECONSTRUCTION below), so
what follows is a reconstruction of the printed text rather than a transcription
of it. What IS claimed: every passage below was checked as an exact substring of
the reconstruction, and the reconstruction was checked against rendered images of
six pages, read directly, which matched character for character. What is NOT
claimed: that the pages not rendered were verified the same way, or that italic
emphasis, line breaks and footnote-marker placement survive. Anything turning on
the exact printed form of a passage should be re-read from a page image before it
is asserted.

PROVENANCE. The repository owner (nathanwdavis) supplied the complete chapter
as a PDF in a Claude Code session on 2026-09-08, exported from their own
licensed copy in Logos Bible Study. Every page carries the running attribution
"Wayne Grudem, Systematic Theology: An Introduction to Biblical Doctrine, Second
Edition (Grand Rapids, MI: Zondervan Academic, 2020)." and the footer "Exported
from Logos Bible Study, 2:09 PM September 8, 2026." The export is 83 pages of
roughly 24,300 words; the whole chapter was read by the session before any note
was written. This is the third owner-supplied capture of the second edition,
after the ch. 12 omniscience export (raw/202609061910) and the pp. 221-222
spirituality drop (raw/202609071342), and by far the largest.

PAGE NUMBERS. The export's own page numbers (1-83, cited below as "export p.N")
are Logos export pages, NOT book pages: the footer numbers the export, and no

TEXT RECONSTRUCTION. The PDF's embedded font subsets map the ligatures "Th",
"tt" and "ft" onto five ASCII glyph slots (! " # $ %), inconsistently from page
to page, and mark line-break hyphenation with U+FFFE. The extracted text was
therefore reconstructed: each affected word-form was resolved individually (84
distinct forms; e.g. word-initial -> "Th", "Co!rell" -> "Cottrell", "o%en" ->
"often", "swi$" -> "swift"), hyphenation joins were applied except where the
break fell on a real hyphen ("behind-the-scenes" and one URL), and running
headers and footers were stripped. Every quotation below was then checked
programmatically as an exact substring of the reconstructed text, and the
reconstruction itself was checked against rendered page images of export pp. 2,
9, 35, 64, 69 and 77 -- read directly by the session -- which matched character
for character. Italic emphasis in the original is not reproduced here; where a
note leans on Grudem's emphasis it says so.

TWO EXTRACTION ARTIFACTS, corrected in the passages below and flagged in place.
(a) Footnote reference numbers sometimes float into the sentence they mark, so
the extracted text reads "God's relation 15 to sin" and "God's control19 or".
The passages below give the reading without the marker and record what the

[export p. 1 | providence defined]
God is continually involved with all created things in such a way that he (1)
keeps them existing and maintaining the properties with which he created them;
(2) cooperates with created things in every action, directing their
distinctive properties to cause them to act as they do; and (3) directs them
to fulfill his purposes.

[export p. 2 | fn1 compatibilism]
The view advocated in this chapter is also sometimes called “compatibilism,”
because it holds that absolute divine sovereignty is compatible with human
significance and real human choices. I have no objection to the nuances of
this term, but I have decided not to use it

[export p. 2 | fn1 determinism]
Though philosophers may use the term determinism (or soft determinism) to
categorize the position I advocate in this chapter, I do not use that term
because it is too easily misunderstood in everyday English

[export p. 2 | fn1 widely understood tradition]
place myself within a widely understood theological tradition represented by
John Calvin and the other systematic theologians listed in the “Reformed”
category at the end of this chapter.

[export p. 2 | three subtopics]
Under the general category of providence, we have three subtopics according to
the three elements in the definition above: (1) preservation, (2) concurrence,
and (3) government.

[export p. 3 | not simply sustain]
It does not mean simply “sustain.” It has the sense of active, purposeful
control over the thing being carried from one place to another.

[export p. 3 | pherō carry bear]
The Greek word translated “upholds” is pherō, “carry, bear.”

[export p. 3 | preservation defined]
God keeps all created things existing and maintaining the properties with
which he created them.

[export p. 5 | concurrence defined]
God cooperates with created things in every action, directing their
distinctive properties to cause them to act as they do.

[export p. 5 | the rock hurts]
I do not just imagine that the rock in my hand is hard—it is hard. If I bump
it against my head, I do not just imagine that it hurts—it does hurt!

[export p. 6 | Eph 1:11 works all things]
In Ephesians 1:11 Paul says that God “works all things according to the
counsel of his will.” The word translated “works” (energeō) indicates that God
“brings about” all things according to his own will. No event in creation
falls outside of his providence.

[export p. 9 | 100 percent]
it is possible to affirm that in one sense events are fully (100 percent)
caused by God and fully (100 percent) caused by the creature as well. However,
divine and creaturely causes work in different ways.

[export p. 9 | not partly caused]
In all of these events, it is not as though the event was partly caused by God
and partly by factors in the created world.

[export p. 9 | primary cause]
The divine cause of each event works as an invisible, behind-the-scenes,
directing cause and therefore could be called the “primary cause” that plans
and initiates everything that happens.

[export p. 10 | secondary causes]
creaturely factors and properties can therefore be called the “secondary
causes” of everything that happens, even though they are the causes that are
evident to us by observation.

[export p. 14 | better to affirm]
It seems better to affirm that God causes all things that happen but that he
does so in such a way that he somehow upholds our ability to make willing,
responsible choices that have real and eternal results for which we are held
accountable. Exactly how God combines his providential control with our
willing and significant choices, Scripture does not explain to us.

[export p. 15 | Macbeth]
On the level of the characters in the play Macbeth fully (100%) caused King
Duncan’s death, but on the level of the creator of the play, William
Shakespeare fully (100%) caused King Duncan’s death.

[export p. 31 | Berkhof mystery, marker removed]
we have to agree with Berkhof that ultimately “the problem of God’s relation
to sin remains a mystery.”  [fn-marker: the reconstructed text reads “relation
15 to sin”; the 15 is footnote 15’s marker, printed after the closing
quotation mark in the book and floated into the sentence by the extractor]

[export p. 31 | we confess we do not understand]
But Scripture does not tell us exactly how God brings this situation about or
how it can be that God holds us accountable for what he ordains to come to
pass.

[export p. 33 | anchor Cottrell 451-55]
see discussion of Jack Cottrell’s objection that freedom must mean more than
willing choices on pp. 451–55, below

[export p. 33 | free in the greatest sense]
But we are nonetheless free in the greatest sense that any creature of God
could be free—we make willing choices, choices that have real effects.

[export p. 33 | free senses denied, marker removed]
Scripture nowhere says that we are “free” in the sense of being outside of
God’s control or of being able to make decisions that are not caused by
anything.  [fn-marker: the reconstructed text reads “control19 or”; 19 is
footnote 19’s marker]

[export p. 34 | libertarian free will]
Libertarian free will is “the ability, all things being just what they are, to
choose differently.”

[export p. 34 | not to exist]
then to be outside of that providential control would simply be not to exist!

[export p. 35 | determined in ways invisible]
it does not rule out the idea that our choices may ultimately be determined by
God in ways invisible to us.

[export p. 35 | fn23 compatibilist freedom]
This “freedom of inclination” is also called “compatibilist freedom.”

[export p. 35 | freedom of inclination]
Freedom of inclination means that “we are free when we choose according to our
strongest inclination or deepest desire. In short, we are free when we do what
we most want to do.”

[export p. 35 | more consistent]
This understanding of freedom seems to me more consistent with the teachings
of Scripture

[export p. 36 | decrees defined]
The decrees of God are the eternal plans of God whereby, before the creation
of the world, he determined to bring about everything that happens.

[export p. 38 | actions have effects]
God has ordained that our actions have effects. God has ordained that events
will come about by our causing them.

[export p. 42 | spur to action]
A hearty belief in God’s providence is not a discouragement but a spur to
action.

[export p. 43 | Pinnock volumes praised]
The term Arminianism was used in the title of a responsible series of essays
representing this position

[export p. 43 | excellent defenses]
These two books are excellent defenses of the Arminian position.

[export p. 44 | Arminian position]
God cannot cause or plan our voluntary choices.

[export p. 44 | control must not include]
Therefore they conclude that God’s providential involvement in or control of
history must not include every specific detail of every event that happens but
that God instead simply responds to human choices and actions as they come
about and does so in such a way that his purposes are ultimately accomplished
in the world.  [page-break: footnote 31 falls between “involvement in” and “or
control of history” in the extracted text; the halves are consecutive on
export pp. 44-45]

[export p. 45 | Cottrell most persuasive]
Cottrell’s essay is, in my view, the most comprehensive and persuasive of the
many excellent Arminian essays in this book

[export p. 45 | Cottrell no specific purpose]
God does not have a specific, unconditional purpose for each discrete
particle, object, person, and event within the creation.

[export p. 52 | anchor examples 422-33]
the examples are so numerous (see above, pp. 422–33)

[export p. 54 | anchor B7 428-33]
many texts that clearly say that God ordains that some people sin or do evil

[export p. 56 | the pair held together]
This causes us to conclude that God has made us in such a way that (1) he
ordains all that we do, and (2) we exercise our personal will and make real,
voluntary choices.  [page-break: footnote 56 and the running matter fall

[export p. 56 | where does Scripture say]
Arminians have simply not answered the question, Where does Scripture say that

[export p. 58 | anchor decrees 440-41]

[export p. 63 | Calvin freedom from worry]
Gratitude of mind for the favorable outcome of things, patience in adversity,
and also incredible freedom from worry about the future all necessarily follow
upon this knowledge.

[export p. 63 | Rom 8:28 in ch16]
all things work together for good for those who love God and are called

[export p. 63 | differences immense]

[export p. 63 | part of his wise plan]
knowing that it has not come about because God was unable to prevent it, but

[export p. 64 | Cottrell foreknowledge]
I affirm that God has a true foreknowledge of future free-will choices without
himself being the agent that causes them or renders them certain.

[export p. 64 | fixed therefore not free]
If our future choices are known, then they are fixed. And if they are fixed,
then they are not “free” in the Arminian sense (undetermined or uncaused).

[export p. 64 | open theism most consistent]
This view (called “open theism”) seems to me to be the most consistent
Arminian position

[export p. 64 | the entailment as argument]
even if God did not plan or cause things to happen, the fact that they are
foreknown means that they will certainly come about.

[export p. 65 | anchor open theism 227-30]
See the discussion of open theism in chapter 12, pp. 227–30.

[export p. 65 | difference between friends]
I thank God for his ministry, and consider the following material a difference
between friends.

[export p. 65 | third Arminian viewpoint]
But I am classifying it here as a third Arminian viewpoint because, according
to middle knowledge, the ultimate factor that determines whether someone is
saved is not God’s eternal choice of that individual person but the person’s
own libertarian free will decision to believe in Christ.

[export p. 66 | set of circumstances]
God knows exactly what the free creature would do were God to place him in a
certain set of circumstances.

[export p. 69 | Frame bridge]
God does have this kind of knowledge, whether we call it “middle knowledge” or
something else. (And John Frame points out that such knowledge is already part
of God’s necessary knowledge, or what Craig calls natural knowledge.)

[export p. 69 | ch12 anchor 225-31]
the debate at this point is not about whether God knows how people would
respond if various kinds of circumstances came about (I affirmed this in
chapter 12, pages 225–31).

[export p. 71 | millions of prior choices]
in order for God to “bring about a situation,” literally millions of prior
human choices would have to be decided in a certain way, or else the situation
would be different.

[export p. 71 | situations are made of choices]
every situation in which people find themselves is itself the result of
thousands, in fact millions, of individual human choices.

[export p. 72 | denies libertarian freedom]
But such a process denies the kind of libertarian free will that middle
knowledge advocates are claiming to protect.

[export p. 77 | Arminian questions diminish]
Their failure to resolve these questions tends to diminish the greatness of

promises for the future.

[export p. 77 | diminishes wisdom and skill]
the Arminian position diminishes the wisdom and skill of God the Creator.

[export p. 77 | effect increases greatness]
So the effect of these unanswered questions is to increase our appreciation of
the greatness of God.
